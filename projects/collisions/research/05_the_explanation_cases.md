## The Court's answer — C-203/22

**Case C-203/22, *CK v Magistrat der Stadt Wien and Dun & Bradstreet Austria GmbH*, Court of
Justice of the European Union, judgment of 27 February 2025.** *Verified: case number, both
respondents, court, date; EUR-Lex CELEX `62022CJ0203`; Curia press release `cp250022`.*

**The facts, and they are a gift — a fact pattern in three sentences.** CK was **refused a mobile
phone contract**. The refusal followed an **automated credit assessment** by Dun & Bradstreet
Austria, which concluded she did not have sufficient creditworthiness. The **Austrian data
protection authority** ordered D&B to give her *meaningful information about the logic involved*
under **Art. 15(1)(h)**; D&B challenged that order before the Federal Administrative Court, saying
disclosure would give away a **trade secret**.

> **Note how neatly this sits beside SCHUFA, and build the episode on the pair.** Both are credit
> scoring. Both are a refusal by one company on a score from another. SCHUFA (docket `02`) decides
> **whether Art. 22 applies at all** — the scorer is making the decision. This decides **what the
> person then gets to be told**. *One fact pattern, two questions, two judgments, fifteen months
> apart.* The listener does not have to learn a second world.

**Holding one — what "meaningful information about the logic involved" means.** It covers **all
relevant information concerning the procedure and the principles** actually applied, such that the
data subject can **understand which of their personal data was used and how**, in arriving at the
result. The standard is set by what it has to achieve: the person must be able to **understand and
contest** the decision.

**Holding two, and this is the one the episode turns on.** Handing over **the algorithm** is **not
what is required, and not sufficient**. Nor is a detailed description of every step of the
automated process. A complex mathematical formula, or a step-by-step account, **does not satisfy
the provision** — because it does not make the decision intelligible to the person it was made
about.

> **This is the Court arriving at Selbst and Powles' position by a different road, and the writer
> should let that land without over-claiming it.** Docket `02` has Selbst and Powles arguing that
> information is not *meaningful* unless it lets the person do something — contest the decision, or
> use their other rights. The Court has now said, in terms, that the test is whether the person can
> **understand and challenge**. It has **not** cited them and this is not a vindication of a paper;
> it is the same reasoning reached independently, which is a stronger thing to be able to say.
> ⚠ **Do not say the Court adopted their argument.** Say what each said, and that they agree.

**Holding three — the trade-secret answer, and it is a procedural move rather than a substantive
one.** A controller may **not simply refuse** on the ground of trade secrecy. Where it says the
information contains trade secrets or third-party data, it must **supply that information to the
competent supervisory authority or court**, which then **balances** the competing rights and
interests and determines how far the right of access extends. **Case by case.**

> **The beat that is available here, and it is the honest one.** The Court did not decide how much
> secrecy must yield. It decided **who decides** — and moved the question from the controller's
> discretion to a supervisory authority or a judge. *That is a real answer to "can they just say
> no", and it is not an answer to "what will I actually be told".* An episode that reports this as
> a transparency victory is overstating it; an episode that reports it as nothing is understating
> it. **Say what changed: the refusal is no longer self-certifying.**

## The facts underneath — the Amsterdam judgments

**Three linked judgments of the *Gerechtshof Amsterdam* (Amsterdam Court of Appeal), 4 April 2023**,
in proceedings brought by drivers — supported by the **App Drivers and Couriers Union** and
**Worker Info Exchange** — against **Uber** and **Ola Cabs**. *Verified: court, date, the three
linked judgments, the parties and the supporting organisations, and the holdings below.*
⚠ **The ECLI numbers are NOT established here.** That is not a gap the episode suffers from: an
ECLI is unsayable and would never be read aloud. **Refer to the court, the date and the parties.**

**Holding one — several of the platforms' automated processes are Article 22 decisions.** The Court
of Appeal held that processes on the Uber and Ola platforms qualified as **automated individual
decision-making** within Art. 22, against the companies' contention that they did not. The Court
also found under **Art. 15** that the drivers' access requests had been wrongly refused, and that
what is provided must be **complete enough for the person to understand the reasons for the
decision, or to challenge it**. Ola was ordered to grant the requests.

**Holding two, and it is the reason this case is in this series.** Uber and Ola both argued that a
**human reviewed** the automated outcomes, which would take the decisions outside Art. 22. The
Court rejected it **on the facts**, finding the review amounted to *"not… much more than a purely
symbolic act"*.

⚠ **That phrase is a translation of a Dutch judgment, taken from English-language reporting.** It
is the single most useful formulation available to Episode 5 and it must be handled honestly:
either **attribute it as reported and translated** — "as reported in English, the court called it
not much more than a purely symbolic act" — or paraphrase it and keep the idea. **Do not read it as
a verbatim quotation of the court.**

> **This is where the word "solely" in Article 22 meets the world, and Episode 4 has already taught
> the word.** Episode 4 teaches that Art. 22 bites on decisions based *solely* on automated
> processing, and that a human in the loop is the standard way out. This is a court looking at an
> actual human in an actual loop and finding the loop **ornamental**. **So the sequence across the
> two episodes is: here is the escape hatch; here is a court measuring whether anyone actually went
> through it.** That is the series' method working exactly as intended, and it is available without
> any new concept at all.

**What it does not give you.** It is a **national appellate court**, not the Court of Justice, so
it does not set a Union-wide test for when human review is genuine. It is a finding **on these
facts about these processes**. ⚠ **Do not generalise it into a rule** that human review is usually
symbolic — the episode's point is that the question is **factual and checkable**, which is stronger
and also true.

## Episode 6's second marker is not a gap

The plan's remaining marker reads: *"if the docket later supplies a scholarly source that draws this
distinction in these terms, attribute it; until then it is the narrator's."* The distinction is
**justification against explanation** — an explanation earning its place only by making a decision
contestable.

**This docket does not supply one, and the marker should stay.** What it supplies instead is better
for the episode: **Selbst and Powles** argue the substance of it (docket `02`), and the **Court of
Justice now states the contestability test in its own operative reasoning** (above). So the
*framing* remains the narrator's, and the *test inside it* is now a court's. **Say both, and keep
the labelling the plan asks for** — it is the series' own honesty move and Episode 1's script
already does it well.
