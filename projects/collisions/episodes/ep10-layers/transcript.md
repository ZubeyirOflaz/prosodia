## Two words first {tone: precise, note: "definitional — even and deliberate, so the two terms read as structure; the rented flat is the one place to loosen into thinking aloud"}

Two words carry this episode. Each one arrives with a meaning attached that will send you wrong.

The first is the phrase itself: {pause: 0.4} layered protection.

The meaning most people bring is arithmetic. One law protects you a bit, and two protect you more. Two blankets are warmer than one.

{tone: curious} Try that on something with nothing at stake: {pause: 0.4} a rented flat. Three sets of rules apply to it. There's a fire code, enforced by an inspector who can close the building. There's an insurance policy, which pays whoever holds it if the building burns. And there's a tenancy agreement, which gives the tenant a right to have repairs done.

Three layers. Is the tenant three times as protected?

{pause: 1.6}

Ask who each layer is addressed to. The fire code works through the inspector. The insurance pays the owner. Only the tenancy runs to the tenant, and only for repairs. Now suppose the boiler fails. The tenant has to stay somewhere else for a month, and pays for it. The tenancy gets the boiler repaired. None of the three pays for the month. {pause: 0.6} You can add rules to that flat all day, {pause: 0.5} and the loss stays exactly where it fell.

{pause: 1.0}

{tone: precise} So, plainly: layered protection is a claim about how instruments interact. It's about whose hands each protection actually ends up in. {pause: 0.8} A claim like that can be tested, and testing it is the whole of this episode.

{pause: 1.0}

The second word is gap.

You'll probably hear a gap as an oversight, {pause: 0.3} something the drafters never thought of. On this record, the gaps that matter were *seen*. Somebody drafted a proposal to fill them, and then the proposal stopped. {tone: pointed} So in this episode, a gap means a hole the legislature saw, {pause: 0.4} wrote against, {pause: 0.4} and then left open. {tone: precise} {pause: 1.4}
