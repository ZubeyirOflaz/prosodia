## Twenty-two words {tone: measured, note: "cold open — let the number sit; give the two readings equal conviction"}

Twenty-two words. {pause: 0.8}

They were drafted once and then copied, unchanged, into three separate articles of the General Data Protection Regulation. They go like this, and this is the only time in this episode I will read them.

> "meaningful information about the logic involved, as well as the significance and the envisaged consequences of such processing for the data subject."

End of quotation.

{pause: 1.0}

{tone: lucid} Now two readings of that sentence.

**The first reading.** The organisation has to describe the system. What it is for, broadly what goes into it, what it does to people, what you can expect to happen if it runs over your file. A good, honest account of a machine — {pause: 0.4} written once, published, kept current. That is a real obligation and it is not a small one. But it is an account of the machine. It arrives before your case exists, or alongside it. It says nothing about why your application came back the way it did.

{pause: 0.8}

**The second reading.** The word *meaningful* is not decoration. Information is meaningful only if the person receiving it can do something with it — {pause: 0.5} and the Regulation itself says what they are supposed to be able to do. {pause: 0.4} Argue. So whatever else that sentence requires, it requires enough about *your* case that you could sensibly dispute the outcome. Which is an explanation, whatever the drafters thought they were writing.

{pause: 1.4}

{tone: curious} Both of those readings are held, in print, by people who know this instrument as well as anyone alive. They were published months apart, in the same journal, in the same year. Neither side has conceded in the nine years since.

And they cannot both be right. {tone: pointed} Because one of them lets an organisation discharge the duty with a web page, {pause: 0.5} and the other does not. {tone: measured} {pause: 1.2}

## Where this is, and what today is for {tone: lucid}

This is The Collisions.

This is also the only episode in the series that teaches you no provision at all. No new article, no test to apply, no fact pattern to work. Today is a tool, and the tool is what you judge the provisions *with*.

You are carrying three things that today spends. The word *solely*, and the fact that a human in the loop is the standard way out of the rule on automated decisions. {pause: 0.4} The duties to tell you things, and the right to ask. {pause: 0.4} And a judgment from last February — from 2025 — in which the Court of Justice said that what you are owed has to be good enough for you to understand and to contest the decision.

That is what you need. Everything else from the last five episodes can stay where it is.

{pause: 1.0}

{tone: warm} The tool is a distinction, and I want to be straight with you about where it comes from. It is mine. I am not reporting it from a scholar and I am not reading it out of a statute, because it appears in no provision of either instrument this series is about.

{tone: precise} It goes like this. An account of an automated decision can be addressed to an institution, {pause: 0.4} or it can be addressed to a person. Addressed to an institution, its job is to show that the process was proper. Addressed to a person, its job is to let that person argue. I am going to call the first one a **justification** and the second one an **explanation**.

{pause: 0.8}

{tone: pointed} Almost everything the instruments in this series require is a justification. {tone: lucid}

{pause: 1.4}

By the end of this episode you should be able to pick up any disclosure duty, any mandatory assessment, any file a statute makes somebody write — {pause: 0.5} and ask one question of it. Who is this addressed to? {pause: 1.4}

{tone: wry} One thing I am leaving out, and I would rather name it than let you notice it missing. {tone: lucid} There is a substantial technical literature on what can be pulled out of a trained model after the fact. I am not going to survey it. This episode is about what would *count* as an explanation, {pause: 0.4} not about what anyone can currently build.

## The paper that said there is no such right {tone: measured}

2017. The journal is *International Data Privacy Law*. The authors are Sandra Wachter, Brent Mittelstadt and Luciano Floridi.

The title is the argument, so here it is whole:

> "Why a Right to Explanation of Automated Decision-Making Does Not Exist in the General Data Protection Regulation".

It was a response to something. After the Regulation was adopted in 2016, a confident claim went around — in the press, and in the literature — that Europe had just legislated a right to an explanation. That claim is what they set out to dismantle.

{tone: precise} Their argument, at its strongest. The information provisions give you a **right to be informed**. Meaningful information, yes — {pause: 0.4} but properly limited information, about the logic involved, about the significance, about the envisaged consequences. {tone: pointed} That is a different and much smaller thing than an account of why one particular decision, already made, came out as it did. {tone: measured}

{pause: 1.2}

{tone: curious} Let me strengthen their case with something you can hear in the words themselves, and the strengthening is mine rather than theirs.

Look at when those duties fire. Two of the three fire at collection, or shortly after it — {pause: 0.4} when the data arrives, before anything has been decided about anybody. You cannot explain a decision that has not happened. {pause: 0.6} And the sentence asks for the *envisaged* consequences. Envisaged is a forward-looking word. It describes what is expected to happen, {pause: 0.4} not what did.

{tone: measured} There is a second limb to their argument that runs on the legislative history — on what was proposed during drafting and what did not survive into the binding part of the text. I have not read that history and I will not characterise it for you. What I can read is the text that came out the other end. This programme works from the consolidated operative text, which does not carry the preamble. So when I tell you what this Regulation requires, I am telling you what its articles say.

{pause: 1.0}

Now the cost of believing them, because every position in this episode comes with one.

If they are right, an organisation complies by publishing a good description of its system {pause: 0.4} and owes you nothing whatever about your own case. The drivers you met last episode get a document about the platform. The woman refused a phone contract gets a page about how credit scoring works in general. {tone: somber} Both organisations would be fully compliant, and both people would be exactly where they started. {tone: measured}

{pause: 1.0}

{tone: wry} That is not a reductio. They would accept it. {tone: measured} Their point is that this is what the text says, and that pretending otherwise helps nobody.

## What they built instead {tone: curious}

Which is why the same group did not stop there.

Before I give you the title of what they built, I owe you a phrase I have been carefully not using. {tone: wry} People say a model is a black box, and nine times out of ten saying it is a way of avoiding saying anything. {tone: curious} So let me say what is actually unavailable, and why.

{tone: lucid} A trained model is a very large collection of numbers, fitted by a procedure, which together turn an input into an output. You can have every one of those numbers. Handing them over would cost the company its commercial position and would tell you nothing, because there is no line anywhere inside that says {pause: 0.3} *this applicant, this reason*. Nothing is being concealed from you. {tone: contemplative} The account you want was never written, {pause: 0.5} because no step in the process ever wrote one.

{pause: 1.2}

{tone: curious} That is what the phrase should mean. And it is the title of the paper: *"Counterfactual Explanations Without Opening the Black Box"*, by Wachter, Mittelstadt and Chris Russell, in the *Harvard Journal of Law and Technology*, volume 31, 2018.

The proposal is one sentence long. {pause: 0.6} {tone: pointed} Tell the person what would have had to be different for the answer to change. {tone: curious}

{pause: 1.2}

It is useful. It is short. It is contestable — {pause: 0.3} you can go and check whether the thing they named about you is true. And it requires nobody to open anything.

{pause: 1.0}

{tone: measured} The limit on it is mine, and here it is. A counterfactual tells you what to change. It does not tell you whether the reason was a legitimate one. And there is usually more than one true counterfactual for any given case, which means somebody is choosing which one you get — and the somebody doing the choosing {pause: 0.5} is the organisation that made the decision.

{pause: 1.0}

{tone: precise} So where we are, three positions in. There is a duty to describe the system, which is real. {pause: 0.4} There is a proposal to hand people something usable instead, which is cheap and clever. {pause: 0.4} And there is not yet, on this account, any obligation to tell you why.

## The reply, published four issues later {tone: measured}

Andrew Selbst and Julia Powles, in the same journal, in November of the same year. The title answers the first paper by name:

> "Meaningful information and the right to explanation".

Their argument turns on one word, and it is the word in the Regulation rather than one of theirs. {tone: pointed} Information is not meaningful unless it lets the person **do** something — {pause: 0.5} contest the decision, or use the other rights the Regulation hands them. {tone: measured} {pause: 1.2} Read that way, they say, the provisions already require explanation in the sense that matters, whatever the drafters believed they were writing.

{tone: warm} Now watch what that argument is standing on, because this is the joint the whole disagreement turns on. {tone: measured}

{pause: 1.0}

Two episodes back you met the safeguards that attach to automated decisions. A human to intervene. A chance to put your side. And the right to contest the decision.

{tone: precise} Notice what that paragraph does not contain. It does not contain a duty to tell you why. {pause: 0.8} The right to argue sits in one paragraph of the Regulation. The duty to inform sits in different articles entirely, and it is *triggered* by the existence of an automated decision {pause: 0.3} rather than *addressed* to one. Nothing in the operative text joins them up and says: {pause: 0.5} you shall be told enough to exercise the right we have just given you. {tone: measured}

{pause: 1.4}

Selbst and Powles' answer is that the word *meaningful* does the joining. A right to contest that comes with no account of what you are contesting is not a right anyone can use — {pause: 0.4} so *meaningful* has to be read as supplying whatever the safeguard needs in order to work.

{tone: wry} The sceptics' answer is that this is a lawyer building a bridge because he would like to cross the river. {tone: measured}

{pause: 0.8}

And keep one limit visible while you decide. Those safeguards do not attach to every automated decision. Where the decision is authorised by a law, that law is supposed to lay down its own protections instead — {pause: 0.4} so the paragraph I have just described is not the universal backstop it sounds like.

The cost of believing Selbst and Powles is large and they know it. Every automated decision of any consequence needs a case-specific account, produced per decision, at whatever volume the organisation runs at. And the trade-secret objection has to give way, at least in part — which, for a credit scoring firm, means the model is the business {pause: 0.4} and the business is what you are asking it to describe.

## What the Court answered, and what it did not {tone: neutral}

You already have the judgment, so I will spend twenty seconds on it rather than ten minutes.

February 2025. A woman refused a mobile phone contract on an automated credit assessment. The Court of Justice held that the standard is set by what the information has to achieve: {pause: 0.4} the person must be able to understand and to contest the decision. Handing over the algorithm is neither required nor sufficient. And an organisation cannot simply refuse on trade-secret grounds — it must put the material in front of a supervisory authority or a judge, who decides how far access reaches.

{pause: 0.8}

{tone: measured} That looks like the reply winning. Let me be careful, and let me mark what follows clearly as mine, because this is my characterisation of the gap and not the Court's.

The Court was answering a question about **access** — about what has to be handed over when a person asks for what the organisation already holds and already knows. Whether anything in the Regulation obliges an organisation to *produce reasons* for a particular decision, to generate an account it would not otherwise have had, {pause: 0.4} is a different question. That judgment was not asked it.

{pause: 0.8}

{tone: pointed} A judgment that answers the question next door has not settled this one. {tone: measured} {pause: 1.2}

## The tool, worked {tone: lucid}

So. The distinction, applied — {pause: 0.4} and it is the whole of what this episode is for.

Take any document a statute makes somebody write, and ask who is on the other end of it.

A compliance file assembled to show a regulator that the correct steps were taken. A published description of a model, written so that other engineers can assess it. Each of those can be entirely honest, carefully made, accurate in every particular — {pause: 0.5} and still leave the person the decision was made about holding nothing they can use. The document is doing its job. Its job has a different reader.

{tone: precise} That is a justification. {tone: lucid}

{pause: 1.0}

{tone: precise} An explanation earns the name by one test only. {pause: 0.5} {tone: pointed} Can the person it was made about use it to argue? {tone: lucid}

{pause: 1.6}

Not: do they understand it. {tone: wry} Understanding is cheap, and a great deal of very clear writing has been produced precisely so that people will understand why nothing can be done. {tone: lucid} The test is whether there is a point of purchase in it — {pause: 0.5} a fact about them they could go and dispute, a step they could say was wrong.

{pause: 1.4}

{tone: warm} That tool does not stay in this episode, and you do not have to remember where it goes. I will bring it back each time. {tone: curious}

In Episode 8, an instrument decides whether something is defective by asking what safety *"a person is entitled to expect"* — those are the Directive's words. That is a justification standard wearing an explanation's clothes, and a court is going to have to decide {pause: 0.3} what a person is entitled to expect of a statistical system.

And in Episode 9, the most important move in this series. The point at which the law stops asking anybody to explain anything, {pause: 0.5} and changes who has to prove what instead.

## The best argument against where I am going {tone: contemplative, note: "arguing against itself — candid and unhurried and never defensive"}

Before I come down anywhere, the strongest objection — {pause: 0.4} and it is strong enough that I have not fully answered it.

The distinction I have just handed you does moral work {pause: 0.3} without doing any legal work. No provision contains it. Which means an organisation can satisfy every word of both instruments in this series and still fail my test — {pause: 0.4} and a test that nothing is obliged to pass costs nothing at all to fail. You can use it to feel clear-sighted about a system while the system carries on exactly as before.

{pause: 1.0}

And the second half of the objection belongs to the sceptics, and it is the reason their paper was worth writing. Telling people they hold a right that the text does not carry is not a kindness to them. They rely on it. They complain, they wait, they spend a year, and they lose — {pause: 0.4} and they lose in a way that looks like their own failure rather than the statute's. A gap that has been named out loud can be legislated. A right that everybody believes in cannot be, {pause: 0.4} because everybody already thinks it is there.

{pause: 1.4}

{tone: measured} So here is where I come down, and it splits.

On the narrow question — what the 2016 text, read as text, actually obliges anybody to do — {pause: 0.4} the sceptics have the better of it. I say that as someone who does not care for the answer.

On what the word *meaningful* has to be made to mean once a judge has a real refusal in front of them, {pause: 0.4} the reply has been closer to what actually happened. Both of those can be true at once, because they are answers to different questions. {tone: wry} Most of the heat in this argument comes from people answering one of them and believing they have answered the other. {tone: measured}

{pause: 1.0}

And underneath both, the thing worth keeping. {pause: 0.5} {tone: pointed} An account the person cannot argue with is a justification, {pause: 0.4} however detailed, however honest, however true. {tone: contemplative} {pause: 1.4}

This Regulation is full of them. So is every other instrument this series is going to reach.

## Where it stands {tone: precise}

Three states, and I will say which is which rather than leaving you to guess.

**Settled**, since 2025: {pause: 0.4} an organisation cannot certify its own refusal by saying the word *secret*. Somebody independent looks. That is a genuine change {pause: 0.3} and it is narrower than it was reported to be.

**Contested**, and actively: {pause: 0.4} whether *meaningful* reaches the reasons for your particular decision, or stops at a description of the system. That is the argument this episode has just given you, and it is nine years old with both original positions still standing.

**Still moving**: {pause: 0.4} the standard itself, which is being worked out one refusal at a time by authorities and courts, case by case, with no general rule yet in sight.

{pause: 1.0}

{tone: curious} If it does not resolve on its own, what would settle it? {pause: 1.4} {tone: measured} A judgment on the safeguard itself — {pause: 0.3} on what an organisation must supply in order for the right to contest to be a right anyone can exercise. On the sources this programme has, as of September 2026, no court has answered that question. That is an absence I am reporting, not a claim that none exists anywhere.

Or a legislature could settle it in a single sentence, by joining the two paragraphs that currently sit apart.

{pause: 0.8}

{tone: contemplative} Neither has happened.
