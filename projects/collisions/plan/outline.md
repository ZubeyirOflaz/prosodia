## The through-line

Every AI system in Europe is governed by more than one instrument at once, and the instruments were drafted by different people, at different times, about different things. This series takes the collisions one at a time — data protection against the product statute, then liability against both — and asks of each: **which rule actually bites, who it binds, and what falls through the seam.**

The series' normative question — **does layered protection actually protect, or does it multiply duties while leaving the same gap?** — is **held until Episode 10**, and the listener is told so, out loud, in Episode 1.

## The lens admission test, worked

**One lens episode in ten, not the two or three the ratio predicts. That is a deviation and here is the reason.**

**Episode 6 — what would count as an explanation.** *Judges rather than applies:* the distinction between a **justification** (an account addressed to an institution, showing the process was proper) and an **explanation** (an account addressed to a person, sufficient to make a decision contestable) appears in no provision of either instrument. It supplies the criterion by which a listener decides whether any disclosure duty is worth anything — and three later episodes are full of disclosure duties. *The single controversy considered and rejected:* **CK v Dun & Bradstreet Austria, C-203/22** `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: the judgment]`. It is the obvious candidate, and it fails condition two from both ends. As apparatus it teaches Article 15(1)(h), which **Episode 5 teaches anyway** — so an apparatus episode built on it would be Episode 5 under another title. And it cannot settle the argument in any case: the Court answered a question about *access* to meaningful information, while Wachter and Selbst are arguing about whether the Regulation obliges anyone to give *reasons for a particular decision*. A judgment that answers the adjacent question is not a controversy that carries the lens. *Two later apparatus episodes that invoke it, by number:* **Episode 8** — "the safety a person is entitled to expect" is a justification standard wearing an explanation's clothes — and **Episode 9**, where the law gives up on explanation entirely and shifts a burden instead. **Episode 7** invokes it a third time: two mandatory assessments, both addressed to institutions, neither to the person assessed.

**Why there is no second lens, stated so the producer can overrule me.** The ratio would give this series two or three. A second lens would need positions with named holders and a place they said it, and **this docket contains exactly one such exchange**. Every candidate I considered — regulatory layering against single-instrument design; data as property against data as relation; whether strict liability suits statistical systems — I could frame, and I could not attribute without reconstructing scholars from memory, which is the failure this docket was written to prevent. **Docket request 12 names what a second lens would need.** Until then, one lens, and the conceptual weight is carried inside the apparatus episodes where the docket can pay for it.

## Episode 1 — The Same File, Twice

**Length:** 38 min. Long because it carries nine load-bearing terms, the series' method, and the promise that the verdict is held.

**Prerequisites.** Series A, and these four things re-earned in a clause each as they arise, never as a recap: that an AI system is defined by inference; that the Act's duties fall on a *provider* and a *deployer*; that they attach when a thing is placed on a market or put into service; and that the Act is enforced by market surveillance and gives the person harmed no right to sue. **Nothing else from Series A is assumed.**

**Opening instance** — *type: an audit.* The **divergence attack**: researchers prompting a production chatbot to repeat a single word indefinitely until it abandons its assistant behaviour and begins emitting memorised training text, including contact details belonging to real people. `**Nasr, Carlini and eight others, "Scalable Extraction of Training Data from (Production) Language Models", arXiv:2311.17035, 28 November 2023** — see `research/03_is_a_model_personal_data.md``

*The move: open on somebody typing one word ten thousand times until a stranger's details fall out of a machine everyone had been told stores nothing.*

**Load-bearing terms** — a listener holding the ordinary meaning gets a wrong answer on something this episode teaches.
- **personal data** (4(1) ✓) — wrong: "data with your name on it", or "data you handed over". Right: any information *relating to* an identified or **identifiable** person, directly or indirectly. The wrong reading concludes a scraped corpus is anonymous, which is the single most consequential error in this series.
- **identifiable** — wrong: "somebody did identify them". Right: *can be* identified, including by reference to an identifier or to factors specific to their identity. It is a test about capability, not about what happened.
- **processing** (4(2) ✓) — wrong: "using the data". Right: any operation — collection, storage, structuring, adaptation, consultation, erasure. Training is processing. So is scraping. So is deleting. The wrong reading produces "we only trained on it".
- **pseudonymisation** (4(5) ✓) — wrong: "anonymised". Right: still personal data wherever the additional information exists, held separately. Wrong answer: that stripping names takes you outside the Regulation.
- **filing system** (4(6) ✓) — wrong: "a database". Right: a structured set accessible by specific criteria. The wrong reading makes the Regulation a law about records that look like records.
- **controller** (4(7) ✓) — wrong: "whoever is holding the data". Right: whoever determines the **purposes and means**. The wrong reading makes the hosting provider responsible and the scraper innocent.
- **processor** (4(8) ✓) — wrong: "the smaller company", or "whatever the contract says". Right: one who processes *on behalf of* the controller — and one who decides a purpose of their own is not a processor whatever the contract calls them.
- **recipient** (4(9) ✓) against **third party** (4(10) ✓) — wrong: interchangeable. Right: different definitions with different consequences, and the distinction decides who owes what after a transfer.
- **anonymous** — wrong: "the file has been anonymised, so we are outside the Regulation". Right: anonymity is a property of a **situation**, not of a file — and on the current case law the same file may be personal data for the party who sent it and not for the party who received it.

**The cast.** The scraper; the model provider — **provider** in Series A's vocabulary, **controller** here, and the collision is that those two words allocate responsibility by different tests; the business customer, a **deployer** under one instrument and a controller or processor under the other; the **data subject** who is in the corpus and was never anyone's customer; the supervisory authority; the **recipient** of a pseudonymised file who holds no key.

**The system, described operationally, before any rule applies.** Crawl, de-duplicate, tokenise; train by adjusting parameters against a prediction objective; ship the weights; serve outputs at inference. **There is no row anywhere.** What the weights encode about any individual sequence is a matter of degree, and the field's word for the recoverable end of that spectrum is **memorisation**, tested by extraction and membership-inference attacks. Machine unlearning — removing a training example's influence after the fact — is an active research area with no accepted guarantee. `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: a definition of memorisation and of membership inference from the extraction literature, so the description can be checked against the field's own words]`

**Does the system exist as described?** Yes, and that is why this opening was chosen over an argument. Extraction has been demonstrated against deployed commercial models, and a European court has separately found that song lyrics were recoverable from a production model in substantially their original form `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: Landgericht München I, GEMA v OpenAI, 42 O 14139/24, 11 November 2025 — used here as one sentence of technical corroboration, not taught]`.

**The operative text.**
> **Art. 4(1) ✓** — "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person ('data subject')" — and an identifiable person is one "who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier".
> **Art. 4(2) ✓** — "any operation or set of operations which is performed on personal data or on sets of personal data, whether or not by automated means, such as collection, recording, organisation, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use".
> **Art. 4(5) ✓, 4(7) ✓, 4(8) ✓** — pseudonymisation; the purposes-and-means test; on behalf of.

Called thereafter: **the subject test** (4(1)) and **the purposes-and-means test** (4(7)).

**Why this rule and not another.** The road not taken was a schedule of protected fields — name, address, identification number — which is how several national laws and most sectoral rules do it. Europe chose an open functional definition that cannot be evaded by inventing a new kind of identifier. What was traded: boundaries. A definition this wide puts almost everything in scope and moves the entire fight to identifiability, which is exactly where the fight now is.

**The legal turn, mid-episode.** `**Case C-413/23 P, *EDPS v Single Resolution Board*, Court of Justice, 4 September 2025** — see `research/03_is_a_model_personal_data.md`` **and** `**EDPB Opinion 28/2024, adopted 17 December 2024** — see `research/03_is_a_model_personal_data.md``. Together they make the episode's title literal: the same file, personal data in one building and not in the next.

**The variants — change one fact.**
1. The key is destroyed before the file is sent. The sender's own position changes, not only the recipient's.
2. The recipient is the firm that scraped the web in the first place and can re-link. Back inside, for both.
3. The model can be prompted to emit a training string that identifies someone. Then the weights are not merely *derived from* personal data — the argument that the artefact itself contains it becomes available, and the deployer who fine-tuned on support tickets is now a controller of data it never collected.

**The levels.** *Technical* — parameters, not records. *Organisational* — nobody owns the corpus; the team that built it has moved on. *Institutional* — supervisory authorities have treated pseudonymised data as always personal; the Court has not. *Economic* — deletion costs a retraining run, which is why "can we delete it" is never asked as a legal question alone. *Rights-based* — a person who never transacted with the controller and has no way to know they are in the set.

**The contested joints.** Absolute against relative identifiability — *contested, and moved by the 2025 judgment; the authorities' published position and the Court's reasoning no longer sit comfortably together*. Whether a trained model can itself be personal data — *contested*. Whether unlearning can be verified — *still moving; no standard, no accepted test*.

**What the rule assumes about the machine.** The Regulation assumes personal data sits somewhere you can point at — a record in a filing system — so that access, rectification and erasure have something to operate on. A model has parameters. **The claim that must land: this instrument's remedies were built for a filing cabinet, and a trained model is not one — which is why the argument moved from "delete my record" to "is this personal data at all".**

**As written against as enforced.** What has actually happened is undertakings, pauses and opt-outs, not deletions. No order to delete a model on data-protection grounds is known to this plan, and the episode should say that as a checkable fact with its date rather than as a generality.

**What it deliberately compresses, and says aloud.** The parts of this Regulation the series is not about: transfers to third countries, breach notification, data protection officers, certification, and the fines architecture. Named once, in one sentence, with the honest reason — this is a series about collisions, not a course on the GDPR — and **without implying that a later series takes them**.

**Say that the verdict is held, and why.** Near the close: the series will end by asking whether layering these instruments protects anybody, or only multiplies duties while leaving the same gap; it cannot be answered until the layers have been laid; **Episode 10** lands it. This promise must be heard here for Episode 10 to keep it.

**Handover.** If the corpus is personal data, then somebody needed a reason to touch it. There are six reasons in the Regulation and only one of them is ever used.

**Transfer questions.** 1. Who could re-identify this, with what they actually hold? 2. Is the claim of anonymity about the file, or about the situation? 3. If a person asked for deletion tomorrow, what exactly would be deleted — and could anyone verify it? 4. Who decided the purpose and the means here, and does the contract match what happened? 5. Can the system be made to emit something it was trained on?

**Sources.** Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Art. 4(1), (2), (5), (6), (7), (8), (9), (10), consolidated text 02016R0679-20160504, EUR-Lex. Plus, once the docket holds them: the extraction paper; C-413/23 P; EDPB Opinion 28/2024.

## Episode 2 — A Reason to Touch It

**Length:** 36 min.

**Prerequisites.** Episode 1 — personal data, processing, controller and processor, and that training is processing.

**Opening instance** — *type: a live controversy.* `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: the Irish Data Protection Commission's High Court proceedings against Twitter International Unlimited Company, issued 8 August 2024, over the processing of EU and EEA users' public posts to train the Grok model; the undertaking to suspend; the conclusion of the proceedings on 4 September 2024 on a permanent undertaking; the statutory power relied on; the date range of the processing already carried out; and the complainants who triggered it]`

*The move: a regulator goes to court to stop a training run that has already happened — and comes away with a promise about the future and nothing at all about the past.*

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **purpose limitation** (5(1)(b) ✓) — wrong: "say what you'll use it for". Right: purposes must be **specified, explicit and legitimate** at the moment of collection, and further processing must be *compatible* with them. "We later decided to train on it" is the textbook breach, not a grey area.
- **the compatibility test** (6(4) ✓) — wrong: "a balancing exercise". Right: five named factors, including the *context* of collection and the relationship between subject and controller. It is a structured test and the writer should walk two of the five, not all of them.
- **data minimisation** (5(1)(c) ✓) — wrong: "don't collect too much". Right: adequate, relevant and limited **in relation to the purposes**. Scale is not a defence; it is the thing being tested.
- **accuracy** (5(1)(d) ✓) — wrong: "the model should be accurate". Right: the *personal data* must be accurate and inaccurate data erased or rectified without delay. A system that confabulates about a named person is an accuracy problem under this principle, not a quality problem.
- **storage limitation** (5(1)(e) ✓) — wrong: "delete old files". Right: kept in a form permitting identification no longer than necessary — which is a question about the model, not only the corpus.
- **accountability** (5(2) ✓) — wrong: "be responsible". Right: be able to **demonstrate** compliance. It converts every principle above into a documentary burden.
- **consent** (4(11) ✓) — wrong: "a tick-box". Right: freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, by a statement or clear affirmative action.
- **legitimate interests** (6(1)(f) ✓) — wrong: "we have a business reason". Right: three cumulative limbs, the third of which is a balance the controller can lose, and which excludes public authorities performing their tasks.
- **necessary** — wrong: "useful", "commercially sensible". Right: there is no less intrusive way to achieve the purpose. The word appears in five of the six bases and decides most of them.
- **further processing for research purposes** (5(1)(b) ✓) — wrong: "we're a lab, so it's fine". Right: a carve-out tied to Article 89(1) safeguards, and not a licence.

**The cast.** The platform as controller; the model developer, which may be a different legal person in the same group; the lead supervisory authority; the consumer organisations who complained; the users, whose posts were public and who were not asked.

**The system, described operationally.** Posts that are public by default; a pipeline that selects, filters and de-duplicates them into a training set; a training run; and, separately, retrieval at inference time, which is a different operation with a different lawful-basis question. **Keep those two apart** — most public argument about this conflates them. `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: the field's own account of what was trained on and when — and note that the operator's description of its own pipeline is evidence about the operator, not about the system]`

**The operative text.**
> **Art. 5(1)(a)–(e) ✓ and 5(2) ✓** — lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; accountability.
> **Art. 6(1)(a)–(f) ✓** — the six bases, of which (f) is the one that matters here: "necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject".
> **Art. 6(4)(a)–(e) ✓** — the compatibility factors.

Called thereafter: **the purpose rule**, **the six doors**, and **the compatibility test**.

**Why this rule and not another.** The alternative, argued through the 1990s, was consent for everything. Six bases with a balancing test were chosen so that ordinary processing could be lawful without consent theatre. What was traded: the balancing door is the one everybody walks through, it is assessed in the first instance **by the controller itself**, and the assessment surfaces only when a regulator asks.

**The variants.**
1. The posts were public. Does that change the basis? For ordinary personal data, barely — publicity is a factor in the balance, not a door. For special categories it is a door, and that is Episode 3.
2. The opt-out is offered *before* the training run rather than after. The balance changes and the question becomes whether an opt-out satisfies it at all.
3. The corpus is bought from a third party. The buyer inherits a question it cannot answer, and the consequences of unlawful development for lawful deployment are genuinely unsettled `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: EDPB Opinion 28/2024's fourth question and its answer]`.

**The levels.** *Technical* — a filtering script decides what "public" meant. *Organisational* — the legitimate-interests assessment is a document written by the party it exonerates. *Institutional* — one lead authority for a platform serving thirty countries. *Economic* — the cost of asking is the loss of the data. *Rights-based* — a person whose posts were public to readers, not to a training run.

**The contested joints.** Whether legitimate interests can cover model training at all — *contested; the Board says it can, conditionally, and the current legislative proposal would say so expressly*. Whether an opt-out can carry the balance — *contested*. Whether unlawful development taints deployment — *contested, and commercially the largest open question in this series*.

**What the rule assumes about the machine.** Purpose limitation assumes a purpose knowable at collection and a bounded set of downstream uses. A foundation model is built precisely so that its uses are *not* specified in advance — that is what "general-purpose" means. **The claim that must land: this Regulation asks what the data is for, and the answer that makes the model valuable is "we don't know yet".**

**As written against as enforced.** The enforcement pattern in this area is undertakings, pauses and opt-outs rather than deletions or refusals. The one substantial fine imposed on a generative model's legal basis was later set aside on jurisdiction rather than on the merits — **one sentence, no retelling; a listener who followed Series A has heard the story and a listener who has not does not need it.**

**Deliberately compressed, and said aloud.** Whether it was lawful to *copy* the training data — as against lawful to *process* it — is a different instrument with different exceptions, and a later series. **This is one of the three spoken deferrals: permission to forget, not a promise to remember.**

**Handover.** Some of what is in that corpus may not be processed at all, on any basis, by anyone — and a machine can work out the rest.

**Transfer questions.** 1. What purpose was stated when this data was collected, and by whom? 2. Which door is this walking through, and who wrote the assessment? 3. Is "necessary" being used to mean "no other way", or to mean "this is how we do it"? 4. If the answer is legitimate interests, what would losing the balance look like? 5. What happens to this model if the basis for its training is found to be wrong?

**Sources.** Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Arts. 5 and 6, consolidated text, EUR-Lex. Plus, once held: the DPC proceedings; EDPB Guidelines 1/2024; EDPB Opinion 28/2024.

**Writability: NOT YET WRITABLE.** Three outside items. **Docket requests 3, 4 and 5.** With them, the single check is the date range of the processing covered by the undertaking.

## Episode 3 — The Category It Cannot Ask For

**Length:** 38 min. Long because it carries eleven load-bearing terms and the sharpest collision in the series; each term is given room rather than named in passing.

**Prerequisites.** Episodes 1 and 2 — personal data; that processing needs a basis; what "necessary" does.

**Opening instance** — *type: a decided case.* `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: OT v Vyriausioji tarnybinės etikos komisija, Case C-184/20, Court of Justice, Grand Chamber, judgment of 1 August 2022 — the facts (a Lithuanian anti-corruption statute requiring publication of a declaration of private interests naming the declarant's spouse, cohabitee or partner), the holding that such publication is liable to disclose data concerning sexual orientation and therefore engages the special-categories prohibition, and the operative part]`

*The move: a form that never asked the question, and answered it anyway.*

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **special categories** (9(1) ✓) — wrong: "sensitive information, handle with care". Right: a **closed list**, and processing is **prohibited** unless one of ten doors opens. The default is no.
- **revealing** (9(1) ✓) — wrong: "stating". Right: data from which the category can be derived. This one word is the whole episode.
- **biometric data** (4(14) ✓) — wrong: "any photograph of a face". Right: data resulting from specific technical processing, **for the purpose of uniquely identifying** a person. A face photo in an album is not biometric data; the same photo in a matching pipeline is.
- **data concerning health** (4(15) ✓) — wrong: "medical records". Right: any personal data related to physical or mental health that *reveals information about health status*, which pulls in inferences drawn from non-medical data.
- **genetic data** (4(13) ✓) — wrong: "DNA on file". Right: data giving unique information about physiology or health, resulting in particular from analysis of a sample.
- **explicit consent** (9(2)(a) ✓) — wrong: the same as consent. Right: a higher bar than 4(11), and Member States may legislate so that it cannot be given at all.
- **manifestly made public by the data subject** (9(2)(e) ✓) — wrong: "it was on the internet". Right: made public **by the subject**, and *manifestly*. The scraping defence's weakest joint, and the episode should press it.
- **substantial public interest** (9(2)(g) ✓) — wrong: "it's important". Right: requires a legal basis in Union or Member State law, proportionate, respecting the essence of the right, with specific safeguards.
- **the research door** (9(2)(j) ✓) — wrong: a general licence for model builders.
- **further conditions by Member States** (9(4) ✓) — wrong: "the GDPR is uniform". Right: on genetic, biometric and health data, national law may add conditions and limits. Twenty-seven answers to the same question.
- **inference** — Series A's word, meeting this Regulation's "revealing". A listener holding the AI Act's meaning and the GDPR's meaning separately will not see that they name the same event from two sides.

**The cast.** OT, a named person required by law to expose his household; the national ethics commission; the model builder who never collected a sensitive field; the bias auditor who needs one to do their job; the supervisory authority that must decide whether a prediction is processing.

**The system, described operationally.** How a classifier acquires a protected attribute it was never given: correlated features, proxies, redundant encodings — postcode for ethnicity, purchase timing for pregnancy, typing cadence for a motor condition. Removing the attribute from the inputs does not remove it from the model; the field's name for the naive fix is *fairness through unawareness*, and it is known not to work. The corollary is the trap: **measuring** whether a system discriminates requires holding the very attribute the law prohibits you from holding. `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: one citation from the algorithmic-fairness literature for the proxy problem and for the measurement requirement]`

**The operative text.**
> **Art. 9(1) ✓** — processing of personal data "**revealing** racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person, data concerning health or data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation **shall be prohibited**."
> **Art. 9(2)(a), (e), (g), (j) ✓** — four of the ten doors; the writer opens these four and names the rest as a set, without enumerating them.
> **Art. 9(4) ✓** — the national add-ons.

Called thereafter: **the prohibition** and **the doors**.

**Why this rule and not another.** The road not taken was a general anti-discrimination rule policing outcomes. Europe instead built a category-based prohibition on *inputs*, because the historical harms it was answering — registries, censuses, lists — were input harms. What was traded: an input rule has nothing to say to a system that manufactures the input itself.

**The collision, and it is the series' sharpest.** The AI Act permits providers to process special-category data where strictly necessary to detect and correct bias in high-risk systems — Series A taught that provision and this episode does not re-teach it. So one instrument creates a permission inside a prohibition that belongs to another instrument. And the live legislative proposal would add the matching door to Article 9 itself, together with an express basis for AI development `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: the Digital Omnibus proposal's amendments to Articles 6 and 9, their current status as at the week of recording, and the EDPB–EDPS objection to the deployment-phase scope]`. **Date-stamp everything in this beat.**

**The variants.**
1. A model given no sensitive field predicts ethnicity correctly four times in five. Is that processing of special-category data? *Genuinely unresolved, and the episode must not pretend otherwise.*
2. The data was scraped from profiles where people stated their religion themselves. Door (e) opens — and then the word "manifestly" has to carry the weight.
3. The output is used to decide credit. A second rule bites, in the next episode but one.

**The levels.** *Technical* — a proxy nobody chose. *Organisational* — the bias audit that cannot be run without the forbidden field. *Institutional* — two instruments legislating on the same prohibition from opposite directions. *Economic* — the cheapest compliance posture is to know nothing and be able to prove nothing. *Rights-based* — the category exists because of what has been done with it before, **and that is where this episode stops: how a court tests a limit on a right is Series C.** *(Spoken deferral, once.)*

**The contested joints.** Whether an inferred attribute is "revealed" within 9(1) — *contested; the 2022 judgment leans towards yes for indirect disclosure, and it was about publication, not prediction*. Whether proxy-based prediction is Article 9 processing — *contested*. Whether the bias-detection permission works without an Article 9 door — *contested, and the reason the amendment was proposed*.

**What the rule assumes about the machine.** Article 9 assumes sensitivity is a property of **data**: that a controller can look at a field and know whether it is inside the prohibition, and that declining to collect is a way of declining to process. A model makes sensitivity a property of **inference**. **The claim that must land: you cannot decline to disclose what can be predicted — and to prove the machine is not discriminating, you must hold the data you are forbidden to hold.**

**As written against as enforced.** Enforcement here is real and biometric-heavy; the prohibition has produced some of the largest penalties in the Regulation's history. What has not been enforced is the inference case: no decision known to this plan holds that predicting a protected attribute is itself Article 9 processing. **State that as an absence with its date.**

**Handover.** If the machine can work out the category, the next question is whether it is allowed to *decide* anything with it — and there is one article about that, with one judgment behind it.

**Transfer questions.** 1. Which of these categories could this system predict, whether or not it was asked to? 2. Is the claim that no sensitive data is processed a claim about the inputs or about the outputs? 3. If somebody wanted to audit this for bias, what would they have to be allowed to hold? 4. Was this data manifestly made public — by the subject, or about them? 5. Which instrument is supplying the permission here, and does it own the prohibition it is carving into?

**Sources.** Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Art. 9(1), (2)(a), (e), (g), (j), (4), and Art. 4(13)–(15), consolidated text, EUR-Lex. Plus, once held: C-184/20; the fairness citation; the current status of the proposed amendments.

**Writability: NOT YET WRITABLE.** Four outside items, which is over the line. **Docket requests 5, 6 and 7**, plus the AI Act's bias-detection provision in its own words from Series A's docket — a copy across, not new research.

## Episode 4 — Solely

**Length:** 36 min. One article, four paragraphs, and the only Grand Chamber judgment this docket holds; the article count understates it.

**Prerequisites.** Episodes 1–3 — personal data; a basis; that the prohibition on special categories exists.

**Opening instance** — *type: a decided case.* **Case C-634/21, *SCHUFA Holding (Scoring)*, Court of Justice, Grand Chamber, judgment of 7 December 2023 ▲.** OQ was refused a loan by a German bank. The bank had relied on a credit-repayment probability score produced by SCHUFA, a credit reference agency. **OQ went after SCHUFA, not the bank.** The Court held that producing the score is itself an automated individual decision within Article 22(1) **where the third party draws strongly on that score** to establish, implement or terminate a contract — so the Article 22 obligations fall on the scoring agency, not only on the lender that acted.

*The move: a man sues the company that did not decide — and wins the point.*

⚠ **The docket paraphrases the operative part; it does not hold the judgment.** The formulation "draws strongly on" is the hinge of this episode and **must be taken from the judgment itself before a word of it is quoted aloud**. This is the episode's single fact-check.

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **decision** — wrong: "the final answer, the yes or no". Right: after this judgment, the **score** can be the decision. The wrong reading puts every model vendor outside the article.
- **based solely on automated processing** — wrong: "no human being was involved anywhere". Right: no *meaningful* human involvement — a person who approves without authority, information or time has not made the decision.
- **profiling** (4(4) ✓) — wrong: "building a marketing profile". Right: **any** form of automated processing used to evaluate personal aspects, in particular to analyse or predict performance at work, economic situation, health, reliability, behaviour or location. Far wider than the ordinary word.
- **legal effects… or similarly significantly affects** — wrong: "anything that matters to me". Right: a threshold, with contested edges — a refused loan is in; a reordered feed is argued about.
- **the right not to be subject to** (22(1) ✓) — wrong: "an opt-out I have to exercise". Right: read by the Court as a **prohibition subject to exceptions**, not a right you must invoke. A listener holding the wrong reading thinks silence means consent.
- **necessary for entering into or performance of a contract** (22(2)(a) ✓) — wrong: "it's how we do business". Right: necessity, strictly, and the burden is the controller's.
- **suitable measures** (22(3) ✓) — wrong: "safeguards generally". Right: **at least** three — human intervention on the controller's part, the ability to express a point of view, and the ability to contest the decision.
- **decisions shall not be based on special categories** (22(4) ✓) — wrong: "sensitive decisions need care". Right: a near-prohibition, with only two doors and safeguards on top.

**The cast.** OQ; the bank, which decided and is a **deployer** in Series A's vocabulary and a **controller** here; SCHUFA, which decided nothing and is both a provider and a controller; the Hessian supervisory authority; the referring administrative court.

**The system, described operationally.** A credit score: a feature set (payment history, account age, queries, addresses), a model trained on historical repayment outcomes, an output that is a probability, and — crucially — **a cut-off that lives somewhere else**, in the lender's policy. The score is continuous; the decision is a threshold applied to it. **Does it exist as described? Yes, and it is one of the oldest deployed decision systems in Europe** — which is why the first Article 22 judgment is about credit and not about anything newer.

**The operative text.**
> **Art. 22(1) ✓** — "the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her."
> **Art. 22(2)(a)–(c) ✓** — contract; Union or Member State law with safeguards; explicit consent.
> **Art. 22(3) ✓** — the three minimum measures. **Art. 22(4) ✓** — the special-category bar.

Called thereafter: **the solely rule** and **the three safeguards**.

**Why this rule and not another.** The road not taken was to regulate the **outcome** — a rule that a credit decision must not discriminate, whoever or whatever makes it. Europe regulated the **process**: who or what decided, and what you may demand afterwards. What was traded, and it is the criticism that has never been answered: **Article 22 is satisfied by adding a human, and says nothing about whether the decision was right.**

**The variants.**
1. A human reviews every file and follows the score in ninety-nine cases out of a hundred. Is the decision still "solely" automated? The answer that has emerged is that a review with no authority to differ is not a review — **but plant this as the question and let Episode 5 bring the court that said so.**
2. The score sets a price rather than refusing the loan. Now "similarly significantly affects" is doing the work, and the answer is genuinely arguable.
3. SCHUFA sells to twelve lenders, eleven of whom use it as one factor among many. The "draws strongly on" hinge moves, and the same company is inside the article for one customer and outside it for another.

**The levels.** *Technical* — a probability. *Organisational* — a threshold in a policy document, which is where the real decision lives and which no instrument in this series regulates. *Institutional* — a private credit bureau operating as national infrastructure. *Economic* — the cost of a false refusal falls entirely on the applicant, who cannot see it. *Rights-based* — contestability, and whether it is worth anything without reasons.

**The contested joints.** Whether 22(1) is a prohibition or a right to be invoked — *contested; the Court leans prohibition*. What "similarly significantly affects" reaches — *contested, and the pressure point is pricing and advertising*. Whether human review at volume can ever be meaningful — *still moving*.

**What the rule assumes about the machine.** Article 22 assumes a decision has a **moment** and a **decider**. A modern stack distributes both: a vendor produces a number, a policy sets a cut-off, a caseworker works a queue in the order the number gave them. The Court's answer was to walk the duty upstream to whoever made the number. **The claim that must land: the law went looking for where the decision actually was, and found it in a company that had never met the man and never said no to him.**

**As written against as enforced.** What OQ got was a ruling about roles and a case sent back. Not money, and not a loan. The same facts today would also reach the AI Act — credit scoring is on its high-risk list — which would give the deployer an assessment duty, the provider a conformity assessment, and OQ a complaint and an explanation. **Two instruments, one refused loan, and still no cheque.** *(This is Episode 10's second deciding fact; plant it plainly and do not argue it here.)*

**Deliberately compressed, and said aloud.** The Member State law exception in 22(2)(b) is named and not explored; it is where national welfare and tax systems live, and the episode says it is leaving them.

**Handover.** The safeguards promise the man a right to contest. To contest something you have to be told what it was — and that turns out to be four articles and an argument.

**Transfer questions.** 1. Who or what actually decided here — the model, the threshold, or the person who followed it? 2. Could the human in this process have said no, and did they have what they would have needed to? 3. Does this produce a legal effect, or something that merely feels significant? 4. If the score-maker is not the decision-maker, who owes the duty? 5. What would contesting this decision consist of, concretely, tomorrow morning?

**Sources.** Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Art. 22(1)–(4), Art. 4(4), consolidated text, EUR-Lex. Case C-634/21, *SCHUFA Holding (Scoring)*, Grand Chamber, 7 December 2023.

**Writability: WRITABLE NOW.** The docket holds the article verbatim and the case as a worked fact pattern. **The single fact-check: the operative part of C-634/21, retrieved from the Court's own text, because the episode quotes the test.**

## Episode 5 — Meaningful Information

**Length:** 34 min.

**Prerequisites.** Episodes 1–4 — controller; basis; profiling; the solely rule and the three safeguards.

**Opening instance** — *type: a decided case.* `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: the three linked judgments of the Amsterdam Court of Appeal of 4 April 2023 in the proceedings brought by drivers against Uber and Ola Cabs — the ECLI numbers, which processes the court held to be automated decisions within Article 22 (ride allocation, fraud-probability scoring, deactivation), what the drivers were actually given in answer to their access requests, and the passage describing the companies' human review as little more than a symbolic act]`

*The move: workers ask for their own file, are handed almost nothing, and a court reads the nothing as the answer.*

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **meaningful information about the logic involved** (13(2)(f), 14(2)(g), 15(1)(h) ✓) — wrong: "the source code" or "the algorithm". Right: information about the procedure and principles by which the result was reached, in a form the person can use. Both wrong readings — that it means everything, and that it means nothing — lose.
- **the significance and the envisaged consequences** ✓ — wrong: "what the system does". Right: what it does **to you**.
- **concise, transparent, intelligible and easily accessible… clear and plain language** (12(1) ✓) — wrong: a privacy policy satisfies this.
- **without undue delay and in any event within one month** (12(3) ✓) — the deadline, extendable by two further months for complexity, with notice inside the first month. **Spoken as two numbers in one sentence, never as a table.**
- **manifestly unfounded or excessive** (12(5) ✓) — wrong: "we can refuse awkward requests". Right: a narrow exception where **the controller bears the burden of demonstrating it**.
- **a copy of the personal data undergoing processing** (15(3) ✓) — wrong: "a summary of what we hold".
- **rights and freedoms of others** (15(4) ✓) — wrong: "trade secrets end the enquiry". Right: a limit that cannot be used to empty the right.
- **disproportionate effort** (14(5)(b) ✓) — wrong: "it would be hard to contact everyone". Right: a narrow exception tied to research and archiving contexts — and the hatch every scraper reaches for.
- **information the data subject already has** (13(4), 14(5)(a) ✓) — wrong: "it's obvious what we do".

**The cast.** The drivers, with names in the judgments; the two platforms as controllers; the unions and the data-rights organisation that ran the litigation; the Dutch courts; and the "human in the loop", who is a cast member in this episode and not a safeguard.

**The system, described operationally.** Allocation, scoring and deactivation as three different processes with three different degrees of automation; a fraud-probability score with a threshold and a review queue; a review that consists of confirming a flag. **The provider's account of its own review process is the object of study here, not the description** — the entire case turned on the gap between what the companies said the humans did and what the record showed.

**The operative text.**
> **Art. 15(1)(h) ✓** — "the existence of automated decision-making, including profiling, referred to in Article 22(1) and (4) and, at least in those cases, **meaningful information about the logic involved**, as well as the significance and the envisaged consequences of such processing for the data subject."
> **Art. 12(1), (3), (5) ✓** · **Art. 13(2)(f) ✓** · **Art. 14(2)(g) and 14(5)(b) ✓** · **Art. 15(3), (4) ✓**.

Called thereafter: **the telling duties** (13 and 14) and **the asking right** (15).

**Why this rule and not another.** The road not taken was a registry: publish what your systems do, once, for everybody. Europe built a **subject-facing** duty instead — information delivered to the person, on request or at collection. What was traded: an individual duty scales badly and is enforced one complaint at a time, and Article 14's exception for disproportionate effort was written for archives and now does most of its work for web-scale collection.

**The legal turn, mid-episode.** `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: CK v Dun & Bradstreet Austria, C-203/22, Court of Justice, judgment of 27 February 2025 — the holding on what "meaningful information about the logic involved" requires, and its treatment of trade secrets, which are put before the supervisory authority or the court rather than allowed to defeat the right]`

**The collision.** Three explanation duties now sit on the same system with three different addressees: this Regulation tells the *person*; the AI Act tells the *deployer* what the system is and, for certain decisions, gives the affected person an explanation — Series A taught both, and this episode only marks where they overlap and where they do not. **The overlap is narrower than either instrument's advocates claim, and that is the finding.**

**The variants.**
1. The controller hands over the feature list and the weights. Satisfies nothing — it is information about the model, not about the decision.
2. The controller hands over a counterfactual: had this one input been different, the answer would have changed. Arguably satisfies, and it is the proposal the next episode argues about.
3. The model belongs to a vendor and the deployer genuinely cannot explain it. The duty does not move; the deployer's contract problem is not the person's problem.

**The levels.** *Technical* — feature attributions that are unstable across methods. *Organisational* — a request queue staffed by people with no access to the model. *Institutional* — litigation funded by a union because individuals cannot run it. *Economic* — the cost of answering properly is the reason not to. *Rights-based* — contestability as the only test that matters.

**The contested joints.** Whether "the logic involved" requires case-specific reasons or system-level description — *contested, and Episode 6 is about exactly this*. Whether trade secrets can narrow the answer — *moved in 2025; leaning against*. Whether Article 14's disproportionate-effort exception covers web-scale collection — *contested, and largely untested*.

**What the rule assumes about the machine.** These provisions assume a decision has **reasons that can be stated** — that somewhere there is a *because*. A learned model has weights and a counterfactual. **The claim that must land: the Regulation asks for the reason; the machine can only offer the difference that would have changed the answer — and whether that is the same thing is the next episode.**

**Handover.** We have a duty to give meaningful information, and no agreement on what would count. **This is a switch: one episode away from the text, and then we are back inside it for good.**

**Transfer questions.** 1. If I asked this operator what they hold about me, what would arrive, and in how long? 2. Is what I am being given about the system, or about my case? 3. Could I use it to contest anything? 4. Who decided that answering properly would be disproportionate? 5. When the answer is "that's a trade secret", who gets to see it instead of me?

**Sources.** Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Arts. 12, 13, 14, 15, consolidated text, EUR-Lex. Plus, once held: the Amsterdam judgments; C-203/22.

**Writability: NOT YET WRITABLE — but the closest of the unwritable ones.** Two outside items. **Docket requests 8 and 2.** With them, the single check is the quoted characterisation of the human review.

## Episode 6 — [LENS] What Would Count As An Explanation

**Length:** 28 min. Short because the argument is small and sharp, and padding it is the failure mode.

**Prerequisites.** Episodes 4 and 5 — Article 22, the three safeguards, and the phrase "meaningful information about the logic involved".

**Opening anchor** — *type: a published disagreement.* **One sentence of the Regulation, read once, and two readings of it that cannot both be right.** *Announce plainly: this episode teaches no provision. It is about how to judge the ones we have.*

**The positions and their ablest defenders.** Every position below has a named holder and a place they said it; where I am supplying the framing rather than reporting a scholar, it is labelled as mine.

1. **There is no right to an explanation in this Regulation.** **Sandra Wachter, Brent Mittelstadt and Luciano Floridi, "Why a Right to Explanation of Automated Decision-Making Does Not Exist in the General Data Protection Regulation", *International Data Privacy Law* 7(2) (2017), p. 76 ✓.** The information provisions create a **right to be informed** — meaningful but limited information about system functionality, its significance and its envisaged consequences, delivered around the time of processing. That is a different and much smaller thing than an account of *why this particular decision came out this way*. *A response to:* the confident post-2016 claim, made in the press and in the literature, that Europe had legislated a right to explanation. *Consequence of adopting it:* a controller complies by publishing a good system description and owes the individual nothing about their own case.
2. **Then build something that does the work instead: the counterfactual.** **Wachter, Mittelstadt and Russell, "Counterfactual Explanations Without Opening the Black Box", *Harvard Journal of Law & Technology* 31 (2018) ✓.** Tell the person what would have had to be different for the answer to change. It is useful to them, it supports contesting, and it does not require opening the model or exposing the training data. ⚠ **The issue and page numbers are not established in the docket. The writer attributes and paraphrases and speaks no page number.**
3. **"Meaningful" means usable, and usable means explanation.** **Andrew D. Selbst and Julia Powles, "Meaningful information and the right to explanation", *International Data Privacy Law* 7(4), November 2017, pp. 233–242, DOI 10.1093/idpl/ipx022 ✓** — published in the same journal, months later, answering the first paper by title. Information is not meaningful unless it lets the person **do** something: contest the decision, or exercise the other rights the Regulation gives them. Read that way, the provisions already require explanation in the sense that matters, whatever the drafters thought they were writing. *Consequence:* every automated decision of consequence needs a case-specific account, and the trade-secret objection has to yield.
4. **The Court has answered an adjacent question, not this one.** `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: C-203/22, 27 February 2025 — the holding, so that this position can be stated in the Court's words rather than mine]` The judgment is about **access** to meaningful information under Article 15(1)(h). Whether Article 22 obliges anyone to give *reasons* remains open. *This framing of the gap is mine, and the script must say so.*
5. **Justification against explanation.** **This distinction is the series' own framing and is labelled as such on air, not attributed to any scholar.** A **justification** is addressed to an institution and shows the process was proper: a risk assessment, a conformity file, a model card. An **explanation** is addressed to a person and earns the name only by making the decision contestable. Almost everything the instruments in this series require is a justification. `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: if the docket later supplies a scholarly source that draws this distinction in these terms, attribute it; until then it is the narrator's.]`

**The contested joint, and the awkward part said out loud.** All four scholars want people to be able to challenge decisions. What they disagree about is whether the **text** delivers it — which means the argument is partly about statutory interpretation and partly about whether it is honest to read a right into a provision because we need one. **Do not resolve it by picking the nicer-sounding side.** Where the weight leans: *on the narrow question of what the 2016 text says, the sceptics have the better of the drafting history; on what "meaningful" must mean once a court has to apply it, the reply has been closer to what actually happened.*

**Applied to, by number.** **Episode 8** — "the safety a person is entitled to expect" is a justification standard, and a court will have to decide what a person is entitled to expect of a statistical system. **Episode 9** — where the law stops asking for explanation and shifts a burden instead, which is the most important move in this series. **Episode 7** invokes it too: two mandatory assessments, both addressed to institutions.

**Handover.** If nobody can explain the decision, the law's other move is to change who has to prove what. **Back inside the text — and into an instrument that, until recently, did not think software was a thing at all.**

**Transfer questions.** 1. Who is this account addressed to — a regulator, or the person it happened to? 2. Could someone use it to contest the outcome, and if not, what is it for? 3. Is the operator explaining the system, or explaining my case? 4. What would have had to be different for the answer to change — and can they tell me? 5. When someone says explanation is impossible, are they describing the model or declining the request?

**Sources.** Wachter, Mittelstadt and Floridi, *IDPL* 7(2) (2017) 76. Wachter, Mittelstadt and Russell, *Harv. J.L. & Tech.* 31 (2018). Selbst and Powles, *IDPL* 7(4) (2017) 233–242. Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Arts. 15(1)(h) and 22(3).

## Episode 7 — Two Assessments, One System

**Length:** 36 min.

**Prerequisites.** Episodes 1, 2 and 3 — controller; purpose; minimisation; special categories. From Series A, in one clause each: that high-risk systems carry engineering duties, and that deployers of some of them owe a fundamental-rights impact assessment.

**Opening instance** — *type: a documentary state.* `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: the list of national competent authorities notified under the AI Act — which office each Member State named as its market-surveillance authority, how many named their data protection authority, how many had not notified by the deadline, and the date the list was last updated]` and `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: "Should data protection authorities enforce the AI Act? Lessons from EU-wide enforcement data", International Data Privacy Law (2026) — for the empirical claim about capacity]`

*The move: read a list of names and find the same office on it twice, under two statutes, with two different sets of powers and one budget.*

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **data protection by design and by default** (25(1), (2) ✓) — wrong: "privacy-friendly settings". Right: a duty biting **at the time of determining the means** — at architecture and procurement, before any data moves — and again during processing.
- **state of the art** (25(1) ✓) — wrong: "the best available". Right: a moving standard the controller is obliged to track, which is an odd thing to say to a field that moves quarterly.
- **likely to result in a high risk** (35(1) ✓) — wrong: "high-risk under the AI Act". Right: **a different test with a different object** — risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons arising from *processing*, not risk arising from a product. Two instruments use the same English word for two different things, and a listener who conflates them gets every allocation question wrong.
- **prior to the processing** (35(1) ✓) — wrong: "before go-live". Right: before the processing, which for a model means before the training run.
- **a systematic and extensive evaluation… on which decisions are based** (35(3)(a) ✓) — the trigger that catches profiling systems.
- **large scale** (35(3)(b) ✓) — undefined in the text, and the episode says so rather than inventing a threshold.
- **the assessment shall contain at least** (35(7) ✓) — four elements: a systematic description, a necessity and proportionality assessment, a risk assessment, and the measures envisaged. **Spoken as four short phrases in one breath, never enumerated as a list.**
- **review** (35(11) ✓) — wrong: "an annual refresh". Right: triggered by a change in the risk, which for a continuously updated model has no obvious moment.
- **main establishment** (4(16) ✓), **supervisory authority concerned** (4(22) ✓), **cross-border processing** (4(23) ✓), **relevant and reasoned objection** (4(24) ✓) — the one-stop-shop vocabulary. Wrong reading: "you complain to your own regulator and they handle it". Right: one lead authority, determined by where the central administration sits, and the others may only object in a defined way.

**The cast.** The controller, who owes the DPIA; the deployer, who may owe a second assessment under the other instrument; the data protection officer, who must be consulted; the supervisory authority; the market-surveillance authority, which in some Member States is the same people; and the person assessed, who is mentioned in 35(9) as someone whose views may be sought "where appropriate" and is otherwise absent.

**The system, described operationally.** What an assessment actually consists of in practice: a description written from a design document, a risk register, a mitigation list, and a sign-off. **What it is not is a test of the system.** The distinction between assessing a *processing operation* and evaluating a *model* is the technical heart of this episode, and the writer should make it concrete: an accuracy figure is not a risk assessment, and a risk register has never detected a proxy.

**The operative text.**
> **Art. 25(1) ✓** — "both at the time of the determination of the means for processing and at the time of the processing itself, implement appropriate technical and organisational measures".
> **Art. 25(2) ✓** — by default, only personal data necessary for each specific purpose, covering amount, extent of processing, storage period and accessibility.
> **Art. 35(1), (3)(a)–(c), (7), (9), (11) ✓.** **Art. 36** is named in one sentence as what happens when the risk cannot be mitigated; its text is not held and it is not taught.

Called thereafter: **by design**, and **the impact assessment**.

**Why this rule and not another.** The road not taken was licensing: a regulator approves the system before it runs. Europe chose **self-assessment with documentation**, because licensing does not scale and because the 1995 regime's prior-notification system had collapsed under its own paperwork. What was traded: an instrument whose central discipline is a document the regulated party writes about itself, which is the accountability paradox in miniature — **and whose paradox it is, and whether the document means anything, is Series D.** *(Spoken deferral, once.)*

**The collision, stated as the episode's spine.** One system, two mandatory assessments, two vocabularies of risk, two authorities, and one bridging provision — the AI Act tells deployers to use the provider's information to help meet their obligation under Article 35 here, which is a legislature admitting the seam exists. And underneath it, a genuine contradiction of instruction: **minimisation and storage limitation say collect and keep less; the product statute's data-governance, logging and post-market duties say keep enough to prove it worked.** Both are binding on the same organisation about the same system on the same day.

**The variants.**
1. The deployer is a public authority in a Member State whose DPA is also the market-surveillance authority. One office, two mandates, two standards of proof.
2. The provider is established outside the Union. The one-stop-shop has nobody to lead, and the Union enforcement gap that produced a well-known annulment opens again — **one sentence, no retelling**.
3. The system is updated weekly. The review trigger in 35(11) never fires, because nothing ever changes enough to count.

**The levels.** *Technical* — a document about a model rather than a test of one. *Organisational* — the assessment is written by the team that wants the project approved. *Institutional* — two regulators, one of whom has thirty years of practice and the other of whom has no case law. *Economic* — assessment costs are fixed, so they fall hardest on the smallest deployers. *Rights-based* — the person assessed may be consulted "where appropriate".

**The contested joints.** Whether DPAs should be the AI Act's enforcers — *contested, and the empirical question is capacity*. Whether one document can satisfy both instruments — *contested; the bridging provision assumes it can be helped, not merged*. Whether "high risk" in two statutes can safely share a word — *settled among specialists that it cannot; unsettled everywhere else*.

**What the rule assumes about the machine.** Article 35 assumes the controller can describe the processing **systematically and in advance** and assess necessity and proportionality against a purpose. A model's behaviour is discovered by evaluation *after* training; its risks are found by use. The assessment is therefore written about a system that does not yet exist in the form that will cause the harm. **The claim that must land: both instruments ask for an assessment before the thing exists, and neither has a mechanism for the moment somebody finds out what it actually does.**

**As written against as enforced.** This is the one part of the Regulation where the paper duty has produced very large penalties — failing to assess is enforceable and has been enforced, including against a facial-recognition company whose fine a listener of Series A will already know about, **named in one clause and not retold**. The product statute's equivalent assessment has produced nothing yet.

**What it deliberately compresses, and says aloud.** The one-stop-shop is taught as vocabulary and mechanism, not as procedure: the dispute-resolution machinery is named in one sentence and not walked. **The episode says that a whole cooperation chapter has been summarised.**

**Handover.** Both instruments ask organisations to assess risk before harm. Neither says who pays when the harm arrives — and for that there is a third instrument, forty years old, rewritten last year.

**Transfer questions.** 1. Which assessment does this system need, under which instrument, and are they the same document? 2. Who wrote it, and what did they want the answer to be? 3. Does "high risk" here mean risk to this person, or risk from this product? 4. Which authority supervises this, and have they ever supervised anything like it? 5. What would have to change before anyone re-opened the assessment?

**Sources.** Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Arts. 25, 35, and Art. 4(16), (21), (22), (23), (24), consolidated text, EUR-Lex. Plus, once held: the authorities list; the enforcement-capacity study.

## Episode 8 — A Product Now

**Length:** 38 min. Long because it carries twelve load-bearing terms — the sanity ceiling — and each is given room. If this episode has to be split, the cut line is named in the load flags above.

**Prerequisites.** Episodes 1–7 for the data-protection half of the series, and from Series A, in a clause: that the AI Act regulates systems as products placed on a market. **This episode re-earns nothing else and teaches an instrument the listener has not met.**

**Opening instance** — *type: an incident.* Tempe, Arizona, the night of **18 March 2018**: a test vehicle operated by a ride-hailing company's automated-driving unit struck and killed **Elaine Herzberg** as she pushed a bicycle across a road. `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: the National Transportation Safety Board's report and findings — the successive reclassifications of the pedestrian by the perception system and the effect of each on the track history, the disabling of the vehicle manufacturer's emergency braking, the role assigned to the safety operator, and the report's number and publication date]`

*The move: a machine sees a woman three times in six seconds and starts counting again each time.*

**The disclosure the writer must make, one sentence, immediately after the facts.** The United States, 2018, and no European product-liability instrument applied — the revised Directive reaches only products placed on the market after **9 December 2026**. Everything after this point in the episode is a counterfactual run under a statute that has not yet applied to anything, and the episode says so once, plainly, and does not apologise for it again.

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **product** (4(1) ✓) — wrong: "a physical object". Right: all movables, "**it includes electricity, digital manufacturing files, raw materials and software**". The wrong reading is the one the law spent forty years having.
- **related service** (4(3) ✓) — wrong: "customer support". Right: a digital service integrated or inter-connected such that **its absence would prevent the product performing a function**. The cloud service a device cannot work without.
- **component** (4(4) ✓) — wrong: "a part". Right: any item, **tangible or intangible**, including a related service, integrated or inter-connected.
- **manufacturer's control** (4(5) ✓) — wrong: "what they own". Right: whether they perform, authorise or consent to integration or modification — **or have the ability to supply software updates**. This is the hinge that keeps a software maker liable long after shipping.
- **substantial modification** (4(18) ✓) — wrong: "a big update". Right: a defined two-limb test where product-safety rules set no threshold — a change to original performance, purpose or type not foreseen in the initial risk assessment, **and** a change to the nature of the hazard. And it makes the modifier a manufacturer (8(2) ✓).
- **defective** (7(1) ✓) — wrong: "it broke", "it malfunctioned". Right: it "does not provide the safety **a person is entitled to expect**". A statistical system can be working exactly as designed and still be defective.
- **damage** (6(1) ✓) — wrong: "loss". Right: a **closed list** — death or personal injury including medically recognised psychological harm; damage to property with exclusions; and destruction or corruption of data **not used for professional purposes**.
- **the exclusions** (6(1)(b)(i)–(iii) ✓) — the defective product itself; a product damaged by a component the manufacturer integrated; and **property used exclusively for professional purposes**. Wrong reading: "the Directive covers business losses". It covers none.
- **placing on the market** against **putting into service** (4(8), 4(9) ✓) — wrong: the same thing. Right: first making available, against first use in the course of a commercial activity where it was never made available.
- **economic operator** (4(15) ✓) — wrong: "the manufacturer". Right: an umbrella term covering six roles, and the liability articles use it deliberately.
- **development risk defence** (11(1)(e) ✓) — wrong: "we didn't know". Right: **the objective state of scientific and technical knowledge** at the relevant time was not such that the defectiveness could be discovered — and Member States may derogate from it ▲ *(the heading of PLD Art. 18, retrieved 16 September 2026)*.
- **the software carve-out** (11(2) ✓) — wrong: "the defect arose later, so we're out". Right: the later-defect escape is **closed** where the defectiveness is due to a related service, to software including updates, to a **lack** of updates necessary to maintain safety, or to a substantial modification — provided it was within the manufacturer's control.

**The cast, and what this instrument calls each.** The manufacturer (4(10) ✓ — including whoever puts their name on it, and whoever makes it for their own use); the component manufacturer; the related-service provider; the importer (4(12) ✓), authorised representative (4(11) ✓) and fulfilment service provider (4(13) ✓), who catch a non-EU manufacturer; the distributor (4(14) ✓), liable only on a failure to identify somebody upstream within one month (8(3) ✓); the online platform (8(4) ✓); and the **injured person, who need not have bought anything and need not prove fault**. Set that against Series A's provider and deployer in one sentence: **this instrument does not care who deployed it; it cares who made it and who changed it.**

**The system, described operationally.** Perception, tracking, prediction, planning, actuation, running as a loop many times a second; an object detected, classified, assigned a track, and the track carrying the history that makes braking possible; reclassification discarding that history; and an operational design domain outside which the system is not expected to work. The safety operator was the **designed mitigation** for exactly this failure, which is why the human's role is a design question and not a staffing one.

**Does the system exist as described?** Yes — and the report is an investigator's account, not the operator's. **The operator's own characterisation of what its system did is evidence about the operator.**

**The operative text.**
> **Art. 4(1) ✓** — "'product' means all movables, even if integrated into, or inter-connected with, another movable or an immovable; **it includes electricity, digital manufacturing files, raw materials and software**."
> **Art. 7(1) ✓** — "A product shall be considered defective where it does not provide the safety that a person is entitled to expect or that is required under Union or national law."
> **Art. 7(2)(c) ✓** — in assessing defectiveness, "the effect on the product of **any ability to continue to learn or acquire new features** after it is placed on the market or put into service."
> **Art. 6(1) ✓, Art. 8(1), (2) ✓, Art. 11(1)(e), 11(2) ✓.**

Called thereafter: **the defect test**, **the damage list**, and **the update trap** (11(2)).

**Why this rule and not another.** Strict liability exists because an injured person cannot investigate a manufacturing process; fault would be unprovable, so the law dropped fault and kept causation. Extending "product" to software closes the gap the 1985 Directive left open — whether code was a movable at all — and the update provisions answer a specific failure: a product that was safe when sold and unsafe after a patch. What was traded, and it is the fact the series turns on: **the damage list stayed narrow.** Pure economic loss is not damage under this Directive. The refused loan of Episode 4, a lost job, a wrongly cancelled benefit — **none of it is compensable here**, however defective the system that caused it.

**The variants.**
1. The model is updated over the air after sale and the update introduces the defect. **11(2)(b)** keeps the maker in, and the "it wasn't defective when it left us" defence fails.
2. The buyer fine-tunes it and redeploys. **8(2)** makes the buyer a manufacturer of the whole product.
3. The manufacturer stops issuing security updates and a known vulnerability is exploited. **11(2)(c)** — the absence of an update is itself a route to liability.
4. The harm is a wrong credit decision. **Article 6 gives nothing**, and the claimant is back in national law.
5. The software is free and open source, supplied outside a commercial activity. Outside the Directive `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: the FOSS exclusion and where it sits in the text]`.

**The levels.** *Technical* — an unstable classifier and a discarded track history. *Organisational* — a braking function switched off to reduce false positives, with a human designated to compensate. *Institutional* — a safety investigator that publishes, in a country with no product-liability directive. *Economic* — the cost of false positives is felt by the operator every day; the cost of a false negative was felt once. *Rights-based* — a pedestrian who was not a user, not a customer, and not consulted.

**The contested joints.** Whether a statistical error rate can be a "defect" at all — *contested, and unresolved by the text*. What a person is "entitled to expect" of a system that is right 99.4% of the time — *contested, and it is a normative question wearing a factual one's clothes*. Whether the development-risk defence swallows machine learning — *contested, and Member States may derogate from it, which means the answer will differ by country*. Whether a model is a product or a component — *still moving*.

**What the rule assumes about the machine.** That a product has a **state** at the moment it leaves the maker, against which defectiveness is judged (7(2)(e) ✓), and that safety is something a person can be **entitled to expect**. Article 7(2)(c) concedes the first point in its own words — it names the ability to keep learning as a circumstance — and then asks a court to judge the thing anyway. **The claim that must land: the Directive knows the machine moves, says so in the text, and has no method for judging a moving thing except to ask what a reasonable person expected of it.**

**As written against as enforced.** Nothing has been decided under this Directive and nothing can be until products placed on the market after **9 December 2026** start failing. Transposition was due on the same date, and by mid-2026 only a handful of Member States had completed it `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: the transposition count and its date]`. **This is where the "nobody has run this yet" beat is spent, in full, for the whole series.**

**Handover.** Strict liability removes the need to prove fault. It does not remove the need to prove that the thing was defective and that the defect caused the harm — and for a system nobody can explain, that is the whole fight.

**Transfer questions.** 1. Is this thing a product, a component, or a related service — and who would say so? 2. Who could still supply an update to it, and does that keep them inside? 3. What damage would this system's failure actually cause, and is that damage on the list? 4. If someone modified it, did they become its manufacturer? 5. What would a person be entitled to expect of this, and who decided that?

**Sources.** Directive (EU) 2024/2853, Arts. 4, 6, 7, 8, 11, and the derogation at Art. 18, EUR-Lex CELEX 32024L2853. Plus, once held: the NTSB report; the transposition state.

## Episode 9 — Who Has to Prove It

**Length:** 36 min.

**Prerequisites.** Episode 8 — product, defect, damage, economic operator. Episode 6 — justification against explanation, which this episode cashes in.

**Opening instance** — *type: a decided case.* `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: the Horizon litigation — the Post Office's prosecutions of sub-postmasters on accounting-system evidence between 1999 and 2015, the number of prosecutions, the English evidential presumption that a computer was operating correctly unless the contrary is shown and the authority for it, and the findings of the High Court in the Horizon Issues judgment of 2019 with its citation and the judge's name]`

*The move: a court that begins by assuming the computer was right, and makes the people it was wrong about prove otherwise — without letting them see it.*

**The disclosure, one sentence, before the facts rather than after:** England, not Union law, and criminal and contract proceedings rather than product liability. **The point being borrowed is the evidential rule, not the instrument** — and it is borrowed because it states, more clearly than any European document, what the revised Directive was written to reverse.

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **burden of proof** (10(1) ✓) — wrong: "convincing the judge". Right: **three separate things** the claimant must prove — the defectiveness, the damage, and the causal link between them. Each can fail on its own.
- **presumption** — wrong: "an assumption the court makes". Right: a **rebuttable** shift: the fact is taken as established unless the other side displaces it (10(5) ✓).
- **disclosure of evidence** (9(1) ✓) — wrong: "American-style discovery". Right: available to a claimant who has first "presented facts and evidence sufficient to support the **plausibility** of the claim", and limited to what is necessary and proportionate (9(3) ✓).
- **trade secret** (4(17) ✓) — wrong: "a reason the defendant can refuse". Right: a reason for the **court** to take specific measures preserving confidentiality while the evidence is used (9(5) ✓). The secret is protected; the claimant is not shut out.
- **presented in an easily accessible and easily understandable manner** (9(6) ✓) — wrong: a formality. Right: a court may require the disclosing party to make the material intelligible — the nearest thing in either instrument to a duty to explain.
- **failure to disclose** (10(2)(a) ✓) — wrong: a procedural slap. Right: **the defectiveness is presumed.** This is the sanction that makes the disclosure article real.
- **non-compliance with mandatory product safety requirements** (10(2)(b) ✓) — wrong: a general reference to standards. Right: a demonstrated breach of Union or national safety requirements intended to protect against the risk of that damage **presumes the defect**. This is where the AI Act's requirements enter a damages claim.
- **obvious malfunction… during reasonably foreseeable use** (10(2)(c) ✓).
- **the causation presumption** (10(3) ✓) — where the product is defective and the damage is "of a kind typically consistent with the defect in question".
- **excessive difficulties… due to technical or scientific complexity** (10(4) ✓) — the AI clause, and the most important sentence in this episode: where the claimant faces excessive difficulty **and** demonstrates that it is *likely* that the product is defective or that causation exists, the court **shall** presume it.

**The cast.** The claimant, who cannot see the system; the defendant, who can; the national court, which decides what is proportionate; the expert, who is the most expensive person in the room; and the regulator, who is not a party.

**The system, described operationally.** What evidence about a deployed model actually exists: version history, training-run metadata, evaluation results, input and output logs, and incident records. What does not exist: a record of *why* an output occurred. **The distinction the episode must land: logs establish what happened, under which version, on which input — not the reason.** That is why the law's answer to unexplainability is procedural rather than explanatory.

**The operative text.**
> **Art. 9(1) ✓** — disclosure on presenting facts and evidence "sufficient to support the **plausibility** of the claim for compensation".
> **Art. 10(1) ✓** — "a claimant is required to prove the defectiveness of the product, the damage suffered and the causal link between that defectiveness and that damage."
> **Art. 10(2)(a), (b) ✓; Art. 10(3) ✓; Art. 10(4)(a), (b) ✓; Art. 10(5) ✓.**

Called thereafter: **the disclosure rule** and **the presumptions**.

**Why this rule and not another.** The road not taken was to require explainability by law — to say that a system whose decisions cannot be explained may not be placed on the market. Europe did not do that. It left the black box intact and **changed who loses when it stays closed**. What was traded: the claimant still needs enough to reach plausibility before disclosure opens, and a person who cannot afford an expert never reaches it.

**The turn, mid-episode.** The instrument that was to do this for **fault-based** claims — the proposed AI Liability Directive — was listed for withdrawal in the Commission's 2025 work programme and formally withdrawn. Nothing specific replaced it. What fills the gap is this Directive's presumptions, twenty-seven national fault regimes, and the data-protection Regulation's own compensation route. `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: the proposal and its date; the work-programme entry and its date; the formal withdrawal notice]` `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: GDPR Art. 82 in its own words — **if the docket does not supply it, this beat is cut rather than reconstructed**, and the episode says only that the Regulation has a compensation route]`

**The collision, and it is the good news of the series.** The documentation, logging and record-keeping duties in the product statute — Series A's material, named in a clause — **create the evidence that this Directive's disclosure rule can compel and whose absence it punishes**. This is the one place where the layers were designed to interlock and appear to do so. **Plant it carefully: Episode 10 needs it as the strongest argument against its own verdict.**

**The variants.**
1. The defendant discloses everything, and the claimant still cannot show a defect. **10(4)** may presume it anyway, on technical complexity plus likelihood.
2. The system was fully compliant with the product statute. No 10(2)(b) presumption — and the defendant reaches for the exemption where defectiveness is due to compliance with legal requirements (11(1)(d) ✓).
3. The loss is purely economic. Episode 8's gap opens and the claimant leaves this instrument entirely.
4. The defendant is outside the Union with no importer and no representative. The chain in Article 8 runs out, and Article 8(5) ✓ offers Member States the option of a national compensation scheme — an admission, in the text, that somebody will not be paid.

**The levels.** *Technical* — logs that record what, not why. *Organisational* — retention policies that decide, years in advance, what a court will be able to see. *Institutional* — a court deciding proportionality about a system it cannot evaluate. *Economic* — the cost of an expert, which is the real threshold. *Rights-based* — equality of arms, which is the principle this whole article is an application of.

**The contested joints.** Whether the complexity presumption will be used, or read narrowly by national courts — *still moving, and it will differ by Member State*. Whether disclosure can reach training data — *contested*. Whether withdrawing the fault-based directive left a gap or removed a duplication — *contested, and both sides are stated: the Commission's simplification case and the critics' access-to-justice case*.

**What the rule assumes about the machine.** That there **is** evidence: that somewhere a record exists which, once produced, lets a court decide. For a learned system the record may establish everything except the thing in dispute. **The claim that must land: the law gave up on making the machine explain itself, and priced silence instead — disclose, or be presumed defective.**

**As written against as enforced.** No damages award on these facts exists in the Union, because the Directive does not yet apply to any product. **Say it once, as a date, and move on — Episode 8 already spent the full version of this beat.**

**Handover.** We have now laid every layer this series set out to lay: an instrument about data, an instrument about products, and an instrument about injury. One question has been held since the first episode, and it is the only one left. **Episode 10.**

**Transfer questions.** 1. Who has the evidence here, and what happens to them if they do not produce it? 2. What would this claimant have to show before a court would even order disclosure? 3. Does a breach of the safety rules here presume the defect — and which rules count? 4. If the system is too complex for anyone to prove causation, who bears that? 5. What in this system is logged, for how long, and who decided?

**Sources.** Directive (EU) 2024/2853, Arts. 9 and 10, and Art. 4(17), EUR-Lex CELEX 32024L2853. Plus, once held: the Horizon judgment; the AI Liability Directive proposal and its withdrawal; GDPR Art. 82.

## Episode 10 — [VERDICT] Layers

**Length:** 30 min. Deliberately shorter than its neighbours: a verdict episode is not a recapitulation, it teaches no new apparatus, and this one has one argument, three facts and a date to land.

**Prerequisites.** All nine.

**Opening instance** — *type: a live controversy, and every fact in it carries a date.* The **Digital Omnibus**: a package that reopens the instruments this series has just taught — an express basis for AI development, a new door in the special-categories prohibition, and adjustments across the data acquis — with its AI half adopted and its data half still in negotiation at the moment of recording, and the two European data-protection institutions objecting in a joint opinion. `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: the package's components, the adoption of the AI half by Council on 29 June 2026, the negotiating status of the data half in the week of writing, and EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 2/2026 and its specific objection]`

*The move: the layers being renegotiated while this was being recorded — and the narrator says so, with the date, because the listener may hear it after the answer has changed.*

**The question, stated as it was held.** Since Episode 1 this series has said it would not answer this until now. **Does layered protection actually protect — or does it multiply duties while leaving the same gap?**

**The competing answers, and who holds them.**
1. **Yes — the layers protect, and the record shows it.** The data-protection instrument reached general-purpose models years before the product statute's obligations bit, and it did so through a *legal basis* finding rather than a safety one; the Court reached past the decider to the scorer; and the liability Directive's presumption is triggered by breach of the product statute's own requirements. Held in substance by the Commission's complementarity framing and by the European Data Protection Board. *Evidence planted:* Episode 2 (the older instrument arriving first), Episode 4 (the role allocation the product statute could not have made), Episode 9 (the interlock, and it is real).
2. **No — the layers multiply duties and leave the same gap.** The duties fall on the organisations already trying to comply; the person harmed still gets a procedure rather than an outcome. *Evidence planted:* Episode 4 (a Grand Chamber victory and no loan), Episode 5 (an access right answered with almost nothing until a court intervened), Episode 7 (two assessments, neither addressed to the person), Episode 8 (the damage list).
3. **The layers are being unbuilt faster than they were built.** The fault-based liability instrument was withdrawn; the data-protection instrument is being amended to accommodate model training; the product statute's own dates moved once already. *Evidence planted:* Episode 3 (the proposed new door), Episode 9 (the withdrawal), and Series A's amending regulation, named in a clause.
4. **The frame is misposed: these are not layers, they are different addressees.** One instrument is about a person's data, one about a market in products, one about an injury. They were never designed to sum, and "layered protection" is a description nobody drafting them would have recognised. *Evidence planted:* Episode 1 (two role vocabularies for one company), Episode 7 (one English word, "risk", meaning two different things), Episode 8 (a damage list written for exploding bottles).

**THE VERDICT — named, and narrow on purpose.**

**On the record available in September 2026, the layers do add protection — and almost all of it accrues to regulators and to claimants who already have a case, while almost none reaches the person the machine decided about.** Answer 4 is the clearest way to *see* why: the instruments have different addressees, so their protections stack for whoever can litigate or investigate, and leave a hole exactly where a private person stands. Answer 2 is what that costs.

**Three facts decide it, each established earlier in the series and each named again here.**
1. **The instrument that reached a general-purpose model first was the data-protection instrument, not the product statute** — and it did so on a legal-basis question, which is the layering argument's best evidence and is conceded without hedging (Episode 2).
2. **The only new route to money for an individual is the liability Directive's, and its damage list excludes the harms these systems actually cause.** A refused loan is not damage under it. The man who won in Luxembourg recovers nothing under the instrument built to pay people (Episodes 4 and 8).
3. **The instrument drafted to close that exact gap was withdrawn**, and the data-protection instrument is being amended in the direction of the systems rather than the subjects (Episodes 9 and 3, and the opening of this episode).

**State it as the narrator's own position, earned — and say plainly what would overturn it.** A completed transposition producing an award to an individual harmed by an AI system; or a final Omnibus text that preserves the special-categories prohibition and adds a route to compensation rather than only a basis for processing. **If either happens, the second fact stops being true and this verdict should be revisited on air.**

**The writer may disagree, and is told how.** If, on the material in front of it, the writer reaches a different answer — most plausibly answer 1, on the strength of the Episode 9 interlock — **it should say so in the narrator's voice, name this plan's verdict as the one it is departing from, and give its reasons.** What it must not do is leave the question open: eleven episodes and an explicit promise in Episode 1 were aimed at an answer.

**The order of the close — a sequence, not a choice.** First the verdict, named and owned. Then **the date, as the last word**: the instruments in this series carry their own scheduled moments for asking whether they worked — an evaluation of the liability Directive five years out, a package still in negotiation, a product statute whose duties phase in. The verdict is the argument's landing; the date is the reminder that the listener can check.

**What the writer must also say out loud:** that the series held this question deliberately, since Episode 1, and that it held it so the listener would have the machinery before the argument.

**Load-bearing terms** — only two, and both are new here. **"Layered protection"** — wrong: more laws means more protection; right: a claim about how instruments *interact*, which can be tested and which is the only thing this episode is about. **"A gap"** — wrong: something the legislature forgot; right: on this record, something the legislature saw, proposed to fill, and then decided not to.

**Deliberately compressed:** no new apparatus is taught. Nothing from Episodes 1–9 is re-explained; every fact is named in the words the earlier episode gave it.

**Transfer questions — the series' closing set.** 1. When two instruments reach the same system, which one actually bites, and on whom? 2. Does the person harmed get anything, or only the regulator and the litigant? 3. What kind of harm does this instrument recognise as harm at all? 4. When a duty gets heavier and a remedy does not appear, who benefited? 5. What single checkable fact would tell me in a year whether the layers are adding up?

**Sources.** Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and Directive (EU) 2024/2853, as taught across the series. Plus, once held: the Digital Omnibus components and status; EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 2/2026.

**Writability: ONE RETRIEVAL AWAY, once Episodes 8 and 9 are sourced.** Its own outside item is the Omnibus status, and **that is its single fact-check — and it moves.** If the data half has been adopted by the time of writing, the opening changes and the third deciding fact must be restated in its final form rather than as a proposal. **Say so to the producer rather than writing around it.**

## The verdict episode

**Episode 10, "Layers."** It lands the series' only normative verdict. The question is named in Episode 1 and explicitly held; Episodes 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8 and 9 each plant a piece of its evidence.

**The verdict named:** on the record available in September 2026, layering does add protection, but it accrues to regulators and to claimants who already have a case, and almost none of it reaches the person the system decided about.

**The three deciding facts:** the data-protection instrument reached a general-purpose model first, on a legal-basis question; the liability Directive's damage list excludes the economic harms these systems actually cause, so the man who won in Luxembourg recovers nothing under it; and the instrument written to close that gap was withdrawn while the data-protection instrument is being amended toward the systems.

**Where the evidence is planted:** Ep 2 (the older instrument arrives first), Ep 3 (a new door proposed in a prohibition), Ep 4 (a win with no remedy), Ep 5 (an access right answered with nothing), Ep 7 (two assessments, neither addressed to the person), Ep 8 (the damage list), Ep 9 (the interlock that works, and the withdrawal).

**What is not the verdict.** Per-episode judgments — that purpose limitation and general-purpose models are in genuine contradiction; that an input-based prohibition cannot reach an inference; that Article 22 is satisfied by adding a human and says nothing about whether the decision was right — are made where they arise. The **general** question of whether Europe should legislate by layered instruments at all is explicitly left open, with the two named events that would settle it.

## What does not fit, and what I propose

**The brief's third block — the DSA, the DMA, the Data Act, the Data Governance Act, copyright and text-and-data-mining — is not in these ten episodes.** Two reasons, and the producer should weigh both.

**The count.** The roadmap gives Series B eight to ten episodes. The GDPR interface alone is five apparatus episodes without padding, and the liability half is two. Adding five instruments would mean either one episode covering all five — which is the cramming the brief forbids — or a fourteen-episode series.

**The docket.** `research/02` states it plainly: those episodes have **no instance at all** and none of the five instruments' operative text is held. Reserving them costs nothing today, because they could not be written today.

**Reserved to: a later series in this production — working title "The Rest of the Acquis".** Named aloud exactly once, in Episode 2, as permission to forget. **It is a promise: no episode of this series covers any of that material silently.**

**Option B, which is the better series if the producer will pay for the docket: twelve episodes, adding two before the verdict.** Both are sketched here without episode headings so that nothing downstream mistakes them for plans.

**Sketch — "The Platform's Own Risk" (the DSA, and the AI Act's seam with it).** Opening candidate: the Commission's extension of its proceedings against a very large platform to cover a generative assistant deployed inside it, under the systemic-risk and mitigation duties `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: Commission press release IP/26/203, 26 January 2026, and the scope of the proceedings; plus the December 2025 non-compliance decision against the same platform and its amount]`. The collision is exact and documented: a recommender system is governed by one instrument as a systemic risk, and may be governed by another as a product — and the two regimes' advocates do not agree which applies. **Needs:** the DSA's operative text for its risk-assessment, mitigation, recommender-transparency, auditing and researcher-access provisions, fetched with `consolidate.py`, and one instance file.

**Sketch — "Reading Is Not Copying" (copyright and text-and-data-mining).** Two decided European cases exist and they point different ways: a German appellate court holding that a dataset builder could rely on the mining exception because a reservation was not machine-readable, and a German first-instance court holding that memorisation and regurgitation of song lyrics are reproductions outside the mining exception `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: Hanseatisches OLG Hamburg, 5 U 104/24, 10 December 2025, with the further appeal permitted; LG München I, 42 O 14139/24, 11 November 2025]`. **This is the best-documented collision in the whole reserved block** and it would make a strong episode as soon as the directive's text and the two judgments are in a docket file.

**The DMA, the Data Act and the Data Governance Act** are the weakest of the five on instances, and honestly so: the AI question under the DMA was still a review question as of the 2026 review, and the Data Act became applicable in September 2025 with no documented AI collision. **If only one extra slot exists, take the DSA. If two, take the DSA and copyright.**

**One conditional trade the producer can make instead.** If docket request 2 comes in rich — the 2025 judgment on meaningful information in full — then the lens at Episode 6 loses its admission test, because that judgment would carry an apparatus episode after all. **Fold the lens into Episode 5, and the freed slot takes the DSA.** That is a genuinely better series and it depends on one retrieval.
