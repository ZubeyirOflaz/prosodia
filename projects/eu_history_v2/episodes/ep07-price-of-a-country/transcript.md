## Two things that cannot both be true {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "open cold on the bald paradox; state it plainly, no scene-setting first"}
Here are two things about the summer of 1990, and they cannot both be true. {pause: 1.0}

The first: a nation is about to become whole again. After forty-five years split down the middle by barbed wire and concrete and the guns of the Cold War, Germany is going to be one country. A people torn in half is going to be sewn back together. By any decent measure, that is a happy ending. {pause: 0.6}

The second: this is the single most *frightening* thing that has happened to Europe since the war. {pause: 0.8} Because a whole Germany — eighty million people, the largest economy on the continent, sitting square in the middle of it — is exactly the thing this entire story has been built, treaty by treaty, to prevent. It is the nightmare under the whole project. It is the Ruhr fear from forty years ago come back, grown now to the size of a country.

A people reunited, and the continent's oldest terror walking back through the door. {pause: 1.2} The same event. At the same moment. Both of them true.

And the men who have to hold those two truths in their hands at once are standing, of all places, outdoors. In the mountains. Under an open sky. {pause: 0.8} So let me take you there.

## The dacha in the Caucasus {tone: warm, rate: slow, note: "settle into the scene; two men outside deciding a country's fate; ease off the dread here"}
July 1990. Not Moscow. Not the Kremlin, with its long tables and its rows of grey officials. A dacha — a country house — in the Caucasus mountains, near a town called Stavropol. The home ground of the man who happens to hold {pause: 0.8} the fate of Germany in his pocket: Mikhail Gorbachev, the last leader of the Soviet Union.

And Gorbachev has done something unusual. He has invited the German Chancellor not to the seat of Soviet power but to his own home country, into the hills, and he has taken part of the negotiation *outside.* In the open air. Two men walking and sitting under the mountain sky, deciding whether a country can exist. {pause: 0.8}

The German is Helmut Kohl. And you should see him, because the physical fact of the man matters. Six foot four. Enormous. His enemies caricatured him as a pear — der Birne — this vast, soft, provincial shape of a man from the wine country of the Rhineland-Palatinate, whom the clever people of Bonn and Paris and London had underestimated for his entire career. They thought he was slow. They mistook patience for stupidity, and bulk for a dull mind. {pause: 0.8} They were wrong, and this is the week they find out how wrong.

Because sitting in those hills, Kohl grasps something with perfect clarity. The prize is right there in front of him — his country, reunited at last. The Soviet Union, cracking and broke and desperate for hard cash, is prepared to let it happen. But there is a price. {pause: 0.6} There is always a price. And Kohl is a man who has come to *pay* it.

## What a country costs {tone: measured, rate: slow}
So watch the deal get made, out there in the open air, because it is one of the great bargains of the century and almost nobody remembers the terms.

Gorbachev needs two things. He needs the reunified Germany's military not to be a threat — not to be the giant war machine the Soviet Union has feared across the plains of Europe for two generations. And he needs, bluntly, money.

Kohl gives him both. He agrees to cap the army of a united Germany at three hundred and seventy thousand men — to build in, from the very first day, a limit on his own country's strength. And he pledges billions of Deutsche Marks to pay for something specific and enormous: to move the Soviet troops home. There are hundreds of thousands of Soviet soldiers sitting on German soil, in the eastern half, and someone has to house them and feed them and physically relocate them back east across a crumbling empire. {pause: 0.6} Kohl agrees that Germany will pay for it. Germany will buy the withdrawal of the army that occupied it.

And that is the shape of the thing. In the open air of the Caucasus, the Pear from the Rhineland-Palatinate — the man they all thought was slow — {pause: 0.8} *buys back his own country.* With a capped army and a river of German marks, he purchases from a dying superpower the right for Germany to be one nation again.

He walks away from those mountains having done what no German leader had managed in half a century. {pause: 1.0} And now he has to sell it to the rest of Europe. Which is a very different negotiation, with a much older kind of fear on the other side of the table.

## The man who remembered {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "introduce Mitterrand through memory, not biography; the fear is personal and old"}
Turn now to Paris, and to an old man who is watching all of this with a dread that runs deeper than politics.

François Mitterrand. President of France. Seventy-three years old in that summer, which means he was born in 1916 — the year of Verdun, the year we began this whole story, the year the two nations fed a generation of their young men into the mud above the Meuse. Mitterrand is not reading about the German danger in a briefing paper. He *lived* it. He was a French soldier in 1940. He was captured. He escaped. He is a man in whose own body {pause: 0.8} the memory of German armies pouring across the border still lives.

And so when the Wall comes down, and reunification suddenly looks not just possible but *fast*, Mitterrand feels something the younger men around him do not quite feel. He feels the ground of 1913 opening back up. He is reported to have worried, in almost those words, about a Germany once again dominant — dominant "as in 1913," on the eve of the catastrophe — a Germany so large and so strong at the center of Europe that it distorts everything around it simply by existing. Not because Germans are wicked. Kohl is not wicked; Kohl is a good European, maybe the best of them. But because *size* is its own kind of threat, and a united Germany would be too big.

This is the founders' original fear, the one from the very first episodes, worn on the face of a man who was a boy when the guns of Verdun were still firing. {pause: 0.8} The furnace of the Ruhr. Three invasions in one lifetime. All of it, awake again, in the eyes of the President of France.

## The unilateral man {tone: tense, rate: slow, note: "Kohl frightens Paris; the ten points as a shock"}
And Kohl — the good European, the patient man — has already done the one thing guaranteed to make that fear boil over.

Back up to the autumn before, to November 1989, days after the Wall is breached. The world is still reeling, nobody knows what comes next, and everything is fragile and provisional and terrifyingly open. And on the 28th of November, Helmut Kohl stands up in the German parliament and announces a ten-point plan for German reunification. {pause: 0.8}

He does it *without consulting his allies.* Without warning Paris. Without warning London. Without warning, in any real sense, Washington or Moscow. The Chancellor of West Germany lays out a road map to a united Germany — the most destabilizing possibility on the continent — and the other Western capitals learn about it essentially from the news. {pause: 0.8}

Stand in Mitterrand's shoes for one second and feel what that does. Here is the exact thing France has feared for a hundred years — a Germany acting on its own, on the biggest question there is, answering to nobody — and it has just happened. In public. Without a phone call. {tone: grave} To an old man who remembers 1940, that is not a policy announcement. That is the sound of the leash {pause: 0.8} coming off. {pause: 1.0}

So the question that lands on Mitterrand's desk in the winter of 1989 is the same question that has haunted every chapter of this series, only now it is urgent and real and moving fast: what do you do about a Germany you cannot stop from growing? {pause: 0.8} And the answer he reaches for is the oldest answer in the book.

## You get your country, we get your currency {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "the central bargain; lay it out cleanly — this is the spine of the hour"}
You bind it. {pause: 0.8}

That is the founders' method, and it goes all the way back to the beginning. You cannot make Germany smaller. You cannot un-unify it without a war nobody wants. So you do the only other thing that has ever worked: you tie the giant so tightly into the European structure, you weave it so deeply into the machinery of the continent, that it *cannot* dominate — because its hands are bound to everyone else's. You solve the problem of German power the way Monnet solved the problem of the Ruhr, forty years earlier: not by taking the coal and steel away, but by locking them into something shared, so that they can never again be turned into a weapon aimed outward.

And in 1990, the thing you use to bind Germany is not coal. It is not steel. It is *money.* {pause: 1.0}

Here is the reported bargain, the one at the very heart of this episode, and it is beautifully simple and enormously consequential. Mitterrand's logic — as it has come down to us — runs like this: France will accept German reunification. France will not stand in the way of the giant becoming whole. In exchange, Germany gives up the one thing that is the very symbol of its power and its discipline and its recovery: the Deutsche Mark. Germany surrenders its beloved, rock-solid national currency and pours it into a single European money that Germany will have to share with everyone else — including with weaker, looser, more spendthrift neighbors it does not entirely trust. {pause: 0.6}

You get your country back. We get your currency. {pause: 1.2}

It is the Ruhr bargain of 1950 scaled up to an entire monetary system. Take the thing that makes you dangerous, and lock it in a room with everyone else's, and throw away the key. And if it is true — if that is really the deal that was struck — then the single European currency, the euro that three hundred million people carry in their pockets today, was born as {pause: 0.8} the price France charged Germany for the right to be one country. {pause: 0.8} A currency created not primarily to make Europeans richer, but to make Germans *safe to live next to.*

## Was it ever actually said? {tone: matter-of-fact, rate: normal, note: "the first contested point; voice both readings as a fair, honest weighing; resolve neither"}
Except — and here I have to slow down and be honest with you, because the honest version is the more interesting one — we do not entirely know that it happened like that. {pause: 0.8}

This is one of the two great unresolved arguments of the episode, and serious people fall on both sides of it. Was reunification *explicitly* traded for the currency — a deal, a transaction, this-for-that, said out loud behind a closed door? Or was it only an *implicit* understanding, a thing everybody grasped and nobody quite spelled out, the way people in a family understand a bargain without ever writing it down?

There is real evidence for the strong version. There are declassified minutes — of conversations involving Mitterrand and the German foreign minister, Hans-Dietrich Genscher — that surfaced years later and read very much like a conditioning of one thing on the other. Mitterrand's own close aide, Hubert Védrine, testified to the linkage. And Karl Otto Pöhl, the head of Germany's central bank, affirmed that the connection between reunification and monetary union was structural and real. That is a heavy body of testimony pointing one direction.

And then, flatly on the other side, stands Wolfgang Schäuble — one of the German architects of reunification, a man who was *in the room* for the making of modern Germany — who denied there was ever any closed-door deal. No transaction. No trade. The currency and the country, he insisted, were separate roads that merely ran alongside each other.

{tone: measured} So the historians split. Some say informal understanding; some say explicit bargain. And I am not going to hand you a false certainty, because the truth is the record genuinely points both ways. {pause: 0.8} But hold on to this much, because it survives the argument intact: whether it was spoken aloud or only understood without ever being said, everybody involved knew that the fate of the currency and the fate of the country were braided together. The giant was going to be bound. The only question the historians still fight about is whether anyone was crude enough to *say so.*

## The treaty that made a Union {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "Maastricht arrives; keep it human — a small Dutch city carrying an enormous weight"}
Whatever was said or unsaid in those rooms, the binding had to be written down. And it was written down in a small Dutch city on a river, a place most of the world had never heard of and would never forget.

Maastricht. In the far south of the Netherlands, where the country narrows almost to a thread between Belgium and Germany. In February 1992, the leaders of the European Community sign a treaty there, and it is not a modest one. It is the most ambitious single act of integration since the founding.

The Treaty of Maastricht does two vast things at once. First, it lays down the road to the single currency — the timetable, the rules, the criteria a country must meet to join. The binding of Germany, turned into law and schedule. And second, it renames and refounds the whole enterprise. Up to this moment they had been the European *Community* — a common market, an economic thing, at least on paper. Maastricht christens something larger and stranger and far more political. {pause: 0.8}

It creates the European *Union.* {pause: 1.0}

The very name we use today is born here, in this treaty, in this small city on the Meuse. Not a market. Not a community of trading partners. A *Union.* The word reaches past economics toward something like a shared political destiny — citizenship, foreign policy, a common home. It is the founders' oldest dream, given at last its permanent name.

And the men who signed it were confident. They had built the machine to bind the giant; they had given the whole project a grander title; the future was arranged. All that remained was the formality of ratification — the nations saying yes, one by one, as they always had. {pause: 0.8} Which is precisely the moment the peoples of Europe decided, for the first time in this entire story, to *speak.*

## Fifty thousand across a river {tone: tense, rate: slow, note: "the Danish no; the shock of it — the small country that broke the confidence"}
The first to speak was one of the smallest. {pause: 0.6}

Denmark. On the 2nd of June, 1992 — four months after the grand signing — the Danish people are asked, in a referendum, to approve the Maastricht Treaty. And the assumption in every capital is that they will. Of course they will. The thing is done; the elites of all twelve nations have agreed; the vote is a rubber stamp.

The Danes vote *no.* {pause: 1.2}

Narrowly — fifty point seven percent against — but no is no. A single small nation of five million people, and it has just said, straight to the face of the entire European project, that it does not consent. And under the rules, the treaty needs *all* of them. Every member has to ratify, or the whole edifice does not stand. One "no," from one of the smallest members, and the most ambitious plan in Europe's history is suddenly lying on the floor. {pause: 0.6}

Now watch what the leaders do, because it tells you something about the whole method of this project that nothing else quite does.

They do not accept the answer. {pause: 1.0}

They do not reopen the treaty and renegotiate it among all twelve nations — that would be chaos, that would be surrender. Instead, that December, at a summit in Edinburgh, the leaders devise a special arrangement just for Denmark. The Edinburgh Agreement. It grants the Danes four "opt-outs" — four specific carve-outs, including, crucially, the right to *not* join the single currency. Denmark gets to stay out of the euro and stay in the Union. And with that concession in hand, the Danish government puts the question to its people a *second* time, in May 1993. {pause: 0.6}

This time they vote yes. Fifty-six point eight percent. {pause: 0.8}

## If a country votes wrong, change the question {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "draw the lesson coldly; this is the moral wound of the episode"}
And there it is. There is the lesson the leaders of Europe quietly took away from Denmark, and it is one they would apply again and again in the years to come, right down to the present day.

If a country votes the wrong way {pause: 1.0} — you redesign the *question.* {pause: 1.2}

You do not force it. You do not send in soldiers; this is not that kind of empire. You are gentler and cleverer than that. You take the "no," and you find a concession that lets the government go back to its people and ask again, in a slightly different form, with a slightly better offer, until the answer comes back the way it needs to. An opt-out here. A reassurance there. A second vote. The Danish "no" of June 1992 did not stop the treaty. It merely got the question re-asked until it produced a "yes."

Remember the very last thing I told you at the end of the previous chapter — that for all the treaties and vetoes, the men building Europe had almost never simply *asked* a whole people, in plain words, whether they wanted this. Well. Now, finally, they were asking. The referendums had begun. {pause: 0.6} And then came the answers that were never in the plan — and what the leaders did with them gave the game away. The question, it turns out, is not really "do you consent." The question is "do you consent — and if not, we will ask you again, more nicely, until you do." {pause: 1.2}

Denmark was the small shock. But it was a warning tremor. The real earthquake was about to come from one of the largest members of all — from the very country that had *invented* the bargain.

## Fewer than five hundred thousand {tone: tense, rate: slow, note: "France's near-death vote; Mitterrand's miscalculation; build the tension of election night"}
France. September 1992. {pause: 0.6}

François Mitterrand — the old man who feared a dominant Germany, the architect of the whole binding strategy — decides to put Maastricht to the French people directly, in a referendum. And he does it, in part, out of confidence. This is his treaty. This is his answer to the German problem, the thing he has spent his political capital building. He assumes an easy win. He assumes France will ratify the currency that binds the giant, because France, of all nations, understands *why* it must.

He assumes wrong. {pause: 1.0}

As the campaign wears on, the "yes" that looked so safe begins to shrink. Ordinary French people — farmers, workers, small-town voters, the people who were never in any of those rooms — look at this enormous treaty they were never really consulted about, this currency that will replace the franc, this Union with its grand new name, and a great many of them do not like the smell of it. It feels like something being done *to* them by the clever people in Paris and Brussels, not something they chose. And the campaign becomes a genuine cliff-edge.

Election night. The results come in. {tone: dramatic} And it is agonizingly close. {pause: 0.8}

Fifty point eight percent, yes. Forty-nine point two percent, no. {pause: 1.2}

France ratified the Treaty of Maastricht — the treaty that would remake the continent, the treaty at the heart of the whole European project — by a margin of {pause: 0.8} under five hundred thousand votes. In an electorate of thirty-seven million. {pause: 1.2}

## A whisker {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "the counterfactual; the whole future hanging on a hair"}
Weigh what nearly happened there. {pause: 0.6}

Shift a few hundred thousand French votes — a rounding error in a country of sixty million people, a bad rainstorm on the wrong day, a slightly better "no" campaign — and France rejects Maastricht. And if *France* rejects Maastricht, the treaty is not wounded; it is dead. France is not Denmark. There is no Edinburgh Agreement for France, no little package of opt-outs to smooth it over. France was the co-author. If the co-author's own people vote it down, the single currency does not happen, the European Union does not get its name, the entire binding of a reunified Germany collapses at the starting line. {pause: 0.8}

The whole future of the continent — the euro in three hundred million pockets, the Union we live in now — passed through the eye of that needle {pause: 0.8} by a hair. {pause: 1.0} By fewer than five hundred thousand French men and women, on a single evening in September 1992, who by the narrowest of margins said yes to a thing most of them had barely been asked about.

{tone: grave} And this is the pattern that ought to unsettle you, the one the planner of this episode wants you to feel in your chest. Europe was building the most ambitious top-down structure in its entire history — a shared currency, a political union, the deepest integration ever attempted by free nations — {pause: 0.8} at the *exact* moment its own peoples were signaling, as loudly as they were ever asked to, that they had not really been consulted. The Danes said no. The French very nearly said no. And the leaders, reading those results, drew a single conclusion. {pause: 0.8}

They pressed on anyway. On the theory that the peoples would come around. {pause: 1.0} Would they?

## The coin with the wrong name {tone: wry, rate: normal, note: "a lighter beat to reset intensity; the naming fight is genuinely funny and revealing"}
Let me lift the weight for a moment, because even in the middle of something this grave there is a very human comedy, and it is the comedy of what to *call* the thing. {pause: 0.6}

You would think naming a currency is the easy part. It is not. Because a currency's name is not neutral — it carries a flag, a language, a history — and *this* currency was meant to belong to no single nation. So the name had to belong to no single nation either. And that turned out to be almost impossible.

For a long time the frontrunner was the ECU — the European Currency Unit — which had the tidy advantage of being an acronym that already existed. But there was a problem, and it was a very old one. Because "écu" — spelled the same way — is also the name of a medieval French coin. A gold coin, from the age of French kings. Which meant that to German ears, adopting the ECU sounded suspiciously like the whole of Europe agreeing to carry French money with a fresh coat of paint on it. Un-neutral. Too Gallic. Exactly the national flavor the currency was supposed *not* to have. {pause: 0.6}

There is a lovely irony in that, if you sit with the shape of this whole episode: the Germans, who were giving up the mightiest currency in Europe, refusing to swallow a name that smelled too French. The bargain cut both ways, right down to the letters on the coin.

And the French were no more pleased with the alternative. When the plain, flat, deliberately boring word "euro" was floated, the French President — Jacques Chirac by then — reportedly grumbled that it was clumsy. Maladroit, was the word. Awkward on the tongue. {tone: wry} And he was right, in a way. "Euro" *is* clumsy. It is a stub, a fragment, half a word. But that clumsiness was the entire point. {pause: 0.6}

At a summit in Madrid, in December 1995, they chose it. Euro. Precisely *because* it favored no language and no nation. Because it was nobody's coin, and so it could be everybody's. {pause: 0.8} The most expensive naming committee in history landed on the blandest possible answer, and the blandness was the achievement. A currency for a continent that had spent a thousand years killing over whose flag flew highest had to be called something that flew no flag at all.

## The queues at midnight {tone: warm, rate: slow, note: "the human high point; ordinary people meeting the abstraction in their hands; let it land as wonder"}
And then, on a single night, the abstraction became something you could hold. {pause: 0.6}

New Year, 2002. Across twelve countries, more than three hundred million people go to sleep on the last night of one world and wake up in another. Because at midnight, the national currencies — the franc, the mark, the lira, the guilder, all of them, the coins their grandparents had earned and saved and buried and spent — begin, formally, to die. And the euro, the coin with no flag, the fragment of a word chosen because it belonged to no one, becomes real money in real hands.

And here is the detail that I love most in this whole episode, because it is so ordinary and so enormous at once. On that New Year's night, all across the continent, people skipped the parties. {pause: 0.8} They left the champagne and the fireworks and they went, at midnight, to stand in line at the cash machines. To be among the first human beings on earth to hold the new money. To pull the first euro notes out of the wall and turn them over in their hands and *look* at them — this strange new currency with its bridges and its arches that were deliberately of no real country, drawn so as to favor no nation. Queues at the ATMs, in the cold, on New Year's Eve, so that ordinary people could touch the future before their neighbors did. {pause: 0.8}

And the machine worked. Within days, ninety-six, ninety-seven percent of the cash machines across the euro zone were dispensing the new notes. It was the largest currency changeover in the history of the world — something like fifteen billion new notes and fifty billion new coins, replacing nine billion old notes and a hundred and seven billion old coins, across a dozen nations, all at once, in a matter of weeks.

{tone: reverent} Whatever else you believe about all of this — however uneasy the bargains, however thin the consent — pause on what those queues actually were. This was the abstraction made flesh. For fifty years, "Europe" had been treaties and summits and men in rooms the public never saw. And now it was a coin in a schoolchild's palm. A thing you could buy bread with. The single most tangible proof, in the whole of this story, that the impossible project was real. {pause: 1.0} You could feel it. It was in your *pocket.*

## The rules the founders broke {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "the fatal flaw; lay it out plainly, then show the founders themselves breaking it"}
But now I have to show you the crack running through the foundation of the beautiful thing, because it was there from the first day, and the men who built it knew it was there.

The euro was a currency with no country underneath it. {pause: 1.0}

Think about what a normal currency has behind it. Behind the dollar there is one government — one treasury, one budget, one set of decisions about taxing and spending made in one capital. When one American state is in trouble, money flows to it from the others, automatically, through a single federal government, and nobody even votes on it. That shared pot, that common government, is what holds a currency together when a storm hits.

The euro had none of that. {pause: 0.8} It was a monetary union with no fiscal union. A single currency, but no single budget. No shared treasury. No common government standing behind it to move money from the strong regions to the weak ones when the weather turned. Twelve separate governments, twelve separate budgets, twelve separate sets of political promises — all sharing one currency and one central bank, and nothing above them to hold the whole thing together when it was tested.

So what held it together instead? {pause: 0.8} Rules. Just rules. A set of promises written into the treaty — above all, the promise that no member would let its government deficit run above three percent of its economy in a single year. Three percent. That was the guardrail. That was the discipline that was supposed to keep the loose, spendthrift members from wrecking the money the disciplined Germans had so reluctantly handed over. It was called, in time, the Stability and Growth Pact. And it was the entire safety mechanism of a currency shared by three hundred million people. {pause: 0.8}

Everything depended on the members keeping that promise. And the first to break it were the last two anyone expected.

## When the founders kneecapped their own pact {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "the betrayal; deliver it as a hinge — the founders telling everyone the rules are optional"}
November 2003. {pause: 0.6}

France and Germany — the two founders, the two authors of the whole design, the two largest economies in the euro zone, the very nations that had *insisted* on the discipline in the first place — both blow through the three percent limit. Their deficits run over the line. The guardrail that everyone else was told was sacred, the founders themselves cannot keep.

And now comes the moment that matters. Because there is machinery in the treaty for exactly this — a process to discipline a country that breaks the rule, to press it, to enforce the pact. And when that machinery starts to turn against France and Germany, the two of them use their weight to *stop it.* They vote, in effect, to suspend the enforcement. To hold the rules in abeyance. The founders break the pact, and then they switch off the alarm that was supposed to sound when the pact was broken. {pause: 1.2}

The German finance minister, Wolfgang Schäuble — the same Schäuble who denied there had ever been a closed-door currency bargain — would later concede the plain truth of what this did. The Stability Pact, he admitted, "lost its bite." {pause: 0.6}

Take the view from a smaller government, watching that happen. The message went out to every capital in the euro zone, loud and unmistakable, and it was this: {pause: 0.8} the rules are *negotiable.* {pause: 1.0} If you are big enough, they do not really apply to you. The discipline that holds the whole currency together is not law; it is a suggestion, and the two biggest members have just demonstrated, in public, that a suggestion can be ignored. {pause: 1.0}

A currency with no country underneath it, held together by nothing but rules — {pause: 0.8} and within four years, the founders themselves had taught everyone that the rules could be broken. {pause: 0.8} That is a bet. It is a bet that the *engineered* solidarity — the discipline you cannot enforce, the togetherness nobody quite chose — will somehow hold anyway when the first real storm comes. Remember that bet. Everything in the next chapter turns on whether it pays off.

## The other accusation {tone: matter-of-fact, rate: normal, note: "the second contested point; two things conflated — separate them carefully, resolve neither"}
And while we are on the subject of a currency held together by trust, there is a second unresolved argument I owe you, and it concerns a country that joined the euro a little later than the rest. {pause: 0.6}

Greece. Which adopted the euro in 2001, a couple of years after the first wave. And there is a story you have very likely heard about how Greece got in — that Greece *cheated* its way into the single currency, that it faked its numbers, dressed up its finances to look like they cleared the bar when they did not. It is a story that gets told with great confidence. And it is worth slowing down on, because it is actually *two* different accusations, and people relentlessly jam them together into one, and the two are not the same and do not deserve the same verdict.

The first accusation is that Greece falsified its way *in* — that at the moment of entry, it lied about meeting the criteria. Here the honest answer is far murkier than the confident telling suggests. There is a serious case that Greece was not so much a lone cheat as a country "let in" under rules that the European statistics office itself was tolerating — that the standards were softer and blurrier at the time than we now pretend, and that Greece's entry sits inside a grey zone that plenty of members were operating in. The picture of a clean bar and a single liar leaping it dishonestly does not really survive contact with the record. {pause: 0.6}

The second accusation is different, and much better documented — and it is the one that tends to get smeared over the first. It concerns a financial arrangement, worked out with the American investment bank Goldman Sachs, a currency swap that had the effect of making a chunk of Greek debt look smaller than it truly was. That arrangement is real, and far more thoroughly evidenced than the crude "they lied at the door" charge.

{tone: measured} So keep the two apart, because the difference is the whole point. Did Greece cheat its way *into* the euro? Genuinely contested, and probably less a solo crime than a symptom of rules everyone was bending. Was there a separate, well-documented piece of financial engineering with Goldman Sachs that flattered the books? Yes. {pause: 0.8} The scandal that actually holds up is not the one people usually mean when they say Greece "cheated." And I leave that where the evidence leaves it — split, unfinished, and a warning that the tidy villain is almost never the true one.

## The Big Bang {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "enlargement; the continental scale; a genuine wonder before the cold turn"}
So set aside the cracks for one more beat, because the confidence of these years was not baseless. Something genuinely astonishing was happening at the same time, and it was happening to the east.

For the whole of this story, "Europe" has meant the western half. The eastern half — Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, the Baltic states, all of them — had been sealed away behind the Iron Curtain, on the far side of the very Wall whose fall opened this episode. Half a continent, gone from the map of the free, for two generations. And now, one after another, those nations threw off Communism and turned west and asked to come *in.* To join the club. To bind themselves, freely, to the union their captors had spent forty years teaching them to hate.

And on the 1st of May, 2004, they did. {pause: 1.0}

Ten states joined the European Union on a single day. In one act, the Union stopped being the western half of a divided continent and became something that stretched from the Atlantic almost to the borders of Russia. It went *continental.* The line that Churchill had called the Iron Curtain, the scar that had split Europe since 1945 — the Union simply grew across it, and healed it, and made it, in a stroke, a memory. {pause: 0.6}

{tone: reverent} A continent that had spent the first half of the twentieth century tearing itself apart, and the second half split down the middle by barbed wire, was — without a single shot fired — being made *whole.* {pause: 1.0} It is one of the most remarkable things human beings have ever done to themselves peacefully. And you should let it be remarkable, before we turn cold. Because by the spring of 2004, the European project looked, for the first time, genuinely *unstoppable.*

## The Pear weeps {tone: reverent, rate: slow, note: "the human coda; the giant of the episode, overcome; earn the emotion, don't force it"}
And here, before the confidence curdles, is the man himself once more. Helmut Kohl. {pause: 0.6}

Go back a few years, to December 1997, to the moment the leaders finally agree to open the door east — to begin the enlargement talks that would end in that continental Union of 2004. And there is an account, passed down by Jean-Claude Juncker, of what Helmut Kohl did in that room when the eastern enlargement was agreed. {pause: 0.6}

The enormous man — the Pear, the provincial they had all underestimated, the Chancellor who had bought back his own country in the open air of the Caucasus — {pause: 0.8} wept. Openly. {pause: 1.0}

And you have to understand *why* to feel the weight of it. Because Kohl was not weeping over a trade agreement or a summit communiqué. He was a German of the war generation. He understood, better than almost anyone alive, what the division of Europe had *cost* — the barbed wire, the split families, the tanks facing each other across the German plain, the whole frozen tragedy of a continent cut in two. And he grasped, in that room, that it was ending. That the nations his own country's history had helped wall off were coming home. That the giant which had once terrified the continent was now the very engine pulling the eastern half back into the family of free nations. {pause: 0.8}

For a man like Kohl, the union of Europe was never really about money, whatever the bargains looked like on paper. It was about making sure the thing that happened to his generation could never happen again. And in that room, in 1997, he could see it: peace, made permanent, on a scale nobody had dared imagine. {pause: 0.8} So the biggest man in the room put his head down {pause: 0.8} and cried.

## The bill not yet presented {tone: somber, rate: slow, note: "the handover; hold the high-water mark, then turn it cold and point hard at Episode 8"}
So look at where we stand in the spring of 2004, at the very top of the mountain. {pause: 0.6}

A shared currency in three hundred million pockets. Ten new members, the eastern half of the continent come home. A Germany reunified and bound so tightly into Europe that the old nightmare of 1913 seemed finally, permanently laid to rest. A divided continent healed, without a shot fired. By every measure the confident men of Brussels liked to use, the project had *won.* The dream of the founders — of the ground above the Meuse, of the ruins of 1945 — had come true beyond anything they could have hoped. {pause: 0.8}

And now count, quietly, what that victory was actually built on. {pause: 0.8}

They had built a currency with no country underneath it. {pause: 0.6} They had written the rules that were supposed to hold that currency together — and then the two founders had broken those rules themselves and switched off the alarm, teaching every government on the continent that the discipline was optional. {pause: 0.6} And they had deepened and enlarged the Union faster and further than any electorate had ever actually endorsed — asking the peoples only rarely, and, when the peoples said no, asking again until the answer came back yes. {pause: 1.0}

The whole magnificent edifice now rested on one unspoken bet. The same bet, in the end, that this entire series has been circling since the very first chapter: that a solidarity *engineered* from the top — treaties and currencies and rules the peoples never quite chose — would hold. That when the first real storm came, the strangers sharing one coin would behave like a family. {pause: 1.0} That the engineered peace would prove, when it was finally tested, to actually *belong* to the people living inside it.

{tone: grave} For sixty years, they had built the peace *at* the peoples more than *with* them. Marshall's money and Schuman's morning and de Gaulle's two-faced sentence and Mitterrand's bargain in the hills — all of it, brilliant, and all of it done to the people rather than by them. And for sixty years, the bill for that had never quite come due. {pause: 1.0}

In 2008, it comes due. {pause: 1.2}

{rate: very-slow} A storm is coming that will test, all at once, every shortcut and every unspoken bargain and every rule they broke. A currency with no country is about to meet the worst financial crisis in eighty years. {pause: 1.2} And the continent is about to find out {pause: 0.8} whether the solidarity it engineered {pause: 0.6} was ever *real.* {pause: 1.4}
