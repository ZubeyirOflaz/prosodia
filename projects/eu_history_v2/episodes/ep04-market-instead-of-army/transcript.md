## The professor behind the wire   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "open in scene on a person; low, close, unhurried — no framing yet, just the man"}

A German officer stands in front of a blackboard in the flat heat of the Mississippi Delta, chalk in his hand, and begins to lecture on the law.

There is a fence behind him. Behind the fence, guard towers. The men in the rows in front of him are prisoners, like he is — captured soldiers of a beaten army, thousands of miles from a country that lies in ruins. This is Camp Como, in the American South, sometime near the end of the Second World War. And this particular prisoner has decided that captivity is no reason to stop working. So he has organized a university. A camp university — behind the wire, in the dust and the mosquitoes — where captured Germans teach one another, and where he, a professor in civilian life, teaches jurisprudence. The rules that are supposed to hold civilizations together, taught inside a cage, {pause: 1.2} by a man whose civilization has just torn itself apart.

His name is Walter Hallstein. {pause: 1.0}

Hold on to him, because he is going to come back at the end of this story wearing an entirely different costume. In about thirteen years, this prisoner in the Mississippi heat will sit at a long table in Rome and put his signature on one of the founding documents of the modern European Union — and then he will run its first government, and behave, quite openly, as though he were the prime minister of a country that does not yet exist. {pause: 0.6}

But the man teaching law behind the wire has not lost only a war. {tone: somber} He has watched the nation-state — the sovereign nation, the thing that raises armies and marches them across borders — produce this. The fence. The towers. The rubble on the other side of the ocean. And somewhere in that experience he arrives at a conviction he will say out loud for the rest of his life: {rate: very-slow} that national sovereignty is a "doctrine of yesteryear." {pause: 1.2} The disease, not the cure. {pause: 1.2}

Remember that phrase. We're going to need it, and so is a very angry Frenchman I'll introduce you to at the very end.

## Standing in the wreckage   {tone: somber, rate: slow, note: "the weight of a recent, total defeat; heavier than the opening"}

To understand why a man like Hallstein matters — why his kind of thinking wins — you have to stand where this whole movement was standing a couple of years before those treaties got signed. Not in a moment of hope. In a moment of total, humiliating defeat.

Cast your mind back to the last chapter. The 30th of August, 1954. The French National Assembly, by a procedural trick, refused even to *debate* the European Defence Community — the European army that France itself had proposed. Not voted down on the merits. Ruled unworthy of discussion. Killed in a single Paris afternoon, without ever being argued at all. {pause: 0.8}

Four years of work. Six nations' signatures. {tone: grave} Gone. {pause: 1.4}

And it wasn't only a treaty that died that day. The whole *strategy* underneath it died too — the belief that you could build Europe by marching straight at the heart of the state, at its army, its constitution, the naked nerve of its sovereignty, and expect a national parliament to simply hand those things over. Altiero Spinelli had smuggled a European constitution into that military treaty. Alcide de Gasperi had staked his life's faith on it — and gone to his grave in August of 1954, weeks before the collapse, {pause: 1.0} never knowing the thing he'd helped launch was already dead.

So feel the mood among the people who believed in this project in the autumn of 1954. They had reached for everything, all at once, and lost all of it. {tone: grave, rate: very-slow} The federalist dream — Europe as a *state* — was a corpse on the floor. {pause: 1.4}

And the man whose name is stamped on the whole enterprise, the great architect himself, {tone: wry} couldn't even manage to quit.

## The man who could not resign   {tone: wry, rate: normal, note: "let the absurdity breathe; a small human comedy inside the gloom"}

Jean Monnet. You've met him across these chapters. The cognac merchant's son who never went to university, who talked his way into a Prime Minister's confidence at twenty-six, who wrote the Schuman Declaration in a private marathon and handed it to another man to read aloud in his place. The inventor of the whole method: don't ask permission, build the finished thing, let the momentum drag everyone along behind it.

By November of 1954, Monnet has had enough of the inside game. He announces he will not renew his mandate as head of the Coal and Steel Community's High Authority — the one real institution his method had actually built. He wants out. He starts, like a man clearing his desk, to pack.

And then his own lawyers arrive with a small, exquisite problem. {pause: 0.6}

He *can't* go. Not legally. Under the rules, he cannot vacate the office until a successor has been formally named — and no successor has been named. So the great man who wanted to walk out in protest is chained to his own chair for five more months {pause: 1.0} by the very machinery he spent his life constructing. {pause: 0.6}

Now, watch what he does with it, because it tells you exactly who Monnet was. A smaller man sulks. Monnet uses those five stranded months — trapped, humiliated, legally bolted to the wreckage of the old idea — to start sketching the *next* one. A new pressure group. A new push. The map of a comeback, drawn at a desk he wasn't allowed to leave.

But the relaunch that truly mattered — the one that built the thing still standing today — {tone: measured} that one didn't come from Monnet at all.

It came from a man who thought Monnet was flat *wrong*. {pause: 0.8}

## The forgotten architect   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "introduce a genuinely fresh face; let the listener feel this is a surprise"}

His name was Johan Willem Beyen. {pause: 0.6}

If someone has told you that this entire story is the story of one man — Monnet, Monnet, Monnet, all the way down — then Beyen is your correction. A Dutchman. Not a lifelong idealist with a manifesto in his coat. A *banker*. He had been a director at Unilever, he'd sat at the Bank for International Settlements, he'd done a turn at the International Monetary Fund. A man of ledgers and exchange rates and balance sheets. When people recite the founding fathers of Europe, they routinely forget he existed. The forgotten architect.

And Beyen was carrying a heresy. {pause: 1.2}

Monnet's whole faith — the thing that had produced the Coal and Steel Community — was that you integrate Europe *one sector at a time*. Pool the coal and steel. It works. Trust builds. You pool the next thing, then the next, and one morning you look up and there's a united Europe, laid brick by patient brick. Sector by sector, forever forward.

Beyen said: no. {pause: 0.8} You've got it upside down.

Integrate one sector at a time, he argued, and each sector simply learns to defend its own wall. The coal men guard coal. The steel men guard steel. The farmers guard the farms. Every last one of them lobbies to keep their own little fortified garden intact — so you meet resistance at every single step, and the steps never end. Since 1953 he'd been saying it: this method has a ceiling, and we are already scraping it.

What you need instead, Beyen said, is to throw *everyone into the same boat at the same time*. A horizontal customs union. A common market. Knock down the tariff walls across the *whole* economy in one stroke, so that every industry is exposed together and no single sector can shelter behind its own fence while the rest of them take the risk. Nobody gets a private exit. Everybody swims, or everybody sinks — together.

It is the exact inverse of the Monnet method. {tone: wry} And here's the turn that ought to make you grin. In April of 1955, Beyen lays his memorandum on the table — the Benelux countries carry it forward as their own — and it *wins the argument*. It wins the argument {pause: 0.8} that founds the modern European Union. {pause: 0.8}

And Jean Monnet — the great man, the father of it all — Jean Monnet *wasn't even in the room.* {pause: 1.4}

## Because a minister missed a plane   {tone: wry, rate: normal, note: "the almost-farcical accident behind a founding moment; keep it light"}

Which room, though? Here we run into one of my favorite facts in this whole saga, because it is so gloriously, absurdly human — so far from the marble-and-frescoes version that gets carved onto monuments.

The foreign ministers of the six coal-and-steel nations agree to meet in the summer of 1955 and talk about relaunching Europe from the ashes. And where do they hold this founding conference?

Messina. {pause: 0.6} A port town on the far northeastern tip of Sicily.

If you sat down to design, from scratch, the ideal birthplace for the European Economic Community — the thing that grows into the EU — you would not, in a thousand attempts, choose Messina. It's the bottom corner of Italy. It's a long, awkward journey for everyone involved.

So why on earth Messina? {pause: 1.0}

Because the Italian foreign minister, Gaetano Martino, was *stuck* there. There were regional assembly elections underway in Sicily, and Martino — who happened to be Sicilian, and whose birthplace happened to be Messina — couldn't get away to host the thing somewhere sensible. He was pinned to the island by local politics.

So the mountain went to Martino. The founding conference of the European common market convened in a Sicilian port town, in June of 1955, essentially because one man couldn't get on a plane. {pause: 1.0}

I linger on this on purpose, because it should tune your ear for the rest of the hour. {tone: measured, rate: slow} The textbook version of all this is destiny and grand vision and men gazing nobly at horizons. The truth is closer to bankers with heresies, foreign ministers stranded by provincial elections, and beaten men rebuilding out of humiliation. The peace of Europe was assembled by human beings, in real and often inconvenient rooms, {pause: 1.0} for reasons that were sometimes as small as a missed flight. {pause: 1.0}

At Messina, the six reach their decision. Relaunch. Not an army this time. Not a constitution. A *market*. And to work out how you'd actually do such a thing, they set up a committee — and they hand it to a Belgian.

## The indispensable Belgian   {tone: warm, rate: slow, note: "introduce the anchor with real warmth and respect"}

His name is Paul-Henri Spaak. {pause: 0.6}

If Beyen supplies the heresy, and Messina supplies the accident, then Spaak supplies the *will*. He is the fixer. The closer. The man whose particular genius is walking into a room full of quarreling delegations and not leaving until they have stopped quarreling. He chairs the committee that comes out of Messina — the one that will forever carry his name, the Spaak Committee. He drives its report. He'll chair the follow-on conference at Venice. He'll preside over the negotiations that grind all of it into treaty text. And in the end, he'll pick up a pen in Rome and sign for Belgium.

Spaak is the thread running through this entire episode. So whenever I say "the Spaak Committee," don't picture a document — picture a man, physically holding six suspicious governments in one room by sheer force of stubborn charm until they agree.

And into Spaak's committee, in the back half of 1955, walks a delegation from a country that is, yet again, the ghost at this particular feast. {pause: 1.0}

Britain. {pause: 1.0}

## The man they sent   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "this is a contested story; seed the ambiguity in the telling, do not resolve it"}

Now, remember the pattern. The British had stayed outside the Coal and Steel Community — in fact they'd been deliberately kept out of the Schuman plan, informed only after the fact, once the thing was already done. That was how it went. So this time, at least, the six do the courteous thing. They invite Britain in. Come to the Spaak Committee. Sit at the table. Help us design the common market.

And Britain sends a man named Russell Bretherton. {pause: 0.6}

Consider, for a moment, who Bretherton was. An economist. A career civil servant — an under-secretary at the Board of Trade. A serious and capable man. He was also — and I confess I love this detail — an amateur entomologist. A collector and student of insects. Butterflies. Beetles.

What he was *not* was a politician. Not a minister. Not a man with the authority to commit his government to anything at all. Britain sent, to the most important economic negotiation of the century, {pause: 0.8} a man with the standing of an observer. {pause: 0.8}

And here is the detail that haunts me, because it survives in Bretherton's own archived reports, in his own hand. He wrote that Britain, sitting there in that room, "had the power to guide the conclusions of this conference in almost any direction we like."

In almost any direction we like. {pause: 1.0}

He could *see* it. The man could see, clearly, that Britain — at that table, in that moment — held enormous sway over the shape of the thing being born. That Britain could have reached out and steered the whole European project toward its own instincts: something looser, freer, more Atlantic, more to London's taste. He could see the lever sitting right there in front of him. {tone: wistful} And he had no authority to put his hand on it and pull. {pause: 1.0} He had come to watch. {pause: 1.0}

And in November of 1955, the British Cabinet reaches down and pulls him out. Britain walks away from the table. {pause: 0.6} Again. {pause: 1.6}

## What did Bretherton actually say?   {tone: measured, rate: normal, note: "openly a historical mystery; refuse to pretend certainty"}

There is a famous story about *how* Bretherton left. A farewell speech, supposedly delivered to the committee on his way out the door. The version that comes down to us runs roughly like this. He tells the assembled Europeans that the treaty they're chasing has no chance. That they will never manage to negotiate it. That even if they somehow negotiate it, they'll never ratify it. And that even if they ratify it, it will never work. And then — so the story goes — he rises, and he leaves them with a line: {rate: slow} "au revoir, et bonne chance." {pause: 1.0} Goodbye, and good luck.

It's a marvelous line. {tone: wry} Dismissive, aristocratic, and wrong in every single particular — the perfect little portrait of British hauteur strolling out on the future of the continent. Jacques Delors would brandish it against the British decades later, in 1991, like a piece of courtroom evidence.

But here is my difficulty, and I'd rather lay it out for you plainly, because the honesty is the whole point. {tone: measured, rate: slow} We are not sure {pause: 1.0} he ever said it. {pause: 1.0}

The quote reaches us essentially through a single French witness. Bretherton's own private papers point the other way entirely — they suggest he genuinely believed Britain could have *shaped* the whole enterprise, which is hardly the state of mind of a man who thought it was doomed nonsense. And there's even a dispute about the manner of his going. Bretherton said he was *asked* to leave — recalled by London. Spaak said he *walked* out. {pause: 0.6}

So which was it? Did he stride out with a witty insult and a wave, or was he quietly summoned home by a Cabinet that had already decided Europe was not for Britain?

I can't tell you for certain, and I won't pretend otherwise. What I *can* tell you is that the substance underneath the story isn't in any doubt at all: Britain sent a man who could see the opportunity and had no power to seize it — and then took away even him. Whether that famous farewell captures Bretherton's own mind, or only the mind of the Cabinet that overruled him, {pause: 0.8} we may simply never know. And the not-knowing is more interesting than any tidy answer.

## The men who stayed at the table   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "callback to the opening image of Hallstein; earn it"}

Britain leaves. The six stay. And it's worth looking hard at *who* stayed, because several of these men were remarkable, and one of them we've already met.

The German who will eventually sign — the man now bent over the technical work of building a market — is our professor from the Mississippi camp. Walter Hallstein. The prisoner who ran a university behind the wire is now, back in a free and rebuilding West Germany, one of the sharpest legal minds in the delegation. And he carries into that room the conviction he formed behind the fence: that the sacred, sovereign nation-state is a doctrine of yesteryear. He does not think the nation is holy. {tone: grave} He watched the sovereign nation-state {pause: 1.2} put him behind that wire. {pause: 1.2}

So gather them at the table. Spaak, physically holding the delegations together. Beyen's horizontal idea as the frame everyone now works inside. The Dutch and the Belgians, the Italians, the Germans, the French, and little Luxembourg — all of them hammering out how you actually *construct* a common market. Tariffs to come down on a fixed timetable. A shared external wall raised against the rest of the world. Institutions built to police every bit of it.

And the whole thing very nearly fell apart. {tone: tense} Because inside one of those delegations — the German one — {pause: 0.6} a private war was being fought. {pause: 0.8}

## The heretic in the German cabinet   {tone: tense, rate: normal, note: "internal conflict, genuine stakes; the treaty almost dies right here"}

Here is something the tidy version of the story quietly leaves out. {pause: 0.6} The single most successful economic mind in all of Europe {pause: 0.8} was *against* this treaty. {pause: 1.0}

Ludwig Erhard. The architect of the *Wirtschaftswunder* — the German economic miracle. The man who had unleashed West Germany's astonishing recovery by *freeing* markets: tearing out the controls, ripping away the rationing, betting the whole country on competition and open trade. Erhard was Adenauer's Economics Minister. And Erhard looked at the proposed common market and *loathed* it.

Why would the great free-marketeer hate a common market? Because to Erhard, a customs union wasn't free trade at all — it was a *protectionist* device wearing free trade's clothes. Six countries dropping their tariffs among themselves while raising one common wall against everybody else on earth — that isn't openness, Erhard said. That's a fortress. A cozy, walled-off club of six. What Erhard wanted was something far bigger and far looser: a broad free-trade area stretching across all of Western Europe, open to the world, open to the British. Not this exclusive little garden with a fence around it.

And he was not standing alone. Franz Josef Strauss stood with him. At a meeting at Saint-Cloud, near Paris, at the very end of October 1956, the German position seized up completely — Erhard and Strauss hauling one way, the Europeanists hauling the other, and the whole thing deadlocked. {pause: 0.8}

{tone: dramatic} The father of the German economic miracle was trying to strangle the European common market in its cradle — {pause: 1.0} and he was doing it from *inside the German cabinet*. {pause: 1.0}

There was exactly one man on earth who could overrule him. {pause: 1.2}

## Adenauer chooses Europe over economics   {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "the psychology of a defeated Germany; deliberate, weighty"}

Konrad Adenauer. The old Chancellor. {pause: 0.8}

Adenauer overrode his own Economics Minister — overrode the most respected economist in the country, on the economist's own turf. He decided that the *political* prize was worth more than the economic argument Erhard was winning on the merits. And the political prize was this: Germany bound irreversibly into Europe. Germany made safe. Germany turned, for good, from a threat into a partner. He chose the fortress of six over the open free-trade area precisely *because* the fortress bound Germany in — and being bound in, tied down, locked to its neighbors, was the entire point of the exercise.

And then Adenauer did something else — something that tells you everything about the strange psychology of a defeated Germany, eleven years after the war.

He told the French ambassador, in effect: Germany cannot lead in foreign affairs. {pause: 0.8} Not after what we have done. *France* must lead. Germany will follow. {pause: 1.2}

Weigh what that actually is. That is the most powerful economy on the continent {pause: 1.0} deliberately going down on one knee. {pause: 1.0} Choosing to be subordinate. And it wasn't weakness — it was cold strategy. A confident, demanding Germany would have frightened everyone at the table and walked away with nothing. But a humble Germany — a Germany that said, "you lead, we'll support you" — allowed France to extract far better terms than it could ever have wrung out of a Germany throwing its weight around the room. Adenauer's deference *was* his instrument of power. He knelt because kneeling worked.

So the German civil war ends with Adenauer overruling Erhard, choosing the political Europe over the economic one, and choosing, on purpose, to walk a step behind France. {pause: 0.6}

But even with Germany's own house finally in order, the whole negotiation was still jammed. And the thing that finally broke it loose wasn't a noble idea, or a stirring speech, or a vision of tomorrow. {tone: dramatic} It was catastrophe. {pause: 0.8} Two catastrophes — landing in the same few days, a thousand miles away. {pause: 0.8}

## Two humiliations, one week   {tone: dramatic, rate: normal, note: "the hinge of the episode; two disasters breaking the deadlock at once"}

The autumn of 1956. {pause: 0.6}

Two things happen almost on top of each other, and together they change the mind of a continent.

In Egypt, Gamal Abdel Nasser has nationalized the Suez Canal. And Britain and France — the old imperial powers, in secret collusion with Israel — invade to take it back by force. Late October into early November of 1956. And it turns into a disaster. Not on the battlefield — militarily, they are winning. A *political* disaster. Total, and humiliating. Because the United States turns on its own allies. Washington applies brutal financial pressure — leans on the pound, refuses to prop up sterling as it slides — and forces Britain and France into a scrambling, shameful withdrawal.

The lesson comes down like a hammer, and it lands hardest in Paris. France and Britain — acting alone, as great powers, in the very world they used to own — could be *overruled by a phone call from Washington*. {pause: 1.2} They were not great powers anymore. {rate: slow} Not on their own. Not in the world as it now was. {pause: 1.0}

And at that exact moment — the very same days — the Soviet Union rolls its tanks into Budapest and crushes the Hungarian uprising. Naked, brutal, and splashed across the front page of every newspaper on earth.

So here is the picture facing Europe, in the first week of November 1956. To the east, {pause: 0.6} the Soviet Union murdering a nation's hope under tank tracks. To the west, {pause: 0.6} the United States humiliating France and Britain in public and reminding them, coldly, that they are clients now — not powers.

{tone: grave, rate: slow} Caught between the two giants. {pause: 0.8} Small, and exposed, and alone. {pause: 1.6}

And on the 5th and 6th of November — *within days* of the Suez ceasefire, the humiliation still raw and stinging — Adenauer travels to Paris to sit down with the French Prime Minister, Guy Mollet. And there, under the shadow of those two fresh disasters, the two men clear away the last obstacles standing between Europe and the Treaties of Rome. {pause: 0.8}

Do you feel the grim machinery of it? {tone: grave} The common market was not, in the end, sold to France by dreamers holding out a better tomorrow. It was sold to France by Nasser, and by Eisenhower, and by the Soviet tanks grinding through Budapest. France reached for Europe because Suez had just demonstrated, in the cruelest way imaginable, that France standing by itself amounted to nothing.

{tone: grave, rate: very-slow} The peace of Europe was midwifed by *humiliation*. {pause: 1.6} Keep that close, the next time someone tells you it was built on hope. {pause: 1.0}

## The bargain nobody would say out loud   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "the central ambiguity; voice both readings, resolve neither"}

Now, with the last obstacles falling away, we arrive at the bargain sitting at the very heart of the treaty — and at the second thing in this episode I genuinely cannot tell you for certain.

Why did France finally say yes? Because even after Suez, France was nervous. A common market meant German industry — efficient, disciplined, frankly terrifying German industry — competing head to head against French factories with the walls torn down. So what did France get in return, to make that bearable?

France got agriculture. {pause: 1.4}

The deal, in its rough shape, was this. Germany wanted the industrial common market — open borders for its manufactured goods, the whole continent as a showroom. France wanted protection and support for its enormous, politically untouchable population of farmers. So the treaty gestured toward what would grow into the Common Agricultural Policy — a system, eventually, of guaranteed prices and subsidies that would pour money into the French countryside. The customs union to feed German industry; the farm support to shelter French farms. Industry on one side of the scale, agriculture on the other, balanced against each other.

And here — right here — is the ambiguity that historians are *still* arguing about. {pause: 1.0}

Was that a clear, *shared* understanding? Everyone at the table knowing precisely what had been agreed — a fair trade, openly struck, eyes open on both sides? {pause: 0.8} Or was it a *deliberate* ambiguity — a bargain left deliberately vague, the details of the farm policy left unwritten on purpose, so that each government could go home and tell its own parliament whatever that parliament needed to hear? So that France, in particular, could come back later and work the vagueness for everything it was worth — extracting far more than Germany ever believed it had promised? {pause: 1.2}

Both readings are alive in the record. Genuinely both. And I'm not going to choose between them for you, because the historians haven't chosen either. {pause: 0.6}

But notice what it tells us, either way — whichever version turns out to be true. Whether it was struck in the open or left vague on purpose, the durable, load-bearing heart of this treaty was a deal about money and markets that ordinary people never voted on and, for the most part, never even understood. {tone: grave} And that was not an accident. That was the lesson of the last chapter, put quietly into practice.

## The lesson of the corpse   {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "the thesis of the episode, stated plainly; let it unsettle, don't answer it"}

Because — cast your mind back — this whole episode opened in the wreckage of the European army.

The men building the common market had just watched the direct approach die on the floor of a parliament. They had asked a national assembly, out loud and to its face, to hand over the army — the raw, exposed substance of the state itself — and the assembly had refused even to *discuss* it.

So they learned something. And it is the darkest, most consequential lesson in this entire series. {pause: 1.2}

{rate: very-slow} *Never again ask the people directly.* {pause: 1.6}

Don't put the sovereignty question on the table. Don't hold the referendum. Don't stand a nation up and demand that it vote, yes or no, to melt itself into something larger. You will lose. You lost with the army, and you will lose again, every time, if you ask.

So instead — bury the integration *inside* prosperity. Build a market. Bring down the price of a car, and a refrigerator, and a holiday across a border. Let trade thicken, let jobs follow the trade, let a whole generation grow up comfortable and prosperous inside a shared European economy — and let the *loyalty* grow up quietly out of the comfort, without anyone ever once having to stand in a chamber and vote on the principle of surrendering their nation. Build the market first. Then let the market build the Europeans. {pause: 0.8}

It is brilliant. It worked. It is arguably the single most successful act of political engineering in the modern age — a Franco-German war made not merely unthinkable, but, in Schuman's old phrase, materially impossible. The two economies were welded together so completely that separating them enough to fight would have wrecked them both.

And it should trouble you a little, even so. Because it means that the thing which lasted — the spine of the entire European Union, still holding it upright today — was engineered by men who had decided, deliberately and on principle, {pause: 1.2} that the surest road to a united Europe ran through making certain the people of Europe were *never quite asked*. {pause: 1.4}

Does that reassure you, or does it unsettle you? {pause: 1.0} Carry the question with you. We are going to need it before this series is done.

## Val-Duchesse, and a treaty with a buried secret   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "the second treaty; let the reveal go cold and quiet"}

Through the winter of 1956 and on into the spring of 1957, the detailed negotiations run inside a chateau on the edge of Brussels — Val-Duchesse. Spaak presiding, as ever. The six grinding, clause by clause, through the technical work, turning the grand political bargains into cold treaty text.

And what came out was not one treaty. It was two. {pause: 0.6}

The first is the giant. The European Economic Community. The common market. Beyen's horizontal customs union, at last made real on paper. That is the one that lasts — the one that grows into the EU we know.

The second was called Euratom — the European Atomic Energy Community. And its public face was gleaming, and hopeful, and clean: the six nations pooling their efforts on *peaceful* nuclear power. The atom harnessed for civilization instead of for war. A bright future in a reactor.

But there was something buried beneath that shining surface, {tone: tense} and the declassified documents have since dragged it into the light. {pause: 1.0}

Underneath the cover of peaceful nuclear cooperation, France, West Germany, and Italy were quietly exploring *weapons* cooperation. A shared bomb, in effect — sheltered inside the civilian program, hidden in plain sight behind the reactors and the good intentions. Adenauer, it is reported, wanted for Germany "as quickly as possible, the chance of producing our own nuclear weapons." {pause: 0.8}

{tone: grave, rate: slow} Weigh that. {pause: 1.0} Eleven years after the war, the men signing a peace treaty were, in one of its two halves, quietly assembling the shared machinery {pause: 1.2} for a nuclear arsenal. {pause: 1.2}

That scheme was not going to survive. Within a year, a man was going to walk back into power in France who found the very idea intolerable — who would insist that France's bomb, the *force de frappe*, be French, and French alone. No sharing. No Europe. He would kill the Euratom weapons scheme dead in 1958. {tone: measured} But that man has not yet returned to power. Just know that he is coming.

## The Room of the Horatii   {tone: reverent, rate: slow, note: "the signing; let the layered symbolism assemble and land with real weight"}

And so we come to the room. {pause: 0.6}

The 25th of March, 1957. Rome. The Capitoline Hill — the hill where the Roman Senate once met, where victorious generals rode in triumph through the crowds. There is a hall inside a Renaissance palace up there, and it has a name. The Sala degli Orazi e Curiazi. The Room of the Horatii and the Curiatii.

The frescoes on its walls tell an old, old story. Two cities at war — Rome and Alba Longa — and rather than bleed two whole armies white against each other, they strike a bargain that is almost merciful in its brutality. Each city will send three brothers. Three champions against three champions. They will fight to the death, {pause: 1.0} and whichever side is left standing, that city takes the victory. A war between nations decided by six men, {tone: grave} so that thousands of others need not die. {pause: 0.8}

That is the story painted on the walls of that room. {pause: 0.8}

And on the 25th of March, 1957, beneath those frescoes, the representatives of six modern nations sit down at a long table and sign a treaty. {pause: 0.8}

Six nations. In a room named for the moment when single combat between champions was substituted for slaughter between whole peoples. {pause: 1.0} None of this was chance. These were men soaked to the skin in symbolism, and they had chosen — deliberately, with care — a hall commemorating the oldest idea in all of European statecraft: that if you are only clever enough, you can find a *substitute* for total war.

Only this time, the substitute isn't three brothers with swords. {pause: 1.0} This time it's a *customs union*. {pause: 1.0}

It is, as it happens, also the feast of the Annunciation — the day the Christian calendar marks an announcement that changes everything that follows. The men who fixed that date would have known exactly what they were doing. Not one detail in that room was left to accident.

Belgium, France, West Germany, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands. Spaak signs for Belgium. Adenauer and Hallstein sign for Germany — and there is our professor from the Mississippi camp, chalk long since traded for a fountain pen, putting his name to the thing at last. The Treaties of Rome. The European Economic Community, and Euratom.

There is no European army anywhere in these pages. No constitution. No Peoples' Chamber elected by the ordinary people of Europe — all of that died with the defence community back in 1954, and was not resurrected. {pause: 0.8} What sits on the table instead is quieter, and, as it will turn out, far, far stronger. A timetable to dismantle tariffs. A common wall against the outside world. A promise about farms. A commission in Brussels to run the whole machine.

{rate: very-slow} A market instead of an army. {pause: 1.6}

Out of the ruins of total, humiliating defeat, the men who believed in Europe had built the thing that would actually endure — the spine still standing under the European Union today, more than half a century on. They had reached for the state, and lost everything. {pause: 0.8} They reached, instead, for the market — and won. {pause: 1.4}

## The prime minister of a country that doesn't exist   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "close the Hallstein arc from the opening; let it turn faintly ominous"}

The treaties come into force on the 1st of January, 1958. And the new machine is handed a government.

Walter Hallstein — our German law professor, the organizer of a prisoner-of-war university in the heat of Mississippi — becomes the first President of the European Commission. {pause: 0.6}

And Hallstein takes the job *seriously*. {pause: 0.6} Almost alarmingly seriously. He describes his Commission as something close to "a kind of European prime minister's" office. He will receive the credentials of foreign ambassadors — that solemn ceremony by which one nation's envoy formally presents himself to a *head of state* — as though Brussels were a national capital and he its leader. {pause: 1.0}

He behaves, in plain terms, like the prime minister of a country that does not yet exist. {pause: 1.2} A country he fully intends to *will* into being, patiently, through the slow machinery of the market — exactly the way the method prescribes. National sovereignty, remember, is to this man a doctrine of yesteryear. He formed that conviction behind barbed wire, and he means to act on it from behind a desk. {pause: 0.8}

For one moment, in 1958, the whole thing looks unstoppable. The machine is built. It has a government. And it has, at its head, a man who behaves as though the united Europe were already real — already born, and merely waiting for the last pieces to be bolted on. {pause: 1.0}

## The giant returns   {tone: tense, rate: slow, note: "the cliffhanger; turn the entire triumph cold; point hard at Episode 5"}

And in that very same year — 1958 — a giant walks back into power in Paris. {pause: 1.0}

Charles de Gaulle. {pause: 1.4}

He does not believe in "Europe." {pause: 0.8} He believes in *France*. He has just watched, with cold and undisguised fury, a scheme to fold France's future nuclear force into some shared European arrangement — and he regards the entire supranational apparatus, Hallstein's little court in Brussels with its ambassadors and its credentials and its airs of statehood, as a fiction. A pretension. {pause: 0.6} A theft of powers that belong, by right and by blood, to nations. To *his* nation. {pause: 0.8}

The engineers have just finished their machine. They have given it a government, and installed at its head a man who runs it like the prime minister of a Europe that is not yet a country.

And now the most formidable nationalist of the twentieth century has returned to power in the largest of the six member states — a man who looks at Walter Hallstein sitting there, claiming, in his eyes, the ceremony and standing of a head of state — a sovereignty that belongs to nations and never to Brussels — {pause: 0.8} and decides, quite simply, that this will not stand. {pause: 1.2}

They built the machine. {pause: 1.0}

{tone: grave, rate: slow} Now the General is going to try to break it. {pause: 1.2} From the inside.
