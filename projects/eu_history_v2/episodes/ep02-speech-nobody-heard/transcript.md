## The morning it was supposed to disappear   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "open quiet and conversational; you're pulling the listener in close"}
I want to begin with a speech.

Not because speeches are where history usually happens — most of the time they're where history *pretends* to happen, the ceremonial froth on top of decisions already made in some back room. {pause: 0.6} No. I want to begin with this one because it is the rarest thing in this whole story: a speech that actually did something. {pause: 0.4} A speech that {pause: 1.0} rebuilt a continent.

And the strangest part — the part I can't get past — is that the men who gave it spent that same morning trying to make sure their own country wouldn't *notice*.

Hold that thought. {pause: 0.5} We'll come back to that morning. Because to understand why anyone would *hide* the most consequential words of the twentieth century, you have to stand where Europe was standing when they were spoken.

## Where we left the continent   {tone: somber, rate: slow}
Remember where we left it.

Nineteen forty-five. The war is over, and Europe is not so much defeated as *erased*. Whole cities are rubble. The dead won't resolve into a number — tens of millions, {pause: 1.2} a horror so large it stops being arithmetic and becomes weather. {pause: 0.5} And in France, underneath the grief, there is a very specific, very old fear, the one we planted at the end of the last chapter: {tone: grave} the Ruhr. {pause: 0.4} The coal-and-steel heartland of Germany. The furnace that fed the war machine that came over the border three times in one lifetime.

{tone: grave} That fear is the lock. Everything in this episode is somebody trying to pick it.

But here is the problem that comes *first*, before any grand design, before treaties, before anyone can be clever. {tone: somber} {rate: normal} A continent flat on its back cannot be bound together, because it cannot *stand up*. There is no coal to fire the factories, no dollars to buy the coal, no food that isn't rationed to the edge of hunger. The winter of nineteen forty-six into forty-seven is one of the coldest in living memory, and people are burning their {pause: 1.0} furniture. {pause: 0.8}

So before anyone can engineer the peace, somebody has to give Europe the money to have a future at all. And that money is going to come from across an ocean — wrapped, as I said, in a speech that almost *nobody* was supposed to hear.

## Harvard, the fifth of June   {tone: measured, rate: slow}
The fifth of June, nineteen forty-seven. Harvard University. Commencement day.

Picture the man. George Marshall — the Secretary of State, the general who had run the entire American war effort, a figure of such gravity that Churchill called him the organizer of victory. He is standing up to receive an honorary degree, and he is wearing — and I love this detail, because it is so ordinary for a moment so enormous — a double-breasted gray sharkskin suit. {pause: 0.6}

And the speech he gives is *short*. {rate: normal} It has, by the accounts of the people who studied it afterward, virtually no details and no numbers. He does not announce a program. He does not say "here is a plan and here is the money." What he does instead is name the disease — hunger, poverty, desperation, chaos — and then he does something almost unheard of for a great power. {pause: 0.4} He throws the problem *back* across the Atlantic. {pause: 0.6} *You* Europeans, he says in effect, must be the ones to draw up the requirements. It is not for the United States to design your recovery. The initiative must come from Europe.

It is not a blueprint. {pause: 0.4} It is a {pause: 1.0} *dare*.

## Nothing of any importance   {tone: wry, rate: normal}
Now. Here's where it gets strange, and here's where this stops being a story about generosity and starts being a story about *method* — which is what this whole episode is really about.

Because the American government did not want you to hear that speech.

This is not a metaphor. {pause: 0.5} On that same day, the State Department was telling American reporters that the Harvard address was — and I'm quoting the brush-off — "nothing of any importance." President Truman, over in Washington, called a rival press conference, timed to pull the White House correspondents away, to make sure the big papers were looking somewhere else.

Why? {pause: 0.6} {tone: matter-of-fact} Politics, mostly. A Congress full of men who did not want to hear the word "Europe" attached to the word "billions." Float a grand rescue plan too loudly, too soon, and you hand its enemies a target before it can breathe. So the safest thing was to let the most important speech of the century go off like a firecracker {pause: 1.0} in an empty field.

## Acheson on the phone to the BBC   {tone: measured, rate: normal}
Except that one man had already decided otherwise.

Dean Acheson — the Under Secretary of State, sharp as a blade, later Secretary himself — understood exactly what he had in his hands, and he understood the peculiar trick of it. If America couldn't hear this speech, fine. But *Europe* had to. Europe was the audience that mattered, and the dare only worked if the people being dared actually caught the words.

So while his own government is busy burying the speech at home, Acheson is quietly on the telephone — to the British. To the BBC. Making sure the full text crosses the Atlantic and goes out over the airwaves before Washington can smother it. {pause: 0.8}

I want you to hold the *shape* of that in your mind, because it is the shape of this entire episode. {pause: 0.6} {rate: slow} The most important act is done in secret, or half in secret. It is routed *around* the public that it will affect. It reaches the people it needs to reach through a side door. And it works — it works *because* it went through the side door instead of the front.

Remember that. {pause: 0.4} We are going to see it again, in Paris, three years from now, and the stakes will be higher.

## Bevin by the radio   {tone: warm, rate: slow}
And here is the human moment I can't resist. {pause: 0.5}

Ernest Bevin — Britain's Foreign Secretary, a great bull of a man who had come up through the trade unions, no university, all instinct — is by his radio that evening. And he catches it. He hears Marshall's words come out of the wireless, and something in him, some old union organizer's instinct for the chance that won't come twice, tells him: {pause: 0.4} *this is it. Don't wait for a formal note. Don't wait for the diplomats.*

He would later describe it as grabbing the offer with *both hands*. The next morning he is on the phone to his French counterpart, Georges Bidault, in Paris. Two foreign ministers, moving before the paperwork, treating a university commencement address as if it were a lifeline thrown to a drowning man — {pause: 0.4} which is exactly what it was.

That is how the Marshall Plan begins. {pause: 0.5} Not with a signature. With two tired men and a radio, {pause: 1.0} deciding to believe an American general before their own governments had finished pretending not to listen.

## The line down the middle of a continent   {tone: grave, rate: slow}
But the offer was not made only to the West.

This is the part people forget. Marshall's dare was open — in principle — to *everyone*. To the whole of Europe, including the East. Including the Soviet Union and the countries the Red Army now sat on top of. And for one dizzy moment, it was a real question which way the continent would break. {pause: 0.8}

Then Stalin answered it. {pause: 0.5} And the answer drew a line down the middle of Europe {pause: 1.2} that would stand for forty years.

Because Stalin looked at Marshall aid and saw exactly what it was: not just food and dollars, but a *gravitational pull*. {rate: normal} Accept American money and you accept American accounting, American conditions, an American orbit. He would not have it. And more than that — he would not let the countries under his control have it either, no matter how badly they were starving.

## Czechoslovakia, and a four o'clock deadline   {tone: tense, rate: slow}
And to see what that meant — to *feel* it — you have to go to Prague.

Czechoslovakia in the summer of nineteen forty-seven is the last real democracy left in the East. A country with a foot in both worlds, trying, desperately, to stay itself. And on the seventh of July, its government looks at the Marshall offer and votes — *unanimously* — to accept it. To go to the Paris conference. To take the American hand. {pause: 0.6}

Unanimously. {pause: 0.4} Every minister. That is a country choosing the West with open eyes.

And then they are summoned to Moscow.

Stalin gives them a deadline. {pause: 0.5} They have until four o'clock on the tenth of July to reverse that vote. Their president, Beneš, is incapacitated — laid low by a stroke — so the men in that room are on their own. And here is the detail that tells you everything about how much choice they actually had: {pause: 0.6} by the end, the ministers are no longer debating *whether* to reverse it. They are debating only *how to explain* the reversal. {tone: grave} How to word the surrender. {pause: 1.2} The decision has already been taken for them, in Moscow, and they know it.

## A lackey of the Soviet Government   {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "let the quote land; do not rush the next line"}
And then there is Jan Masaryk.

The Czech Foreign Minister. Son of the founder of Czechoslovak democracy, a man who embodied his country's whole hope of standing free between two giants. He goes to Moscow to bend the knee, because there is no other option, and when he comes back, he says the thing that should be carved somewhere. {pause: 0.4} He says: {pause: 0.6}

{rate: very-slow} *"I went to Moscow as the Foreign Minister of an independent sovereign state. I returned as a lackey of the Soviet Government."* {pause: 1.2}

{rate: slow} Sit with that. {pause: 0.5} That is the sound of a country's independence ending — not with an invasion, not yet, but with a phone call and a four o'clock deadline.

And there is a darker coda, and I'll give it to you plainly because the record does: {tone: somber} eight months later, in March of nineteen forty-eight, after the Communists have taken full control of the country, Jan Masaryk is found dead in the courtyard below, {pause: 1.2} three floors beneath the window of his bathroom. {pause: 0.8} Whether he was pushed or whether he jumped, men have argued ever since. But the *timing* — the timing needs no argument. {tone: grave} The last free voice in the East went out that window, one way or another, and the line down the middle of Europe hardened into concrete.

## What the money actually did   {tone: measured, rate: normal}
So the Marshall Plan becomes a *Western* plan. That is the first thing it does — it doesn't just rebuild the West, it *defines* the West, by drawing the border of who was in and who was locked out.

And in the West, it works. {tone: warm} The dollars flow — billions of them. Factories restart. The furniture goes back in the houses. The near-starvation eases. And, quietly, something else happens that matters even more for our story: to receive the aid, the Europeans have to sit in a room *together* and agree on how to divide it. They have to cooperate. The Americans practically insist on it. {pause: 0.6}

For the first time since the war, the nations of Western Europe are forced into the habit of pooling their problems. That habit — that *muscle* — is about to be used for something far more ambitious than dividing up American dollars.

Because a continent that can stand up can now be asked a harder question. {pause: 0.5} Not "how do we survive?" {pause: 0.5} but "how do we make sure we never do *this* {pause: 1.0} to each other again?"

## The dreamers gather at The Hague   {tone: measured, rate: slow}
And there was no shortage of people with an answer. That was never the problem. Europe in these years is *drowning* in men with a vision of unity. The problem — the problem that has haunted this idea since the nineteen twenties — is that visions are cheap and sovereignty is *dear*.

Watch what happens when the dreamers actually get in one room.

May of nineteen forty-eight. The Hague. The Congress of Europe. Around seven hundred and fifty delegates — statesmen, intellectuals, former prime ministers — gathered under the honorary chairmanship of the most famous voice in the world, Winston Churchill, who had stood up in Zurich two years before and called, in that rolling thunder of his, for a "United States of Europe." {pause: 0.6}

It should have been the founding moment. Seven hundred and fifty of Europe's finest, and Churchill on the platform. {pause: 0.4} And instead it *splits* — almost the instant it convenes — straight down a fault line that is going to run through the *entire* rest of this series.

## Federalists and the men who wouldn't jump   {tone: measured, rate: normal}
On one side: the federalists. The French, the Belgians, the Italians, the Dutch. Their instinct — burn the box, build something *new*, a real European authority with real power that sits *above* the nations. They have watched the nation-state fail catastrophically, twice, and they are done trusting it.

On the other side: the intergovernmentalists. The British, above all, and the Scandinavians. Their instinct — nations are real, nations are the only thing that can actually be held accountable by voters, so let sovereign states *cooperate*, sit at a table, sign agreements — but never, ever hand a foreign body the power to overrule a national parliament. {pause: 0.6}

Cooperation versus surrender. A club of nations versus a government above them. {pause: 0.4} That is the whole argument, and it is worth understanding that both sides are made of serious, decent people who have both looked at the ruin and drawn *opposite* lessons from it. The federalist looks at the rubble and says: the nation did this, so we must rise above the nation. The Briton looks at the same rubble and says: only a nation answerable to its own people can be trusted with power at all.

Churchill's own son-in-law, Duncan Sandys, works the room, brokering language — the diplomat's art of writing a sentence vague enough that two people who disagree can both sign it. {pause: 0.5} {tone: wry} Nobody fully accepts it. Everybody signs anyway.

## A council with no teeth   {tone: wry, rate: normal}
And what comes out the other end of all that passion?

The Council of Europe. {pause: 0.6}

Now — the Council of Europe is not nothing. It exists to this day; it gives us the European Court of Human Rights, which is a genuinely great thing. But as a vehicle for *uniting* Europe, for building the thing the federalists dreamed of, it is — let's be honest — a talking shop. Consultative. Powerless over the things that count. An assembly that can debate and recommend and *implore*, and cannot make a single government do a single thing it doesn't already want to do.

The intergovernmentalists had won the first round. {pause: 0.4} The nations kept every scrap of their sovereignty. {pause: 0.6}

And so the federalists — beaten, but not converted — do the thing beaten idealists do. {tone: measured} They go looking for another vehicle. Something smaller, maybe. Sharper. Something that doesn't require seven hundred and fifty people to agree, because seven hundred and fifty people will *never* agree. Something that could be built by a handful of men who simply stopped asking permission.

Which brings us, at last, back to the strangest man in this entire story.

## The man who never ran for anything   {tone: warm, rate: slow, note: "affectionate, intrigued; introducing our protagonist"}
His name is Jean Monnet.

If you have been with me from the beginning, you've met him — the cognac merchant's son who never went to university, who talked his way into a Prime Minister's office at twenty-six, who spent thirty years insisting on one idea that everybody applauded and nobody acted on: {pause: 0.5} that states acting *together* can do what states acting separately cannot.

He is, on paper, absurd as a father of a continent. {tone: wry} He has never won an election. He will never win an election. He does not lead a party, a movement, a country. He holds no office that a voter ever chose. He is, in the most literal sense, an *unelected planner* — {tone: measured} and I want you to keep that phrase, because we are going to have to reckon with it before this hour is done.

His method was set by his own father, years before, on a dock, when the young Monnet was heading off to sell cognac in the wider world. The father's advice — {pause: 0.5} *"Don't take any books with you. No one can think for you. Look out the window, talk to people."* {pause: 0.6}

That is the whole man in one sentence. {tone: warm} Monnet does not persuade by memorandum or by grand oratory. He persuades over dinner. One person at a time. He finds the man who holds the lever, and he sits with him until the man decides that Monnet's idea was his own idea all along. He is not a prophet shouting in the square. He is the quiet guest who rearranges the furniture while you're pouring the wine.

## The lock and the key   {tone: measured, rate: slow}
And by nineteen fifty, Monnet is staring at the lock we planted at the very start. {pause: 0.6}

The Ruhr. The German coal and steel. In the French files of the time, the Ruhr is described — I'm giving you their own words — as "a symbol of German power and a source of French humiliation." That heartland had armed the enemy three times, and now, five years after the war, Germany is getting back on its feet, and its industry with it, and every Frenchman can feel the old dread rising: {pause: 0.4} {tone: tense} *it is happening again.*

{tone: measured} The old answer to that dread was control. Occupy the Ruhr. Cap it. Strip it. Keep the German boot off the German throat by keeping a French boot on it. But Monnet, looking out the window as his father taught him, sees that the old answer is a *trap*. Hold Germany down forever and you don't get peace — you get nineteen nineteen all over again, a humiliated giant nursing a grievance, waiting for its moment. He had watched that movie once. {pause: 1.0} He knew how it ended.

So he does the thing that makes him Monnet. {pause: 0.6} He turns the lock into a *key*.

## What if nobody owns it?   {tone: measured, rate: normal}
Here is the idea, and it is so simple that its audacity hides inside its simplicity.

What if France and Germany didn't *fight* over the coal and steel? {pause: 0.5} What if they didn't fight over *who controls it* — because *nobody* would control it? What if you took the coal and steel of France and Germany — and anyone else who wanted in — and put it *all* under a single authority that stood above every one of those nations? {pause: 0.4} A common pool. A shared roof.

Think about what that does. If the French and German steel industries are run under one shared authority, then France can *see* into the German furnace. It cannot be secretly re-forged into an army in the dark, because there is no dark anymore — {pause: 1.0} it's all one lit room. And here is the sharp end of it, the line that will be spoken aloud in a matter of weeks: {pause: 0.6} {tone: dramatic} if you pool the coal and steel, then war between France and Germany becomes not merely unthinkable — but *materially impossible.* {tone: measured} You cannot build the tanks in secret when your rival is co-managing the steel.

It is the federalist dream — an authority above the nations — but shrunk to something so concrete, so technical, so *small*, that maybe, just maybe, it can slip past everyone who would strangle a grand new "United States of Europe" in its cradle. Not a parliament. Not a flag. {pause: 0.4} Coal. Steel. A quota. A pool.

Don't ask them to love Europe. {pause: 0.4} Just get them to share a furnace, and let the loving come later — if it comes at all.

## Nine days in a farmhouse   {tone: measured, rate: slow}
So how do you turn an idea into a treaty when you hold no office and command no votes?

You go to your farmhouse. {pause: 0.6}

In the spring of nineteen fifty, Jean Monnet gathers a tiny team of trusted men — Reuter, Uri, Hirsch, Clappier — at his own house at Houjarray, in the countryside outside Paris. And there, over nine days, they write and rewrite. {rate: normal} Nine drafts in nine days. Not a committee of hundreds like The Hague — {pause: 0.4} four or five men in a farmhouse, arguing over a text, sleeping little, sharpening every line.

Picture it. {rate: slow} The kitchen table. The crossed-out pages. The one idea being hammered thinner and thinner {pause: 1.0} until it's sharp enough to cut through decades of fear. This is the *opposite* of The Hague. The Hague was seven hundred and fifty people building a debating society. This is five people, in secret, building a *bomb*.

And on the ninth draft, Monnet writes across the top, in blue ink — {pause: 0.5} *"final, to be sent to Schuman"* — and puts it in the hands of a courier.

## The man who would say it out loud   {tone: measured, rate: slow}
Because Monnet cannot deliver this himself. He is a planner, not a politician. He needs a man with actual power, a man of the government, willing to stand up and *own* this in public — and, more than own it, to risk his entire career on it.

He needs Robert Schuman.

And Schuman is the perfect, almost unbearably perfect, man for it. {pause: 0.5} He is the French Foreign Minister. But he is from Lorraine — that borderland that France and Germany have passed back and forth like a bone between two dogs. Schuman's *first language was German.* He served in the *German* army in the First World War, because at that moment his home was on the German side of the line. He is, in his own person, {pause: 1.0} the wound this whole plan is trying to heal. {pause: 0.4} A Frenchman who was once a German soldier, about to propose that France and Germany fuse their war-making industries so that no boy from Lorraine ever has to choose a side again.

The courier reaches Schuman's home at Scy-Chazelles. Schuman takes the document away for the weekend. And on the Sunday, he telephones Monnet. {pause: 0.6}

He says he will do it. He'll deliver it that Tuesday.

And he asks for *no revisions*. {pause: 0.8} None. He takes the text a planner wrote in a farmhouse, and he agrees to stake his name and his office on it, word for word, unchanged.

## Told before Britain. Told before France, almost.   {tone: tense, rate: normal}
Now watch the method — Acheson and the BBC, all over again, but sharper.

Because how do you get a plan like this past everyone who would kill it? {pause: 0.4} You do *not* consult them. You present it as a done thing.

The German chancellor, Konrad Adenauer — leader of a country that is still occupied, still under Allied control, a defeated nation with barely any sovereignty of its own — Adenauer is told *before Britain is.* A private message reaches him even as Schuman is preparing to speak. And Adenauer, who has been longing for exactly this — a way back into the family of nations, a France that offers partnership instead of a boot — Adenauer will later write that he "agreed to his proposal with all my heart."

But Britain? {pause: 0.5} Britain is deliberately *not* brought in. Not consulted, not shown the text in advance, not given the chance to water it down. Because Monnet and Schuman have learned the lesson of The Hague: bring in the British, and the British — who want cooperation, never surrender — will file the teeth off the thing until it's another toothless Council. So they keep it from the one government most likely to dilute it.

Even the French cabinet is barely looped in — some ministers learn the details only hours before. {pause: 0.4} This is not a proposal offered up for debate. It is a *fait accompli*, sprung on the world nearly finished. Engineered from the top, by men who mostly never won an election, and presented to everyone else as something that has *already happened.*

## The ninth of May   {tone: dramatic, rate: slow, note: "this is the peak; slow, weighty, historic — let the sentences breathe"}
The ninth of May, nineteen fifty. Paris. The Salon de l'Horloge — the Clock Room — at the French Foreign Ministry.

More than two hundred journalists in the room. And Robert Schuman stands up and reads the words a planner wrote in a farmhouse. {pause: 0.6}

He proposes that French and German coal and steel production be placed under a common High Authority, open to other European countries. And he says the sentence that this whole hour has been walking toward — {pause: 0.5} that this pooling will make war between France and Germany {rate: very-slow} "not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible." {pause: 1.5}

{rate: slow} That is the moment. {pause: 0.4} That is the founding act of the European project — not a battle, not a revolution, but a man reading a page in a room with a clock on the wall. Everything that follows — every treaty, the common market, the single currency, all of it — traces back to those words in the Clock Room. {tone: measured} Weeks later, Adenauer would stand before the German parliament and tell them the plan's importance was "above all political, and not economic." {pause: 0.5} Everyone in the know understood: this was never really about coal. It was about ending a thousand years of war.

## The founding of Europe was never photographed   {tone: wry, rate: normal, note: "a wry, almost eerie aside; reset intensity"}
And now let me hand you the detail that I think about more than any other in this entire episode. {pause: 0.5}

That moment — the founding of Europe, the ninth of May, two hundred journalists — was never *photographed*. {pause: 0.6}

No photographer. No radio recording. No newsreel camera. The single most important announcement in the postwar history of the continent went off, and *nobody captured it.* Every image you have ever seen that claims to show Schuman making the declaration — every one — was actually shot on the twentieth of June, weeks later, at the opening of the negotiations. And Schuman himself had to go back into a studio and *re-record* the declaration, after the fact, so that posterity would have something to play.

Think about what that means, and how perfectly it fits the man who made it. {pause: 0.6} {tone: measured} Monnet's whole method was to work in the quiet, through side doors, without spectacle — to rearrange the furniture while everyone else was watching the front of the house. And so the founding act of a united Europe has *no authentic image of itself.* It had to be staged, afterward, for the cameras. The most public consequence in modern European history was born in a room that, for the historical record, {pause: 1.0} was essentially empty. {pause: 0.5}

{tone: wry} It is almost too on the nose. The peace was engineered off-camera. And then, later, someone remembered to take the picture.

## Attlee's beautiful contradiction   {tone: wry, rate: normal}
And Britain? Britain, kept out of the room, now has to decide whether to walk into it.

And Britain says no. {pause: 0.5}

The reasons are tangled and, in one case, gorgeously self-contradictory. Clement Attlee's Labour government refuses to join the coal and steel community, and one of the objections is that Britain will not, in the words of the moment, hand its economy "over to an authority that is utterly undemocratic and responsible to nobody." {pause: 0.6}

Now — hold on. Sit with the irony. This is a Labour government that has *just nationalized* British coal and steel. They have just taken those industries into the hands of the British state, the British people, the British parliament. Of course they won't surrender them to a supranational body answerable to nobody — they've only just finished handing them to *themselves.* {pause: 0.5} You cannot surrender to Europe the very thing you have just triumphantly seized for the nation.

{tone: measured} It is a completely rational position. And it is a completely *fateful* one. Because Britain, right here, at the founding, chooses to stand outside — and that choice, made in nineteen fifty, is going to echo down every decade of this series, {pause: 1.0} all the way to a referendum sixty-six years later. {tone: wry} But that, as they say, is a story for much later.

## Who actually built Europe?   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "raise the ambiguity honestly; do NOT resolve it"}
And before we go on, I have to stop and name an argument, because I promised you I would never hand you a false-clean story.

Whose founding was this, really? {pause: 0.6} Who *authored* Europe?

The obvious answer is Monnet. He conceived it. His team wrote it — nine drafts in his farmhouse. He engineered the whole fait accompli. And Schuman, on this reading, was the mouth: a distinguished, courageous mouth, but a mouth for another man's mind.

But listen to the other side, because it's fair. {rate: normal} Schuman's defenders point out that Monnet's brilliant text was, until Schuman touched it, just another *planning paper* — one more clever memo in a decade drowning in clever memos. It was Schuman who staked his career on it. Schuman who stood up in the Clock Room and made it real, knowing that if it failed he'd be finished. A plan nobody dares to *deliver* is not a plan; it's a daydream. And there's a third man some historians push forward — Bernard Clappier, Schuman's own chief of staff, the quiet bridge who may have been the one who got Schuman to embrace the thing at all. {pause: 0.6}

{rate: slow} So who built Europe? The man who designed the lock, or the man who was willing to be caught turning the key? {pause: 0.5} I'm not going to resolve it for you, because the honest historians haven't resolved it either. I'll only say this: it seems to have needed *both* — the planner who wouldn't seek the spotlight, and the politician willing to stand in it. Which is its own quiet lesson about how big things actually get done.

## The uncomfortable question   {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "the moral core of the episode; let it breathe, do not answer it"}
But there's a larger question underneath that one, and it's the one this whole hour has been circling, and I'm not going to let us off the hook.

Look at what we've watched. {rate: normal} A rescue plan hidden from the American public and slipped to Europe through the back door. A declaration written in secret by a man no voter ever chose. A German chancellor told before the British. A British government deliberately shut out so it couldn't dilute the thing. A founding act sprung on the world nearly finished, so that no parliament could pick it apart before it drew breath. {pause: 0.6}

{rate: slow} The founding of a *democratic* union was, in its method, {pause: 1.0} profoundly *undemocratic.* A fait accompli, engineered from the top by a handful of men, most of whom never won an election in their lives.

## The pool comes into force   {tone: measured, rate: normal}
For now, the gamble holds. {pause: 0.5}

The treaty is signed. Six nations come in — France, West Germany, Italy, and the three small Benelux countries, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg. The European Coal and Steel Community. In July of nineteen fifty-two it comes into force, and Jean Monnet — the man who never ran for anything — becomes the first President of its High Authority. {pause: 0.6}

Stop and feel how strange that is. The unelected planner is now, quite literally, the head of the first supranational European authority in modern history. {tone: warm} The furniture-rearranger has been handed a house of his own. His method — theory for thirty years, mocked as the daydream of a cognac merchant's son — has become a treaty, an institution, a *fact.*

{tone: measured} For the first time in modern European history, sovereign nations have handed a piece of themselves — a real piece, the coal and the steel, the very sinews of war — to something *above* them. The lock has been picked. The furnace is shared. And the men who did it are allowed, for one brief moment, to believe they have found the formula. That from here it is only a matter of scaling up. Pool the coal, then pool the next thing, and the next, and let the loving come later.
