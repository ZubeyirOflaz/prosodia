## The question with nowhere left to hide   {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "open cold on one hard sentence; no scene yet, no date; lay it down and let it stand"}

If a peace can only be kept by overriding the people it was built for — if its architects reach past the voters again and again, every time the voters say no — {pause: 1.0} is it still their peace? {pause: 1.6}

That is the sentence this whole story has been walking toward for seven episodes. No date under it yet, no name to hide it behind. Just the question. {pause: 1.0}

For sixty years, the men in this series had an answer to that question, and their answer was: *not yet.* Not yet, but soon. Give them prosperity, give them time, give them a generation of open borders and cheap holidays and a shared coin in the pocket, and the loyalty will grow up quietly on its own, and one day the gap between what we built and what they wanted will simply close, and nobody will remember it was ever there. {pause: 0.8}

This is the episode where the bill for that answer comes due. {pause: 0.6} All of it. At once. {pause: 1.2}

## A conference room in London   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "the hook scene; drop the listener into the room, unhurried, before the stakes are named"}

The 26th of July, 2012. A conference room in London — investors, cameras, the flat institutional light of a hotel function suite. On the platform, a compact, grey, precise Italian named Mario Draghi. {pause: 0.6}

He is the President of the European Central Bank — the man who sits at the very top of the machinery that manages the euro, the single currency of some three hundred million Europeans. And on this particular morning, that currency is dying. {pause: 1.0}

Not failing slowly. *Dying* — measured in days. The cost for Italy and Spain to borrow money, to roll over the debts that keep two of Europe's largest economies functioning, has been climbing all summer toward the exact altitude that had already bankrupted Greece. The markets have looked at the euro, this grand experiment in binding nations together through their wallets, and concluded it is going to come apart. And markets do not merely predict a collapse like that. {pause: 0.6} By betting on it, they *cause* it.

So Draghi stands up to speak. And what he is carrying up to that podium is the whole point. {pause: 0.8} He has *nothing.* No new program. No rescue package agreed in some midnight summit. No fresh authority handed to him by the governments. He has not cleared what he is about to say with his own board. {pause: 1.0}

He has a sentence. {pause: 1.4}

## Whatever it takes   {tone: dramatic, rate: slow, note: "the peak of the hook; deliver the quoted line slowly, then let the silence do the work — do NOT verbalize it"}

He is talking, in that dry central-banker's register, about the strength of the currency. And then, almost as an aside, without raising his voice, he says it.

{rate: very-slow} "Within our mandate, the ECB is ready to do whatever it takes to preserve the euro." {pause: 1.2} And then he leans very slightly forward, and adds five more words that are not economics at all — they are pure will. {pause: 0.8} "And believe me, {pause: 0.5} it will be enough." {pause: 2.0}

Within hours, the borrowing costs that had been climbing all summer begin to fall. Not because anything had changed in the actual economies of Italy or Spain — nothing had. Not because a treaty had been signed or a parliament had voted or a single euro had yet been spent. {pause: 0.8} The panic broke because one man stood up and *said* it would. {pause: 1.0}

The concrete policy those words implied — the mechanism, the fine print — the bank did not even announce until September, weeks later. It almost didn't matter. The *words* had already done the work. {pause: 0.6}

So sit at the front of that room for a moment and take the measure of what you just watched. The single currency of three hundred million people — the crowning achievement of the whole project, the thing they had spent forty years engineering — was saved. Not by a vote. Not by a treaty. Not by the peoples whose money it was. {pause: 0.8} It was saved by one sentence, from one man that no European had ever elected, spoken past his own board, on his own authority, into a hotel microphone. {pause: 1.4}

{tone: grave} That is the method this series has been describing since the first hour — the method of building over the heads of the people {pause: 0.6} — in its purest form. {pause: 1.0}

To understand how Europe arrived at a room where an unelected banker held the fate of a continent in one sentence, we have to go back a few years — but not far, and not wide.

## A constitution for a place that isn't a country   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "the first collision begins; lay out the ambition plainly, warmly even, before the fall"}

Start in the early years of this century, with the project at what looks, from the outside, like the summit of its confidence.

{tone: warm} The Community that began as six nations pooling their coal and steel is now a Union of twenty-five, sweeping east across the old Iron Curtain into countries that, within living memory, sat behind Soviet tanks. It has a single currency. It has a parliament, a court, a flag, an anthem. And now its leaders decide it is time for the capstone — the thing that would make Europe, at last, feel like a *thing:* a Constitution. {pause: 0.8}

Not a treaty. A *Constitution.* A single grand document to replace the tangle of accumulated treaties with one clean, dignified text — the kind of founding parchment that nations have. And to write it, they do something that sounds, on its face, almost democratic. They convene a great Convention. Over a hundred members — parliamentarians, governments, the Commission — chaired by a towering figure from the French past, a former President of the Republic named Valéry Giscard d'Estaing.

And Giscard takes the assignment with enormous seriousness. He compares himself, without much embarrassment, to the founders who gathered in Philadelphia to write the American constitution. He believes he is drafting the birth certificate of a European nation. {pause: 1.0}

{tone: measured} There is only one difficulty with drafting the birth certificate of a nation. {pause: 1.2} You need a nation. {pause: 1.0} And what Charles de Gaulle stood up and said forty years before this — you heard him say it, in an earlier chapter — was still, stubbornly, true. There is no single European people. No *demos.* No one body of citizens who feel, in their bones, that they are one people, that this is *their* constitution being written in their name. {pause: 1.0} There are Frenchmen, and Dutchmen, and Poles. And you are about to find out, the hard way, that they know it. {pause: 1.2}

## The peoples say no   {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "the first rejection; two hard votes, delivered with weight, no rush"}

The Constitution is finished, and signed by the governments, and now it has to be ratified — approved — country by country. And some countries, unusually, decide to do the thing the project almost never does. They decide to *ask the people.* Directly. In a referendum. {pause: 0.8}

France goes first. The 29th of May, 2005. The founding nation. The country of Monnet and Schuman and the whole idea. The country whose own former President chaired the Convention that wrote the thing. {pause: 0.6}

France votes no. {pause: 1.0} Fifty-five percent against. {pause: 1.2}

Three days later — the 1st of June — the Netherlands. Another founding member, one of the original six who signed at Rome. {pause: 0.6}

The Netherlands votes no. {pause: 0.8} By an even wider margin. Sixty-one percent against. {pause: 1.4}

Two founding nations. Within four days. Both asked directly, in plain words, *do you want this* — and both answering, without much ambiguity at all, {pause: 0.6} *no.* {pause: 1.2}

And Giscard — the man who thought he was writing Europe's Philadelphia — watches his life's crowning document rejected by his *own country.* Not by some reluctant newcomer on the fringe. By France. {tone: wistful} He was, by every account, devastated. {pause: 0.6} The birth certificate had been torn up by the very people it was meant to name. {pause: 1.4}

## The thing that would not stay dead   {tone: tense, rate: slow, note: "the resurrection; this is the pivot of the whole first act — let it feel uncomfortable"}

Now. What do you do, if you are the men who built this, and the peoples have just said no? {pause: 1.0}

You already know the answer, because you have watched these men solve this exact problem for seven straight episodes. You do not accept the no. You do not put the same question back to the same people and risk the same answer. {pause: 0.6} You find another road. {pause: 1.0}

So the Constitution does not die. {pause: 0.8} It goes into a room, and it comes back out wearing a different coat. {pause: 0.8}

They take the substance of the rejected Constitution — the same institutional machinery, the same transfers of power, very nearly the same text — and they repackage it. They strip away the word "Constitution," and the flag, and the anthem, and the grand founding language that made it *feel* like a nation being born. And they call what is left a *treaty* — an ordinary amending treaty, of the boring technical kind that governments sign all the time without asking anybody. They sign it in Lisbon, in 2007, and they call it the Lisbon Treaty. {pause: 0.8}

And because it is now merely a treaty and not a Constitution, almost no country has to hold a referendum on it. The peoples who said no are simply {pause: 0.8} not asked again. {pause: 1.2}

One of the men who negotiated it as good as admitted the trick out loud — that the changes were cosmetic, the substance kept, the labels swapped precisely so that referendums could be *avoided.* The no had not changed a single mind at the top. It had changed the *packaging.* {pause: 1.0}

{tone: grave} Read that carefully, because it is the whole method laid bare. The peoples were asked. The peoples answered. {pause: 0.6} And the answer was routed around. Not defied — that would have been honest. {pause: 0.6} *Routed around.* {pause: 1.4}

## Ireland, and the question asked twice   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "the sharpest illustration; keep it plain and let the second vote land as an indictment or a rescue — do not decide which"}

There was one country that could not route around its own people, because its constitution would not let it. Ireland. In Ireland, a treaty like this had to go to a referendum. The people had to be asked.

Ireland votes no. {pause: 0.8} Fifty-three point four percent against. {pause: 1.0}

And here the pattern reaches its purest, and to some its ugliest, expression — because you have seen it before, in an earlier chapter. And now it is done not to a single sub-clause, but to the whole reborn constitution of Europe. {pause: 0.8}

Ireland is given some assurances, some clarifications, some promises about what the treaty does and does not mean. And then, sixteen months later, in October 2009, the very same treaty is put to the very same people. A second time. {pause: 1.0}

{tone: wry} You can say: the Irish were bullied. {tone: measured} Or you can say: the first vote was fought on confusion and scare stories about things the treaty never contained, the concerns were genuinely addressed, and a better-informed people freely changed its mind — which is a thing that free peoples are entirely allowed to do. {pause: 1.0}

## The fuse in Athens   {tone: tense, rate: normal, note: "the second collision opens; brisker now — a countdown lighting"}

So that is the first collision. A constitution rejected, and resurrected in disguise. The gap between the architects and the peoples, papered over one more time.

But you cannot paper over a currency. {pause: 1.2}

Because at almost the exact moment Ireland is being asked to vote again — the autumn of 2009 — a fuse is being lit at the other end of the continent, in Athens, that is going to burn straight into the heart of everything these men built. {pause: 0.8}

A new government comes to power in Greece, led by a man named George Papandreou. And within days of taking office, his people open the national accounts and find something that stops the breath. {pause: 0.6} The budget deficit — the gap between what the Greek state spends and what it takes in — that Greece had officially reported was around three point seven percent. {pause: 0.8}

The real figure was over twelve percent. {pause: 1.0} And when they finished digging, it was revised higher still — to more than fifteen. {pause: 1.2}

Not a rounding error. Not an accounting quarrel. The books of a member state of the eurozone — a country sharing the single currency with Germany and France — had been, in plain terms, false. And now the truth was out. {pause: 0.8}

The markets looked at Greece and did the cold arithmetic, and concluded Greece could not pay what it owed. And so, in the spring of 2010, came the first enormous rescue — a hundred and ten billion euros — and with it a word that would become a curse across southern Europe for a decade: the *Troika.* The three institutions — the Commission, the central bank, the International Monetary Fund — that would now, in exchange for the money, effectively run Greece's finances from the outside. {pause: 1.0}

{tone: grave} Sit with the shape of that, because it is the method again. A nation's budget — the most sovereign thing a democracy has, the very substance of what a people votes for — taken out of the hands of the people who elected the government, {pause: 0.6} and placed under the supervision of three bodies that no Greek ever voted for at all. {pause: 1.2}

## The thing they built with no exit   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "the structural trap; explain why this crisis was different — calm, analytical, ominous"}

Here is why Greece was not just Greece. Why a small country at the edge of the map could threaten the whole.

When these men built the euro — and again, I am not re-telling that story, only naming the flaw at its heart — they built a currency shared by many nations, but they gave it no shared treasury. No common budget of any size. No mechanism for the strong to carry the weak. They joined the nations at the wallet {pause: 0.6} but left them separate at the state. {pause: 1.0}

Which meant a country in the euro could not do the ordinary thing a country in trouble does — print its own money, let its own currency fall, cushion the blow, buy itself time. It had handed all of that away when it joined, in exchange for the shared coin and a seat at the top table. And there was no agreed way out the other side, either. No fire exit. No procedure, anywhere in the founding treaties, for a nation to leave the euro and go back to its own money. Membership was built, quite deliberately, to be a one-way door — you could come in, {pause: 0.6} but there was no way back out. {pause: 0.8}

So when the markets stopped believing in Greece, they turned, coldly and logically, to the next weakest — and the next. If Greece could go under inside the euro, why not Portugal? Why not Ireland, drowning in the debts of its own collapsed banks? {pause: 0.6} And then the truly unthinkable ones. Spain. {pause: 0.6} Italy — the third-largest economy in the eurozone, too vast to rescue, too vast to let fall. {pause: 1.0}

The borrowing costs climbed, country by country, all through 2011 and into 2012, like water rising up the side of a hull. And every summit of Europe's leaders produced statements, and half-measures, and midnight communiqués — and the water kept rising. {pause: 0.8}

Which brings us back, at last, to that room in London. {pause: 0.6} And now you understand what Mario Draghi was actually standing in front of. {pause: 1.2}

## Eight words, and the whole method inside them   {tone: dramatic, rate: slow, note: "return to Draghi with full context; this is the intellectual heart of the second act — go slow"}

Not a market wobble. The end of the thing. {pause: 0.8} By that July, serious people in serious rooms were quietly modelling the breakup of the euro — how you would even do it, what happened to the contracts, the banks, the savings of three hundred million people, on the morning the currency died. {pause: 0.8}

And into that, with no new weapon in his hands, Mario Draghi said that the bank would do whatever it takes. And that it would be enough. {pause: 1.0}

Now watch what makes it the perfect, terrible emblem for this entire series.

Think about what actually saved the euro that summer. It was not the wisdom of the peoples — they were never consulted. What saved the single currency of a continent was the *will* of one unelected official, and the market's belief that he had the power to make good on it. {pause: 1.2}

Go all the way back to the first hour of this series, to a cognac merchant's son named Jean Monnet — a man who never won an election in his life, who changed the fate of hundreds of millions of people from inside quiet rooms, one persuaded mind at a time, without ever once asking the public for permission. {pause: 0.8} I told you, back then, that his method would one day become the great uncomfortable question hanging over everything.

Here it is. {pause: 1.0} Draghi is Monnet's method, seventy years on, distilled to its absolute essence. The project saved, brilliantly, decisively, {pause: 0.6} over the heads of the people it belongs to. {pause: 1.4} {tone: grave} It worked. {pause: 0.8} It always works. {pause: 0.8} That is exactly the problem. {pause: 1.6}

## The question nobody had ever asked   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "the third collision opens; the register turns toward Britain — steadier, almost fated"}

For sixty years, through every episode of this story, one thing had never happened. {pause: 0.8}

No large nation had ever been asked, straight out, in a single plain question — *do you want to belong to this thing, yes or no, in or out* — and been allowed to give a binding answer that the project would actually honor. {pause: 0.8} They had been asked about treaties, and asked to vote again when they said no, and had integration buried inside prosperity precisely so that they would never *have* to be asked. That was the lesson these men drew from their earliest defeat, and you heard me name it in an earlier hour: {pause: 0.6} never ask the people directly. {pause: 0.6} You will lose. {pause: 1.0}

For sixty years they held to that lesson. {pause: 0.8}

And then one man broke it. {pause: 1.0} Not a revolutionary. Not an enemy of Europe. An ordinary, fluent, faintly complacent British Conservative politician named David Cameron — the Prime Minister of the United Kingdom — who broke the oldest rule in the book {pause: 0.6} for reasons that had almost nothing to do with Europe at all. {pause: 1.2}

## Cameron's gamble   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "the human anchor for the third act; sympathetic but clear-eyed about the miscalculation"}

Cameron's problem was not, in his own mind, the European Union. Cameron's problem was his own political party. {pause: 0.8}

A hard core of his own members would not stop pulling at it. He was being bled from a direction he could not defend by ordinary means. And so Cameron reached for the same instrument gamblers always reach for when they are behind and the ordinary moves have failed: {pause: 0.6} the one big throw that settles it once and for all. {pause: 0.8}

In January 2013, in a speech at the London headquarters of Bloomberg, he made a promise. Re-elect me, he said, and I will renegotiate Britain's relationship with the European Union — and then I will put it to the British people. In or out. A referendum. {pause: 0.8}

{tone: grave} He asked the plain question the whole project had spent sixty years engineering itself never to have to ask. {pause: 0.8} And he did it not out of some grand democratic conviction, {pause: 0.6} but to quiet a faction. {pause: 1.0} History does not much care about the smallness of the reason. {pause: 0.5} It only records the size of the result. {pause: 1.4}

{tone: wry} There was only one problem. {pause: 0.6} The figure was wrong. {pause: 0.8} Britain's own official statistics watchdog said so, publicly and bluntly — the real net figure, once you subtracted what came back to Britain, was closer to two hundred and fifty million, and even that was not simply going to sit in a pot for the health service. {pause: 0.8}

## The one document that meant two things   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "callback to the Luxembourg Compromise motif from ep5; earn it, don't over-explain"}
