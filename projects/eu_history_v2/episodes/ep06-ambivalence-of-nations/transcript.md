## The counting on the coast   {tone: tense, rate: slow, note: "open cold, in scene, in motion — no date, no name, no thesis; the room, the weather, the tally"}

The lamps are on in a hall near the harbor, and outside the wind is coming in off a cold sea, the way it always does this far up the coast, and inside a handful of men and women are counting paper.

They count it into piles. One pile grows and the other barely moves. Every ballot in the tall pile says the same word, and it is the same word the fisherman down the quay would give you, and the man who mends the nets, and the woman who guts the catch when the boats come in — the word is *no*. {pause: 1.0}

Somewhere to the south, in the capital, in a warm room with better light, they are counting too, and there the other pile is winning. There the word is *yes*.

And between those two rooms — the fishing town in the dark and the capital in the glow — runs a crack that is about to split a whole country cleanly down the middle, so cleanly {pause: 0.8} you could almost draw it with a ruler. {pause: 1.0}

The country is Norway. The year is 1972. And what these people are counting, up and down this coastline and across the fjords and in the streets of Oslo, is the answer to a question that in more than twenty years of building Europe has never once been put, in plain words, to a whole people. {pause: 1.0}

Do you want to join? {pause: 1.4}

## A country cut in two   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "lay the map out plainly; let the symmetry of the split do the work"}

Look at what the map showed when the counting was done. {pause: 0.6}

Oslo — the capital, the educated south, the ministries and the newspapers and the men who negotiate treaties — Oslo voted to go in. Not narrowly. Something like two out of every three of them said yes. The people closest to the machine, the people who deal in Europe for a living, wanted Europe. {pause: 0.8}

And then you travel north. Up past the cities, up the long spine of the coast, into the fishing counties at the top of the world — Nordland, Troms, Finnmark. And the numbers turn completely over. Up there it is not two-to-one for yes. It is better than two-to-one *against*. Seventy percent and more, saying no. {pause: 0.8}

One country. A near-perfect mirror. The center for, the edges against; the desk for, the boat against; the men who trade in abstractions for, and the men who pull a living out of the water {pause: 1.2} against. {pause: 1.0}

You could hang that map on a wall and read the whole tension of this series straight off it. {pause: 0.8}

## What the water was worth   {tone: somber, rate: normal, note: "give the fishermen their real reason; not sentiment, sovereignty over the sea; grave, because it's a way of life at stake"}

Because the fishermen were not voting out of some vague suspicion of foreigners. They were voting over something exact, and something theirs.

The sea. {pause: 1.0}

Norway's fishing organizations — Norges Fiskarlag, the fishermen's union; Norges Råfisklag, the body that handled the raw catch — campaigned hard for a *no*, and their argument was as concrete as a cod. If Norway joins this Community, they said, then Norway's waters stop being Norway's. The control of the richest fishing grounds in the North Atlantic gets pooled into Brussels, handed to a common policy, opened to fleets from countries a thousand miles south who have already fished their own seas half-empty.

To a family that had worked one stretch of that coast for generations, this was not a trade policy. It was a stranger walking into the room {pause: 0.8} and reaching for the one thing you owned outright. {pause: 1.0}

And notice — because this is the whole point of standing here — the argument the north was making was not stupid. It was not backward. It was a clear-eyed defense of control over the thing that fed them, made by people who understood exactly what they stood to lose. {pause: 0.8} The men in Oslo had their reasons. The men on the coast had theirs. {pause: 0.8} And the coast was not wrong to have them.

Keep that coastline in view. Because Norway is going to be asked this same question again — twenty-two years from now, in 1994 — and it is going to give the very same answer, for the very same reason, which ought to tell you something about how deep this particular no runs. {pause: 0.8}

## The man who kept his word   {tone: somber, rate: slow, note: "the human beat; Bratteli's resignation, quiet, without melodrama"}

And there is a man in Oslo that night who has staked everything on the other answer. {pause: 0.6}

His name is Trygve Bratteli. He is the Prime Minister of Norway, a Labour man, and he has thrown the full weight of his government behind the yes. He has told the country, in effect: this is where the future is, and I am leading you into it. He has tied his own standing, and his government's life, to the word coming out of the capital and not the word coming off the coast.

The coast wins. {pause: 1.2}

Fifty-three to forty-seven, in round numbers, across the whole nation — the north and the countryside outvoting the confident center. And Bratteli, having asked his people to follow him and been refused, does a thing that in our own age has come to feel almost quaint. {pause: 0.6}

He keeps his promise. He resigns. {pause: 1.2}

He does not explain that the people misunderstood. He does not commission a second referendum to correct the first. He does not announce that the result, on reflection, was advisory. He had staked his government on a *yes*, the people said *no*, {pause: 0.8} and so he goes. {pause: 1.0}

A leader stepping down because his people would not follow him into Europe. {pause: 0.8} Hold that image lightly, and carry it a while. Because by the end of this hour you are going to watch the men who build Europe get extraordinarily good at making sure they never again have to ask the public anything so dangerous. {pause: 0.8}

## The gap begins to show   {tone: measured, rate: normal, note: "step back and name the central tension of the episode; the spine of the hour"}

Now let me widen the lens, because Norway is the first clear sighting of the thing this whole episode is about. {pause: 0.6}

For twenty years, the men we have been following built Europe from the top. Schuman, Monnet, Adenauer, Hallstein, Spaak — they signed treaties, they engineered a market, they wired six economies together so tightly that war between them became not just unthinkable but mechanically impossible. And it *worked*. At their own level, the elite project was a triumph.

But the whole of it rested on a wager the founders made and mostly kept quiet about. The wager was that the peoples of Europe would come along. That prosperity would do the persuading. That if you built the market first, the loyalty would grow up quietly underneath it, and one day Europeans would simply feel European, the way they felt French or Dutch or Italian — without ever having been marched into a voting booth and asked to choose. {pause: 0.8}

Norway is the first time the wager gets tested against a whole population, in the open. {pause: 0.8} And the population says no. {pause: 1.2}

So here is the question that runs under everything from here to the end of the series, and I am not going to be able to answer it for you tonight — because I do not think the people living through it could answer it either. {pause: 0.6}

Is this ambivalence a *lag*? A temporary thing — the hearts of ordinary Europeans simply running a few years behind the paperwork, waiting for prosperity to catch them up? {pause: 0.8}

Or is it *permanent*? A real and lasting gap between what the architects want and what the peoples feel — a gap the top-down method can plaster over, and buy off, and paper across, {pause: 0.8} but never actually close? {pause: 1.0}

Lag, or feature. {pause: 1.0} Hold the two possibilities open, one in each hand, and watch the next fifteen years try to decide between them.

## The door the giant left open   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "callback to Ep5; brisk — the audience knows de Gaulle is gone"}

Because while Norway was saying no, three other countries were saying yes — and one of them had been trying to get through this door for the better part of a decade.

You will remember how the last chapter ended. Charles de Gaulle, the great mountain of a man, had blocked Britain from the Community not once but twice, and looked as though he might block her forever. And then, over a small referendum he did not need to hold, he lost, and kept an old soldier's promise, and walked away. The door he had bolted swung open the instant he let go of it.

So now the enlargement de Gaulle strangled twice at birth finally happens. On the first of January, 1973, the Community of Six becomes the Community of Nine. In come Britain, Ireland, and Denmark. Norway was supposed to make it ten — she had negotiated her entry alongside the others, the treaty was ready to sign — and then her people reached down {pause: 0.6} and tore up the ticket. {pause: 0.8}

The others go in. Norway stays on her coast. {pause: 1.0}

And for Britain, this ought to be a moment of something like joy. Twice refused, twice humiliated, finally admitted. You would expect flags. You would expect crowds. {pause: 0.6}

Let me take you to the party they threw. {pause: 0.8}

## The empty seats at Wembley   {tone: wry, rate: normal, note: "the half-empty stadium; let the deflation land as image, not lecture"}

To mark Britain joining Europe, the government put on a festival. They called it *Fanfare for Europe* — a great national celebration, days of concerts and exhibitions and events, all to warm the country to the thing its leaders had just done on its behalf. The Prime Minister who took Britain in, Edward Heath — a genuine European believer, a man who loved the Continent and its music and had wanted this for years — Heath wanted the country to *feel* it.

And so, in January of 1973, part of the fanfare was a football match at Wembley Stadium. A grand symbolic fixture. A team called "The Three" — players from the three new members — against a team called "The Six," players from the founding nations. Old Europe against new Europe, on the grass, under the lights, in the great cathedral of English football. {pause: 0.6}

Wembley held a hundred thousand people. {pause: 0.8}

About thirty-six and a half thousand turned up. {pause: 1.2}

See it from the pitch. Sixty-odd thousand empty seats, gray and vacant, climbing away into the January dusk on every side. The players go through the motions of a celebration of Europe {pause: 1.0} in a stadium nearly two-thirds empty. {pause: 0.8}

The same signal again. Not on a ballot this time, but on a terrace. {tone: measured} The leaders have delivered Europe — Heath has spent years of his life delivering it — and the people it was delivered *to* could not, in the main, be bothered to come and watch. {pause: 0.8} The gap from the Norwegian coast has followed us across the North Sea and sat itself down in the empty half of Wembley. {pause: 1.0}

## The question Britain almost couldn't ask   {tone: tense, rate: slow, note: "set up the 1975 referendum and the Labour split; build to the mechanism"}

And Britain, having only just squeezed through the door, immediately begins to wonder aloud whether it wants to be inside at all.

The problem is the governing party. Labour comes to power in 1974, and Labour is torn straight down the middle over Europe — every bit as split as that Norwegian map, only the fault line runs through a single cabinet instead of a coastline. Half the party sees the Community as a capitalist club that will tie Britain's hands. The other half sees it as the future. And the Prime Minister, Harold Wilson — the same supple, survivor's mind we watched try the door under de Gaulle — has to hold this thing together or watch his government tear apart in his hands. {pause: 0.6}

So Wilson reaches for a device. He will let the *people* settle it. Britain will hold a referendum — the first nationwide referendum in the country's history — on whether to stay in the Community it joined barely two years before. {pause: 0.8}

But that only moves the problem. Because his ministers, the men who are supposed to speak with one government voice, cannot agree even among themselves. If he orders them all to campaign for staying in, a third of his cabinet will resign and the government falls. {pause: 0.6}

So Wilson does something close to constitutionally astonishing. {pause: 1.0}

## Agreeing to disagree, in cabinet   {tone: wry, rate: normal, note: "the suspension of collective responsibility; a genuinely strange manoeuvre; then the spending gap, crisp"}

He suspends the oldest rule in British government. The rule called collective responsibility — the iron principle that the cabinet decides together and then every last minister defends that decision in public or gets out. {pause: 0.6}

Wilson simply switches it off. For the length of the referendum, he tells his ministers: you may disagree. Out loud. In public. Against your own government's position. Seven of his twenty-three cabinet ministers take him up on it and campaign for Britain to *leave* — while the government they sit in officially campaigns to stay. {pause: 0.8}

A government arguing with itself, on purpose, in front of the electorate, because it was the only way to keep from flying apart. {pause: 0.8}

And when the two sides go out to campaign, they are not evenly matched — not remotely. The campaign to stay in, "Britain in Europe," has the money. It has business behind it, and the press, and the establishment, and it outspends the other side by something like ten to one — on the order of a million and a half pounds against well under two hundred thousand for the campaign to leave. {pause: 0.6}

So the country that could barely fill a third of Wembley is now handed the question directly, with the scales tilted hard toward yes. And in June of 1975, Britain votes to stay in. Two to one. {pause: 0.8}

It looks, on paper, like enthusiasm. {tone: wry} It was closer to a well-funded shrug. {tone: measured} The people had been asked, and the people had said yes — but the yes had been bought a good deal more expensively than it had been *believed*. {pause: 1.0} File that away, because the distance between a yes you buy and a yes you win is going to matter enormously before this series is out.

## The word for a decade of disappointment   {tone: somber, rate: slow, note: "Eurosclerosis; set the mood of the 1970s, then open the contested diagnosis"}

Meanwhile, the machine everyone is arguing about joining is running badly. {pause: 0.6}

The 1970s are not kind to Europe. The great postwar boom — the miracle years that made the whole project feel like destiny — those years end, and end hard. In 1973 the oil-producing states of the Middle East turn off the taps, and the price of oil explodes, and the industrial economies of the West, built on cheap energy, go into a long, grinding slump. Unemployment climbs and stays climbed. Inflation and stagnation arrive together, which economists had half-believed was impossible. The Community that was supposed to be an engine of endless prosperity spends the decade looking tired, jammed, unable to agree on anything or move in any direction. {pause: 0.8}

And somebody gives the condition a name. A German economist coins a word, and the word sticks, because it is cruel and it is memorable. {pause: 0.8}

*Eurosclerosis*. {pause: 1.0}

Europe as an aging body. The arteries hardening. The limbs stiffening. A continent grown fat and over-regulated and slow, seizing up under the weight of its own rules and its own welfare states. That is the diagnosis, and it becomes the conventional wisdom — Europe is sick, Europe is calcified, Europe cannot move. {pause: 1.0}

## But was the patient really sick?   {tone: measured, rate: normal, note: "named uncertainty #1 — voice both readings, resolve neither"}

Except — and here is the first of two arguments in this hour that the historians and the economists are still having, so I am going to give you both sides and decline to settle it — it is not at all clear the patient was as sick as the word suggests. {pause: 0.8}

The man who coined "Eurosclerosis," and the school of thought behind him, had a particular diagnosis in mind, and it was a *political* one. Their answer was: too much government. Too much regulation, too much welfare, too many rules protecting workers and propping up firms — strangle all that red tape and the market will spring back to life. The word was not neutral. It was an argument for a smaller state, dressed up as a medical fact. {pause: 0.6}

And there are serious scholars who look at the same decade and say: not so fast. The slump of the 1970s, they argue, was not caused by European over-regulation at all — it was driven by *global* shocks that hit everyone. The oil crisis. The collapse of the postwar monetary order when America cut the dollar loose from gold. Those were world events, not European failings. And underneath the gloom, they point out, the Community was quietly *innovating* even in its supposedly sclerotic decade — building new machinery that would matter later. One economist, looking back years afterward, noted the irony that France's employment record eventually came out ahead of America's — hardly the mark of a continent that had regulated itself to death. {pause: 0.8}

So which is it? {pause: 0.8} Was Europe a genuinely arthritic system, seizing up under its own weight and badly in need of the deregulating cure that is coming? Or was "Eurosclerosis" a clever slur — a real but ordinary global recession, rebranded as a European disease in order to sell a particular politics? {pause: 0.8}

I will only say this. The label won. Whatever the truth of the diagnosis, enough powerful people came to *believe* Europe was sick that they went looking for a cure — and the cure they found is going to remake the whole Continent. Sometimes what a generation believes about itself matters more than what was true. {pause: 1.0}

## We are simply asking for our own money back   {tone: tense, rate: slow, note: "Thatcher arrives; build to the rebate war; the recurring antagonist"}

And into this tired, jammed, arguing Community walks the figure who is going to dominate its next fifteen years — and who will, decades on, end by pulling her own country back out of it altogether. {pause: 0.6}

Margaret Thatcher. {pause: 1.0}

She becomes Prime Minister of Britain in 1979, and she arrives at the European table with a grievance, and the grievance is money. Because of the peculiar way the Community's finances worked — a system built years earlier, remember, to suit French farmers, and paid for in ways that fell hard on a country like Britain that imported a great deal and had few farms to subsidize — Britain was paying far more into the common budget than it got back. One of the poorer members of the club was quietly subsidizing some of the richer ones. And Thatcher decided she would not stand for it. {pause: 0.6}

Now, there is a phrase everyone thinks they know. The story goes that she banged the table at the European summits and demanded, "I want my money back." {pause: 0.6}

She did not, in fact, put it that way — and the difference tells you something about her. {pause: 0.8} What the record actually has her saying is cooler, and lawyer-like, and in its own way harder to argue with. *We are not asking the Community or anyone else for money.* {pause: 0.6} *We are simply asking to have our own money back.* {pause: 1.0}

Hear the move in that. Not a beggar demanding a handout — a creditor demanding repayment. It is not your money that I want; it is mine, and you are holding it. That is a far more formidable thing to say across a negotiating table, and she said versions of it, coldly, relentlessly, for five straight years. {pause: 0.8}

## The handbag on the table   {tone: measured, rate: normal, note: "named uncertainty about the myth; flag it, refuse the tidy legend"}

There is another story about how she fought — the image of Thatcher slamming her handbag down on the summit table, browbeating a room of continental statesmen into submission by sheer force of personality. It has become part of the legend. The word even entered the language: to be *handbagged*. {pause: 0.6}

And I have to be honest with you: the famous handbag-on-the-table, the single dramatic moment, is very probably myth. {pause: 0.8} There is no authoritative record that settles the great confrontation the way the legend tells it. What is *not* myth is the grinding, five-year reality of it — summit after summit, Thatcher refusing to move, holding whole meetings hostage to the British rebate, wearing her partners down not with one handbag but with an infinite, exhausting supply of patience for saying no. {pause: 0.8}

And she won. {pause: 0.6} At Fontainebleau, in 1984, the Community finally gave in. Britain got a large lump sum for the year in hand — a thousand million ECU, the Community's unit of account — and, far more importantly, a permanent formula. A standing rebate, roughly two-thirds of the gap, written into the arrangements and locked so that it could not be changed without Britain's own agreement. {pause: 0.6}

She had reached into the machinery of Europe and bolted in a permanent advantage for her country that no one could remove without her say-so. It was, on its own terms, a total victory. {pause: 0.8}

But look at what kind of victory it was. {pause: 0.8} It was not a vision of Europe. It was a bill successfully disputed. Thatcher's great European achievement, in these years, was to pay less. The other leaders around that table were arguing about what Europe should *become*. She was arguing about the invoice. {pause: 1.0} And the day she finished winning that argument was the day she was free to start a much larger one — the one that ends this episode.

## We belong to the West   {tone: reverent, rate: slow, note: "southern enlargement; Greece and the democracies; give it real weight — this is rescue, not commerce"}

But before we get to that, the club grows again — and this time the new members are not rich northern democracies looking for a bigger market. This time, joining Europe means something closer to being *rescued*. {pause: 0.6}

Look at the map of southern Europe in the middle of the 1970s. Three countries there had spent much of the twentieth century under dictatorship. Greece had been ruled, until 1974, by a junta of colonels. Portugal had thrown off a decades-old authoritarian regime in a revolution in 1974. Spain had lived under Franco until his death in 1975. Three young, fragile democracies, blinking in the light, none of them certain to survive — because a new democracy can always slide back into the darkness it came from. {pause: 1.0}

And for these countries, the Community became something the founders had not quite designed it to be. It became an anchor. A way of nailing the new democracy to the mast so it could not drift back. {pause: 0.6} If you were bound into Europe — into its rules, its market, its club of democracies — then the generals could not easily come back, because coming back would mean tearing the whole country out of the Western world. {pause: 0.6}

The Greek statesman who led his country back from the junta and into Europe, Constantine Karamanlis, put the instinct in four plain words. *We belong to the West.* {pause: 1.0} Not "this will be good for our exports." *We belong.* Joining Europe was, for Greece, a declaration of where a country's soul lived — and a way of putting the new democracy somewhere the generals could no longer reach it. {pause: 1.0}

Greece goes in first, in 1981. {pause: 0.6} And then, almost immediately, Greece does something that ought to make you smile, because it is a perfect little lesson in what this club actually is once you are inside it.

## Greece vetoes its own neighbors   {tone: wry, rate: normal, note: "the veto gambit; the club as a machine for extracting money; keep it light and sharp"}

The moment Greece is a member, Spain and Portugal come knocking to join too. And Greece — a newcomer itself, barely a year inside — realizes it is holding something valuable. {pause: 0.6}

A veto. {pause: 0.8}

Because new members are admitted only by the agreement of *all* the existing ones, and Greece is now one of the existing ones. So Greece turns to its partners and says, in effect: I will block Spain and Portugal from joining — I will veto my fellow struggling southern democracies — unless you first do something for *me*. Specifically, unless you set up a large new programme of aid for the Mediterranean regions, Greece very much included. {pause: 0.6}

And it works. The gambit wins Greece something on the order of two billion dollars in what get called the Integrated Mediterranean Programmes — real money, poured into Greek development — and *then*, having been paid, Greece drops its objection and lets its neighbors in. {pause: 0.8}

Look at what has just happened, because it is the club in miniature. {tone: measured} A country that joined Europe as an act of belonging — *we belong to the West*, driven by soul and survival — turned around within a year and used its brand-new membership as a lever to shake loose two billion dollars. {pause: 0.8} Idealism at the front door, hard bargaining the moment you are inside — both at once, and by now entirely ordinary. {pause: 0.6} That is what the Community had become: a place where the noblest motives and the coldest arithmetic sat down at the same table and shook hands. {pause: 1.0}

## The fleet that froze the talks   {tone: matter-of-fact, rate: normal, note: "Spain's fishing fleet; a single fact; then the flag-raising with no ceremony"}

And when Spain finally does get to negotiate its way in, it turns out to be carrying a problem of almost comic size. A problem shaped, once again, like fish. {pause: 0.6}

Because when officials sat down to work out how Spain would fit into the Community's fishing arrangements, they ran straight into a single, staggering fact. {pause: 0.8} Spain's fishing fleet, all on its own, was *larger* than the fleets of every existing member of the Community combined. {pause: 1.0}

Every existing member. Put together. And Spain's fleet was bigger than the whole lot. {pause: 0.8}

That one number froze the fisheries negotiations for years. Because letting a fleet that size loose into the Community's already-quarreled-over waters — the very waters, remember, that Norway's fishermen had refused to share at any price — was a nightmare no one could quickly solve. The fish, again. Always the fish. The most concrete thing in the sea, jamming the most abstract project on land. {pause: 0.8}

But eventually it gets done, and in 1986 Spain and Portugal join, and the Community of Ten becomes the Community of Twelve. Two more young democracies bolted safely to the Western mast. {pause: 0.6}

And here is the small, telling detail of how it happened. This enlargement — the completion of Europe's embrace of its recovered democracies, a genuinely historic thing — took place, in the end, without any official ceremony at all. {pause: 0.8} No grand hall. No frescoes. No solemn signing beneath a painted ceiling, the way it had been at Rome. Just the raising of the Spanish and the Portuguese flags {pause: 0.6} over Brussels. {pause: 1.0}

The Community had grown up. It no longer bothered with the theater. It just added the flags {pause: 0.6} and went back to work. {pause: 0.8}

## The Frenchman who came to a deathbed   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "Delors arrives; the anchor of the episode; the institution many think is dying"}

So take stock of where we are by the middle of the 1980s. {pause: 0.6}

The Community has grown from six members to twelve. But it is also, by common consent, a bit of a wreck. Sclerotic or not, it *feels* stuck. Fifteen years of oil shocks and stagnation and Thatcher fighting over the invoice have left it looking less like a rising power and more like a large, quarrelsome committee that argues about money and never actually goes anywhere. A great many serious people, in the mid-1980s, look at the European project and quietly conclude that it has run out of road. That the dream is over, and what is left is bookkeeping. {pause: 1.0}

And into that mood — into an institution half its own members privately believe is dying — walks a man who is about to prove them all spectacularly wrong. {pause: 0.6}

His name is Jacques Delors. {pause: 1.0}

A Frenchman. A Socialist. A former finance minister, a serious Catholic, a man of the left who worked through problems with an accountant's precision and waited them out with something close to a priest's patience. In 1985 he becomes President of the European Commission — the same office Walter Hallstein once held and ran like the prime minister of a country that did not exist. And Delors surveys the sick, stalled Community he has inherited, and instead of managing its decline, he decides to relaunch the whole thing. {pause: 0.6}

And the way he does it is the reason he is the center of this hour. Because Delors does not offer Europe one big idea. He offers it *two* — two cures at once, deliberately, one aimed at each half of the political battlefield. {pause: 0.8}

## Two cures in one prescription   {tone: measured, rate: normal, note: "Delors's double strategy — the market for the right, the social dimension for the left"}

To the businessmen, and to the right, and above all to a Margaret Thatcher who believed in nothing so much as free markets, Delors offered the first cure. {pause: 0.6}

A single market. {pause: 0.8} The idea was simple to say and enormous to do. The Community was supposedly one market already — but in truth it was still riddled with invisible walls. A lorry crossing from France into Germany still stopped at the border for paperwork and inspections. A product legal to sell in one country had to be tested all over again to be sold in the next. A bank or an insurer licensed in one member state could not simply operate in another. Twelve national markets that were a single market only on the letterhead. {pause: 0.6}

Delors proposed to tear all of that out. To build, by a fixed deadline, a genuine single market — one space in which goods and people and money and services moved as freely from Lisbon to Copenhagen as they did within any one country. He gave it a name that was really a promise with a date attached. {pause: 0.6} *1992*. The year it would all be finished. {pause: 0.8}

And to the right, this was irresistible. It was deregulation on a continental scale. It was capitalism with the borders knocked down. Thatcher, who loathed most of what Brussels stood for, looked at the single market and saw her own philosophy written across a continent — and she backed it. {pause: 0.6}

But Delors was a man of the *left*. And he had a second cure, aimed at the workers and the unions and everyone who feared that a single market would simply become a race to the bottom — a great continental bonfire of wages and rights and protections, with every country undercutting the next. For them, Delors offered what he called the *social dimension*. The promise that alongside the free market for business there would be a floor for labour — European guarantees, European rights, a Europe that protected the worker as well as the trader. {pause: 0.8}

Two cures. The market for the right; the social dimension for the left. One prescription written to be swallowed by both sides of an argument that had been poisoning Europe for a decade. {pause: 0.6} It was, as political engineering, close to genius. {pause: 0.8} And, as we will see, it contained the seed of a war.

## The man who went native   {tone: wry, rate: slow, note: "Cockfield's White Paper; Thatcher's man who betrayed her; the SEA"}

Now, to build the single market you needed more than a slogan and a date. You needed a plan detailed enough that no government could wriggle out of it. And here the story hands us one of its quiet ironies. {pause: 0.6}

The man who wrote that plan was Thatcher's own. {pause: 0.8}

His name was Arthur Cockfield, and Thatcher had sent him to Brussels as her Commissioner precisely because she trusted him — a reliable, hard-headed ally who would keep an eye on the Continentals and defend British interests inside the machine. She sent a watchdog. {pause: 0.8}

And Cockfield got to Brussels, looked at the problem of the single market, and fell completely in love with it. He threw himself into the work with the zeal of a convert. In June of 1985 he produced a White Paper — a forensic document listing some three hundred specific measures, every last barrier that would have to come down to make the single market real, each one itemized and scheduled. It was so comprehensive that it became almost impossible to block: you cannot object to "the single market" in the abstract when what sits in front of you is three hundred concrete, reasonable, technical steps. {pause: 0.8}

It was a masterpiece of the old founders' method — build the finished, detailed thing, and let its sheer completeness drag everyone along. And Thatcher's watchdog had built it. {pause: 0.6} She felt, not unreasonably, betrayed. In the phrase people used about him, Cockfield had *gone native* — sent to guard British interests in Brussels and captured, instead, by the mission of Brussels itself. {pause: 0.8}

And to give this single market legal teeth, the Community passed its first big revision of the founding treaties in nearly thirty years — the Single European Act. It set the 1992 deadline in law. And, crucially, to stop any one country from vetoing the hundreds of measures one by one, it expanded majority voting — the very thing de Gaulle had frozen with his two-faced sheet of paper twenty years before. {pause: 0.6} The engine the General had jammed {pause: 0.8} was starting to turn over again. {pause: 1.0}

## The afternoon he converted the enemy   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "build to Delors at the TUC; the standing ovation; Ron Todd's line — a genuine peak. Let tone warm, then lift at the ovation"}

And now we arrive at the moment that shows you both things together — how real Delors's achievement was, and how brittle. {pause: 0.6}

The place is Bournemouth, an English seaside town, in September of 1988. The occasion is the annual congress of the Trades Union Congress — the TUC, the great gathering of the British trade unions. {pause: 0.6}

You have to understand who these people were and how they felt about Europe. The British unions had, for years, been deeply *hostile* to the Community. To them it was exactly the thing Delors's left-wing critics feared — a bosses' club, a capitalist market designed to weaken workers, hollow out British industry, and drive down wages. If you had wanted to find the most Eurosceptic room in Britain, a room full of union men, {pause: 0.8} you had found it. {pause: 0.8}

And into that room walks Jacques Delors. A French Socialist. The President of the European Commission. To make the case for Europe. {pause: 0.6}

And he does not make it with the market. He makes it with the *second* cure. He stands in front of that suspicious, hostile hall of British trade unionists and he speaks to them about the *social dimension* — about a Europe that would guarantee workers' rights, that would protect them, that would put a floor under them that their own government, under Margaret Thatcher, was busy tearing away. He tells them, in effect: Europe is not your enemy. Europe is your ally. The protections you are losing at home, you can win back {pause: 0.8} in Brussels. {pause: 0.8}

{tone: warm} And the most Eurosceptic room in Britain {pause: 0.8} rises to its feet and gives the French Socialist a standing ovation. {pause: 1.2}

Weigh what has just happened. A French Eurocrat, in a single afternoon, in front of the hardest anti-European audience in the country, has turned the British labour movement toward Europe. He has taken people who saw Brussels as the enemy and sent them home seeing it as their last, best hope. {pause: 0.6} The engineered consensus — the two-cure prescription — is working *magnificently*. ## Bruges   {tone: dramatic, rate: slow, note: "Thatcher's answer; the fuse for Episode 8; deliver the quote deliberately"}

Twelve days after Delors won that standing ovation in Bournemouth, Margaret Thatcher travelled to the Belgian town of Bruges to give a speech. {pause: 0.6}

She had watched it all. She had watched the market she liked start to grow the social dimension she despised. She had watched her own man Cockfield go native. She had watched majority voting come creeping back. And now she had watched a French Socialist stand up in *England* and seduce the British trade unions with the promise of a Europe that would overrule her own government. {pause: 0.6}

And in Bruges, on the twentieth of September, 1988, she answered. {pause: 1.0}

She did not attack the market — she still wanted the market. What she attacked was the *state* growing up around it. The thing Delors was really building. And she said it in a sentence that would echo through British politics for the next thirty years, so let me give it to you slowly, in her own words. {pause: 0.6}

{rate: very-slow} *We have not successfully rolled back the frontiers of the state in Britain,* {pause: 0.5} *only to see them re-imposed at a European level,* {pause: 0.5} *with a European super-state exercising a new dominance from Brussels.* {pause: 1.4}

{rate: slow} There it is. The whole coming war, in one breath. She had spent her career shrinking the state at home — and now she looked at Brussels and saw a *new* state, a bigger one, being assembled over her head to undo everything she had done. Delors had sold Europe as protection. Thatcher named it domination. And she drew a line, on behalf of the nation, against the super-state. {pause: 0.8}

The reaction told you how deep the wound went. Delors refused to attend. And Edward Heath — the man who had taken Britain in, who had thrown the half-empty party at Wembley, a member of Thatcher's *own party* — was so appalled that he travelled to Brussels to rebut a sitting Prime Minister of his own side. ## An engineered consensus, and how brittle   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "draw the two moments together — the ovation and the speech — into the episode's thesis"}

So set those two moments side by side, twelve days apart, and look at what they tell you together — because between them they hold the entire argument of this hour. {pause: 0.6}

On the eighth of September, Jacques Delors converts the most hostile audience in Britain in a single afternoon. That is the lesson of the whole episode, and let me put it as sharply as I can. {pause: 0.6} The engineered consensus is *real* — and it is *brittle*. It is agreement built from the top, welded together by clever men offering clever bargains — and underneath it the old loyalties, the old suspicions, the fisherman's feeling for his own water and the nation's feeling for its own sovereignty, are all still there. Delors could win the room. {pause: 0.8} He could not, it turns out, close the gap. {pause: 1.0}

But all of this — the ovation and the speech, Delors's triumph and Thatcher's declaration of war — {pause: 0.6} all of it, so far, is still only *argument*. Words in halls. A quarrel about how much Europe there ought to be, fought out in speeches and summits and rebate negotiations. {pause: 0.8}
