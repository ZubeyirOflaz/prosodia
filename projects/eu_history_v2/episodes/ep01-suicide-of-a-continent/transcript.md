## The ground that is still forbidden   {tone: somber, rate: very-slow, note: "hushed, almost reverent; let each fact land in its own silence"}

I want to start not with a date, and not with a treaty, but with a piece of ground.

It sits in the northeast of France, above a river called the Meuse. If you drove there today, you would pass through ordinary French countryside — villages, fields, the sort of place where nothing has happened for a very long time. And then you would reach a line. A boundary. Fences, and signs, and forest that has been allowed to grow wild for over a hundred years.

They call it the *Zone Rouge*. The Red Zone.

It is land that has been sealed off — closed to farming, closed to building, in places closed to walking at all — for more than a century. Because the soil is still full of steel, and poison, {pause: 1.0} and the dead. Millions of shells that never exploded lie under those trees, some of them filled with gas that is still lethal. Bomb-disposal teams still work that ground, year after year, and they expect to be working it for centuries more. And mixed into that earth, never recovered, {pause: 1.2} are the bones of men. {pause: 1.4}

This is not a legend. This is a place you could stand outside of, right now, in the twenty-first century, and be told: *you cannot go in there. It is not safe. It will not be safe in your lifetime, or your children's.*

So here is the question I want to hang over everything that follows. {pause: 0.8}

What kind of civilization does *this* to itself? {pause: 1.6} And then — this is the part that should keep you awake — {tone: grave} what kind of civilization does it, looks at the result, {pause: 1.2} and does it *again* twenty years later?

## Ten months over nothing   {tone: grave, rate: slow}

The name of that ground is Verdun.

In February 1916, the German army launched an offensive there against the French. And what happened over the next ten months is one of those events that the human mind slides off of, because it refuses to make sense.

For ten months, two of the most advanced nations on Earth — nations of Goethe and Descartes, of cathedrals and universities and railway timetables — fed their young men into a few square miles of mud and fire. When it was over, {pause: 1.0} something on the order of three hundred thousand men were dead. {pause: 1.4} Others put the total casualties far higher.

And here is the detail that turns the stomach. {tone: tense, rate: very-slow} For all of that — for all those hundreds of thousands of lives — the front line had barely *moved*. The whole point of Verdun, on the German side, was never really to take ground. It was, in the cold phrase of the time, to *bleed France white*. To make the killing itself the objective. To turn a battlefield into a machine {pause: 1.0} whose only product was corpses.

{tone: grave} Think about what it means that serious, intelligent, educated men sat in headquarters {pause: 1.2} and planned it that way. {pause: 1.4}

That is the ground that is still forbidden. That is why the Red Zone exists. Not because of one bad day — because of ten months of days, so saturated with metal and death that the earth itself never recovered.

Hold Verdun in your mind. We are going to come back to it — at the very end of this whole series, in fact, years and eight episodes from now. Because everything that these people are going to try to build, they build in the shadow of that poisoned ground.

## The war to end all wars   {tone: somber, rate: slow}

Verdun was one battle in a war we now call the First World War. And the phrase people used for it at the time — with total sincerity — was *the war to end all wars*.

Sit with that phrase for a moment, because it is going to become one of the cruelest sentences of the century. {pause: 0.8}

They believed it. Not all of them, but many of them, genuinely believed that the sheer scale of the horror they had just lived through had settled something. That no one — no general, no emperor, no parliament — could ever again look at the cost and choose it. That humanity had finally received a lesson violent enough to be permanent.

You can understand why they thought so. An entire generation of young men across the continent {pause: 1.0} had simply been deleted. There are villages in France and Britain and Germany where the war memorial carries so many names, from so few families, that you understand instantly why people spoke of a *lost generation* — {pause: 1.2} not as poetry, but as *arithmetic*.

And when the guns finally stopped, on the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month of 1918, the survivors had to do the thing that always comes after a war. They had to build the peace.

And this is where our real story begins. {tone: grave, rate: very-slow} Because the peace they built after the war to end all wars — {pause: 1.0} became the thing that guaranteed the *next* one.

## A peace that carried its own poison   {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "the historian's cool, connecting the dots the people alive then could not yet see"}

The victors gathered at Versailles, outside Paris, in 1919.

Now, I'm not going to walk you through every clause of that treaty, because we could spend the whole hour there and never reach the point. I want you to feel just one thing about it.

The treaty contained a clause that came to be known as the *war-guilt* clause. In it, Germany was required to accept responsibility for causing the war — and, flowing from that, to pay. To pay reparations of a size that Germans experienced not as a bill, but as a humiliation with no bottom to it. A punishment designed to be *felt*.

Now, whether the treaty was truly as harsh as the German public believed, or whether it was — in the historians' phrase — too harsh to be forgiven and too soft to be effective, people will argue forever. But that argument almost misses the point. Because what mattered was not the treaty's precise weight in gold. What mattered was the *feeling* it planted. A feeling, in the heart of a defeated, proud, wounded nation, that it had been robbed and shamed — {pause: 1.0} and that someday, somehow, the account would be settled.

You can already hear it, can't you. {tone: tense} From where we sit, knowing what's coming, we can hear the next war {pause: 1.0} being loaded into the chamber of the peace that was supposed to end all wars. {pause: 1.2}

But — and this is the thing I most want you to hold onto — {tone: measured} the people alive then could *not* all hear it. Most of them were exhausted, and grieving, and desperate to believe it was finally done. To feel the danger, in 1919, you had to be a particular kind of person. You had to be willing to be unpopular. You had to be willing to sound like a crank.

And the strange truth of this whole story is that the people who *did* see it coming — the ones who were right — {tone: wistful} got almost nothing for being right. Being right in advance, in this story, buys you nothing. {pause: 1.2} Remember that. It's going to happen again and again.

## The count who should not have existed   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "lean into the strangeness; this is a genuinely improbable human being"}

So let me introduce you to one of those cranks.

His name is Richard von Coudenhove-Kalergi, and he is one of the more improbable human beings ever to walk into history.

{tone: warm} His father was an Austrian count and diplomat, a European aristocrat of the old school. And the story his family told — the story he grew up on — is that this Austrian count, traveling in Japan, was on horseback when something happened, and a young Japanese woman named Mitsuko Aoyama helped him. Helped him up. He married her. And their son — this half-Austrian, half-Japanese count, born of a meeting between two worlds {pause: 1.0} that were not supposed to meet — is our crank.

{tone: measured} Think about what that heritage *meant* in the Europe of the 1920s, an age drunk on the poison of racial nationalism, an age obsessed with blood and purity and belonging. Here is a man who is, in his own person, {pause: 1.0} living proof that the neat little boxes were nonsense.

And in 1923, at the age of just twenty-nine, he publishes a book. He calls it *Pan-Europa*.

Its argument is simple, and for its time it is almost insane. {tone: dramatic} He says: the nation-states of Europe, these proud separate countries, are the *disease*. They are what did Verdun. Left to themselves they will do it again, and again, until the continent is a graveyard. The only cure is to bind them together — into one Europe.

{tone: measured} He is not a man with an army, or a party, or a treasury. He is a man with an idea and an astonishing address book. He pulls in some of the most famous minds of the age — he draws the interest of Einstein, of Freud. He builds a movement of the mind.

And how does the rising tide of European nationalism answer him? There is a man in Germany, at this time, writing a book of his own. And in it, that man reaches for a phrase to dismiss Coudenhove-Kalergi. He calls him — and I'm cleaning this up only slightly — a *cosmopolitan bastard*.

Now, here is the wry, terrible joke of it. {tone: wry} As an *insult*, it fails completely. Because as a plain description of the man's heritage — a mixed-blood aristocrat with no single national home — it is simply, factually, *accurate*. The man who wrote it thought he was landing a blow. {pause: 1.0} He was writing a caption. {pause: 1.4}

{tone: grave, rate: very-slow} The man writing that book {pause: 1.0} was named Adolf Hitler.

## Twenty-six delegations, and not one inch   {tone: measured, rate: slow}

So the dream had a prophet. Now watch it get its one real chance in the sunlight — and watch exactly how it dies.

In September 1929, in Geneva, at the League of Nations — the great international body that itself grew out of the hope that talking could replace killing — the foreign minister of France stood up to speak. His name was Aristide Briand.

And Briand did something remarkable. He stood before the assembled nations of Europe and proposed, out loud, a kind of *federal link* between them. A European union — not a fantasy in a young count's book, but a proposal, on the floor, from the foreign minister of a great power.

And the response? {pause: 0.6} They *applauded*.

Twenty-six European delegations applauded the idea. It went to a formal enquiry. Sixty-eight ministers attended six sessions to study it. On paper, the machinery of a united Europe was actually turning.

And now I want you to watch very, very carefully, because what happens next is the pattern that defines this entire episode. It is the beating heart of the tragedy. {pause: 1.4}

{tone: grave, rate: very-slow} They applauded — {pause: 1.2} and they built *nothing*.

{tone: measured, rate: slow} Every government in that room could stand and clap for the *idea* of a united Europe. And not one of them — not one — would surrender a single inch of its own sovereignty to actually make it real. They loved the destination. {pause: 1.0} Not one of them would take a step toward it.

There is the gap. Universal agreement in principle. Total paralysis in practice. Everyone wants the peace; no one will pay the only price that buys it, {pause: 1.0} which is a piece of themselves.

And Briand? {tone: wistful} Aristide Briand died in March 1932. And when he died, his great plan for a European union was still sitting exactly where he had left it. On the table. Discussed. Applauded. Studied. {pause: 1.0} Never built.

He was right. {pause: 0.8} It bought him nothing.

## Don't take any books with you   {tone: warm, rate: slow, note: "a change of register; almost intimate, a story told across a table"}

Now let me change the temperature for a moment. Because I have been telling you about ideas and treaties and men who failed. I want to introduce you to a different kind of man — and I want to be careful, at the start, about *how* I introduce him. Because he is going to matter more than anyone else in this whole series, and it would be easy to make him sound like a hero. He is not a hero. He is a *method*. Hold that distinction; it's the key to the man.

His name is Jean Monnet.

And on paper, he is almost a joke as a candidate for father of a continent. He is not a general. He is not a statesman. He never won an election in his life — not one. He never even went to university. He is the son of a cognac merchant, from the town of Cognac itself, in the southwest of France.

And there is a story he told about his father — a small thing, but it explains the whole shape of the man. When young Monnet was sent off to do business, out into the world, his father gave him this advice. He said: *"Don't take any books with you. No one can think for you. Look out the window, talk to people."* {pause: 1.2}

Look out the window. Talk to people. {pause: 1.0}

That is not the philosophy of a man who writes manifestos, like our count. It is not the philosophy of a man who gives speeches to twenty-six delegations, like Briand. It is the philosophy of a man who gets things done in *rooms* — over dinner, one persuaded mind at a time, by talking to the person across the table until they come around. Monnet's whole life, everything he ever achieved, he achieved not by publishing and not by campaigning, but by sitting with the right person and *convincing* them.

Keep that in your mind. This is a man who will change the fate of hundreds of millions of people, and he will do almost all of it in private, in rooms, without ever asking the public for permission. Later in this series, that is going to become the great, uncomfortable question hanging over everything. But we're not there yet.

## Twenty-six years old, and unfit to fight   {tone: measured, rate: slow}

Here is where Monnet enters the machinery of history, and it tells you everything.

It's 1914. The First World War has just begun — the one that will produce Verdun. Monnet is twenty-six years old. And he is medically unfit to fight; his health keeps him out of the trenches.

Now, a different young man, kept from the front, might have felt useless. Monnet does something else. He *looks out the window*. And what he sees is this: France and Britain are allies, fighting the same enemy — and they are running their war efforts *separately*. Two nations, buying ships, buying supplies, competing against each other in the same markets, bidding up their own prices, wasting what they cannot afford to waste.

And this young man — no rank, no title, twenty-six years old, unfit even to carry a rifle — decides that the answer is obvious, and that someone in charge needs to hear it.

So he *talks his way in* to see the Prime Minister of France, a man named Viviani. Understand what that takes. A nobody, a cognac merchant's son, {pause: 1.0} arranges to sit in front of the head of the French government in the middle of a world war. And he makes his case: stop competing. *Pool* the shipping. Combine the Allied resources and manage them together, as one.

And the astonishing thing is — {pause: 1.0} it worked. What grew out of that became the Inter-Allied bodies that coordinated Allied shipping and supply. You can make a strong case that this — this thing a twenty-six-year-old talked his way into — was one of the first real experiments in nations pooling their power together, managing shared resources through a common body instead of fighting over them.

And notice the *shape* of it. He didn't win an argument in public. He didn't pass a law. He found the one man who could say yes, sat in front of him, and convinced him.

There it is already, in 1914. The whole Monnet method, fully formed, in a man not yet thirty. The belief underneath it — the thing he already knows that almost no one else does: {tone: reverent, rate: slow} {pause: 1.0} *states acting together can do what states acting separately cannot.*

{tone: measured} Everything else in this series flows from that one sentence. But — and this is crucial — in 1914, and in the 1920s, and in the 1930s, it is just a technique for running a war more efficiently. It is not yet a plan to save a continent. It is a tool, lying on the table, {pause: 1.0} waiting for a catastrophe big enough to make anyone reach for it.

## The lesson that would not take   {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "the hinge of the episode; the horror returns, on schedule"}

So we have the ground that is forbidden. We have the peace that carried its own poison. We have the dreamers who were applauded and ignored. And we have our strange, indispensable man, with his method sitting unused.

Now the machine that the peace of Versailles had loaded — {tone: tense} fires. {pause: 1.2}

{tone: grave} I'm not going to narrate the rise of Hitler in detail; you know its outline, and this series has other places to sit with it. What I need you to feel is the sheer, grinding *return* of it. The war to end all wars had ended in 1918. And barely twenty years later, the continent went back into the fire.

Go back to that Frenchman I want you to imagine — a man born around 1900. By the time he is a small child, he has already lived through the shadow of the war of 1870, when Germany first invaded and humiliated France. Then, as a young man, he lives through the First World War — through Verdun. And then, in 1940, middle-aged now, he watches German armies pour across the French border {pause: 1.0} *again*.

Three times. {pause: 1.0} Three German invasions, in a single human lifetime. {pause: 1.2} 1870. 1914. 1940.

Now do you understand the fear? This is not paranoia. This is *pattern recognition*. For a Frenchman of that generation, a German invasion is not a catastrophe that might happen. It is a thing that happens roughly once a generation, like a flood that comes back. {pause: 1.0} The lesson of the war to end all wars had not taken. It had not taken at all. {pause: 1.2}

{rate: very-slow} And this time, the flood was worse than anything the human race had ever built.

## The dead we cannot count   {tone: grave, rate: very-slow, note: "the moral center of the hour; slow, plain, unflinching. The inability to count is itself the point."}

I have to try, now, to tell you the size of the Second World War. And I have to warn you, before I do, that I am going to *fail* — and that my failure is not sloppiness. It is the truth.

Because we cannot count the dead. {pause: 1.6}

Not "we haven't gotten around to it." Not "the records are messy." We *cannot* count them. The scale is such, and the chaos was such, that the most careful historians alive can only hand you {pause: 1.0} a *range*.

Worldwide, the estimates for the total dead of the Second World War run somewhere between seventy and eighty-five million human beings. {pause: 1.2} In Europe alone, the figure is something like thirty-five to forty million. And the Soviet Union by itself — one country — {pause: 1.0} may have lost on the order of twenty-seven million people. And even that staggering number is disputed; Soviet and Western figures diverge, and the true count is lost inside the chaos of the Eastern Front and the deliberate murders that were never recorded at all.

Now I want to stop on this, because it would be easy to slide past those numbers the way we slide past Verdun.

We are not talking about a rounding error of a few thousand. We are talking about *tens of millions of human beings* — the gap between the low estimate and the high estimate is itself larger than the entire population of many countries. There are that many people about whom we cannot even say, for certain, *that they lived and died*.

Think about what that requires. To lose count of a person is a small horror. To lose count of *millions* of them — {pause: 1.2} to have a death toll with a margin of error measured in the population of nations — that tells you something about the machine that produced them. {pause: 1.4}

The inability to count the dead is not a footnote to the horror. {pause: 1.0} It *is* the horror. A civilization that kills on a scale it can no longer measure has done something to itself that no treaty, no reparations clause, no applause in Geneva was ever going to fix.

This is the bottom of the pit. This is the thing the whole rest of this series is a response to. Everything those men are going to build — every quota and treaty and shared currency that voters will one day resent — they build it because they stood at the edge of *this*, and looked in.

## Do you want to marry Lebrun to King George?   {tone: tense, rate: normal, note: "a genuine peak; a hinge of history decided in a few frantic hours"}

Let me take you inside one of those frantic hours, because it shows you something no one expected — how *far* people were willing to go, once the abyss was truly open in front of them.

It is June 1940. France is falling. {tone: urgent} The German army is tearing through the country and the French government is disintegrating in real time. This is not a debate about the future of Europe. This is a fire, and the house is France, and it is burning down around the people in the room.

{tone: tense} And in that emergency, an idea surfaces that is so radical it still barely seems real. On the sixteenth of June, 1940, the British and French governments seriously proposed — and I want to be exact about this — a complete *union* between France and Britain. Not an alliance. Not a treaty of cooperation. A *merger*. {pause: 1.2} One shared citizenship, so that every French citizen would become British and every British citizen French. A single joint war cabinet. Two nations becoming, in the space of a document, {pause: 1.0} one.

Jean Monnet — our man — was one of the hands behind this. Of course he was. Nations pooling everything to do together what they cannot do apart, in the teeth of catastrophe: this is his whole method, taken to its absolute limit.

And now watch two men react, because their two reactions are the entire drama of this series in miniature.

{rate: slow} There is a French general in London, a stiff, proud, immovable man named Charles de Gaulle. He is shown this plan for a Franco-British union. And de Gaulle — dry as bone — turns to Monnet and asks him, in effect: {tone: wry} *"Monsieur Monnet, do you want to marry President Lebrun to King George the Sixth?"* {pause: 1.0}

{tone: measured} It is a joke, and it is not a joke. It is de Gaulle saying: nations are not corporations, Monsieur Monnet. You cannot simply *merge* France. France is not a company. It is a soul, a history, a thing that cannot be dissolved into another by signing a paper.

Remember de Gaulle standing there, saying that. Remember it, because roughly twenty years from now, in this series, that same man is going to walk back onto this stage and try to break the whole European project precisely because he still believes it. Some threads run the entire length of this story.

And yet — here is the extraordinary thing — even de Gaulle, even *he*, agreed the plan was worth carrying to Churchill, for the sheer morale of it, for what it might do to steady a collapsing ally.

## Two days   {tone: grave, rate: slow}

So how did it end, this astonishing gamble — two great nations proposing to become one, overnight, to hold the line against Hitler?

It didn't. {pause: 1.0}

On the French side, the men now taking control — Marshal Pétain, General Weygand, the old soldiers preparing to make their peace with Germany — looked at this proposed union with Britain and saw not salvation but *surrender*. Surrender to the wrong enemy. They called it, in effect, being made a slave of England. They would rather deal with Hitler {pause: 1.0} than merge with London.

And so the most audacious act of European unity anyone had yet proposed simply — {rate: very-slow} evaporated. {rate: slow} Within *two days*, France signed an armistice with Nazi Germany. {pause: 1.2} Two days.

Sit with how close that was, and how completely it failed. In a single week in June 1940, Europe swung from the boldest union ever proposed {pause: 1.0} to one of its proudest nations lying down before the swastika.

There is the pattern again, in its cruelest form yet. When the crisis is total, the boldest ideas become briefly, genuinely possible. And then the moment passes, and old fears and old prides slam the door. The union was on the table for a heartbeat. Then it was gone, and the tanks rolled on.

## The lock   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "planting a seed the listener won't see pay off until episode two; deliberate, quiet"}

Now I need you to hold one more thing before we reach the end — something small, and specific, and easy to overlook. But it is the *lock* on which this entire series turns, so let me set it down carefully.

There is a region in the west of Germany. It is called the Ruhr.

The Ruhr is not a battlefield. There is no great massacre there. It is something quieter and, in a sense, more important. The Ruhr is the industrial heart of Germany — its coal, its steel, its factories. It is the *forge*. And here is the thing you have to understand about coal and steel in this era: they are not just commodities. {pause: 1.0} They are *war itself*. {tone: grave} You cannot build tanks, or battleships, or shells, or the guns that fired at Verdun, without coal and steel. The Ruhr is the place where the raw earth becomes the machinery of death.

And it had done it *twice* now. The Ruhr fed the German war machine in the First World War, and it fed it again in the Second.

So look at how the French came to think of it. In their own internal documents, the Ruhr appears as something almost mythic — described as a symbol of German power, and a source of French humiliation. Read that again. A symbol of German power. A source of French humiliation. This is not economics. {pause: 1.0} This is a *wound*. The Ruhr is the thing that keeps rebuilding the army that keeps invading France.

{tone: measured} I want you to *plant* the Ruhr in your mind right now and leave it there. Because everything I've told you tonight — Verdun, the poison in the peace, the dreamers ignored, the uncountable dead — all of it is going to come down, in the next episode, to a single practical question. {pause: 1.0} What on earth do you *do* about the Ruhr?

Chain it up? Smash it? Occupy it forever? Every answer anyone tried in the past just planted the next war. The Ruhr is the lock. And somewhere out there, our cognac merchant's son is going to figure out how to *pick* it. But that — that is a story for next time.

## What serious people were prepared to do   {tone: grave, rate: slow}

Before we reach the ruin itself, one last glimpse — a dark one — of just how desperate the thinking had become, even among the winners.

By 1944, the Allies could see they were going to win. And so they had to answer the oldest question in this whole story, the question that had defeated everyone since 1919: *what do you do with Germany, so that it never does this a fourth time?*

And one of the most serious answers on the table was breathtaking. It was called the Morgenthau Plan, after the American Treasury Secretary who championed it. And its logic was brutally simple: {pause: 1.0} if German industry — the Ruhr, the coal, the steel — is what keeps producing these wars, then *destroy the industry*. Not the army. The industry itself. De-industrialize Germany altogether. Turn one of the most advanced manufacturing nations on Earth back into a pastoral, agricultural country. Flood the mines of the Ruhr and the Saar. {pause: 1.0} Un-build the forge, permanently.

And for a moment — briefly — this was not a fringe idea. At a conference in Quebec in 1944, it was initialed at the very top; Roosevelt and Churchill actually put their marks on a version of it.

Now, the Morgenthau Plan was pulled back; it did not become policy in that form. But I don't want you to remember it for what it did. I want you to remember it for what it *reveals*.

It tells you the mental universe these people were living in at the end of the war. The most respectable minds in the Western world had reached a point where turning a nation of seventy million people back into farmland {pause: 1.0} sounded like a *reasonable* answer to the German problem. That is how frightened they were. {pause: 1.0} That is how completely the old solutions had failed. When your best idea is to erase a country's ability to make things, you have run out of every gentler idea there ever was.

Keep that in mind, too. Because the man who is going to solve the Ruhr will do it by proposing almost the *opposite* of Morgenthau — and you'll only understand how radical his answer was if you remember how dark the alternatives on the table had gotten.

## The rubble, and the man who was right   {tone: somber, rate: very-slow, note: "the handover; do not resolve anything. End in the dark, with the door just barely open."}

And so we arrive at the ruin.

The spring of 1945. The Second World War in Europe is ending. And the continent that had produced Verdun, and the war to end all wars, and then a second war so vast we cannot count its dead — that continent is now, physically, rubble.

Picture it not as a map but, as I said at the start, almost as a smell. {pause: 1.0} Brick dust. Ash. Cities across Germany and beyond flattened into fields of broken stone. Tens of millions dead, and tens of millions more displaced — wandering, starving, homeless, on roads all over Europe. {pause: 1.0} This is the bottom. This is what the nation-state, left to its own devices, had finally achieved. This is the exhibit that proves the crank's case: Coudenhove-Kalergi had said the nation-states were the disease, and here — in the ash — was the diagnosis confirmed, written across an entire continent.

Now — take stock of where our characters stand, in the rubble, at this final moment.

France is *free* again. But France is also, once again, terrified. Because Germany is beaten now, yes — but Germany was beaten in 1918, too, and look how that turned out. And that forge, that Ruhr, that lock — it is still sitting there in the west of Germany. Broken for the moment. But coal is still coal, and steel is still steel, and France knows, in its bones, that the forge can always be rebuilt, and the invasion can always come a fourth time. The oldest fear is not gone. It is just waiting.

And Jean Monnet? {pause: 0.8}

He is now in his fifties. For thirty years — since he was that twenty-six-year-old talking his way into a Prime Minister's office — he has believed one thing. That states acting together can do what states acting separately cannot. For thirty years he has been, essentially, *right*. And for thirty years it has bought him almost nothing, because no one was frightened enough, or desperate enough, to actually pay the price his idea demanded. The dreamers were applauded and buried. {pause: 1.0} Being right in advance bought no one anything. {pause: 1.4}

But now — {tone: wistful} now something has changed. And it is a terrible thing to have to say, but it is the truth this whole series turns on.

{tone: somber} The ruin is *total* enough, at last, that someone might finally listen.

For the first time in thirty years, the catastrophe is so complete, the old way has failed so absolutely, that a man who says *we must stop being separate nations* might no longer sound like a crank. {pause: 1.0} {tone: reverent} He might sound like the only sane person left standing in the ash. {pause: 1.6}
