## A room named for a killing   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "unhurried, setting a scene; let the irony assemble itself"}

I want to begin not with a treaty, but with a room.

Rome. The 25th of March, 1957. On the Capitoline Hill — the hill where the Roman Senate once met, where the generals rode in triumph — there is a hall inside a Renaissance palace, and it has a name. The Sala degli Orazi e Curiazi. The Room of the Horatii and the Curiatii.

The frescoes on its walls tell an old story. Two cities at war — Rome and Alba Longa — and rather than bleed two whole armies white, they agree to something almost merciful in its brutality. Each city will send three brothers. Three champions against three champions. They will fight to the death, {pause: 1.0} and whichever side's men are left standing, that city wins. A war decided by six men, {tone: grave} so that thousands need not die.

That is the story painted on the walls. {pause: 0.6}

And on the 25th of March, 1957, in that room, under those frescoes, the representatives of six nations sit down at a long table and sign a treaty. {pause: 0.8}

Six nations. A room named for the moment when combat between champions was substituted for slaughter between peoples. {pause: 1.0}

Nobody chose that room by accident. These were men soaked in symbolism, and they had picked, deliberately, a hall commemorating the oldest idea in European statecraft — that if you are clever enough, you can find a *substitute* for total war.

Only this time the substitute isn't three brothers with swords. {pause: 0.8} It's a *customs union*. {pause: 1.2}

## What they were still standing in   {tone: somber, rate: slow, note: "the weight of a recent defeat; heavier than the opening"}

Now hold that image — six men, the long table, the frescoes — and let me show you what it cost to get them into that room. Because two and a half years earlier, this same movement had been not just defeated but *humiliated*.

Remember what happened in the last chapter. The 30th of August, 1954. The French National Assembly, by a procedural motion, refused even to *debate* the European Defence Community — the European army that France itself had proposed. Not voted down on its merits. Ruled unworthy of discussion. Killed in an afternoon, {pause: 1.0} unspoken. {pause: 0.8}

Four years of work. Six nations' signatures. {tone: grave} Gone. {pause: 1.2}

And it wasn't only a treaty that died. The idea underneath it died with it — the idea that you could build Europe by going *straight at the state itself*, at its army, its constitution, its raw nerve of sovereignty, and expect a national parliament to hand those things over. {pause: 0.6} Altiero Spinelli had smuggled a European constitution into that military treaty. De Gasperi had staked his faith on it — and died in August 1954, weeks before the collapse, {pause: 1.0} never knowing the thing he'd helped launch was already dead.

So picture the mood among the men who believed in this project in the autumn of 1954. They had reached for everything and lost everything. {tone: grave, rate: very-slow} The federalist dream — Europe as a *state* — was a corpse. {pause: 1.2}

And the great irony, the almost comic irony, {tone: wry} is that the man most associated with the whole enterprise couldn't even leave his own office.

## The man who could not resign   {tone: wry, rate: normal, note: "let the absurdity breathe; a small human comedy inside the gloom"}

Jean Monnet. You've met him. The cognac merchant's son who never went to university, who talked his way into a Prime Minister's office at twenty-six, who authored the Schuman Declaration in a nine-day writing marathon and handed it to another man to read aloud. The architect of the method: don't ask permission, present the finished thing, let the momentum carry it.

By November 1954 Monnet has decided he's had enough of the inside game. He announces he will not renew his mandate as head of the Coal and Steel Community's High Authority. He wants out. He starts packing.

And then his own legal service comes to him with a small, exquisite problem. {pause: 0.6}

He *can't* leave. Not legally. He cannot vacate the office until a successor has been named — {pause: 0.8} and no successor has been named. So the man who wanted to walk away is trapped at his desk for five more months {pause: 1.0} by the very machinery he helped build. {pause: 0.6}

Here is the thing about Monnet, though. A lesser man sulks. Monnet uses those five stranded months to plan his next move — an Action Committee for a United States of Europe, a pressure group of parties and unions to push integration from outside government.

But the real relaunch — the one that mattered, the one that built the thing still standing today — {tone: measured} that one didn't come from Monnet at all.

In fact it came from a man who thought Monnet was *wrong*. {pause: 0.6}

## The forgotten architect   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "introduce a genuinely fresh face; let the listener feel this is a surprise"}

His name was Johan Willem Beyen. {pause: 0.6}

And if you've been told this whole story is the story of one man — Monnet, Monnet, Monnet — then Beyen is your correction. A Dutchman. Not a lifelong idealist. A banker. He'd been a director at Unilever, he'd worked at the Bank for International Settlements, he'd been at the International Monetary Fund. A man of ledgers and exchange rates, not manifestos. When people list the founding fathers of Europe, they usually forget him entirely. The forgotten architect.

And Beyen had a heresy. {pause: 1.0}

Monnet's whole faith — the thing that had produced the Coal and Steel Community — was that you integrate Europe *one sector at a time*. You pool coal and steel. It works. Momentum builds. You pool the next thing, and the next, and one day you look up and you've built a Europe, brick by careful brick. Sector by sector.

Beyen said: no. {pause: 0.6} That's backwards.

Integrate one sector at a time, Beyen argued, and each sector just learns to protect itself. The coal men guard coal, the steel men guard steel, the farmers guard the farms, and every one of them lobbies to keep their own little walled garden. You get resistance at every step, forever.

What you need instead, Beyen said, is to put *everyone in the same boat at once*. A horizontal customs union. A common market. Tear down the tariffs across the *whole* economy in one stroke, so that every industry is exposed together, and no single sector can hide behind a wall while the others burn.

It is the opposite of the Monnet method. {tone: wry} And here is the part that ought to make you smile. In April 1955, Beyen puts his memorandum on the table — the Benelux countries carry it forward — and it wins the argument. It wins the argument {pause: 0.8} that founds the modern European Union. {pause: 1.0}

And Jean Monnet — the great man, the architect, the father of it all — Jean Monnet *wasn't even at the conference.* {pause: 1.2}

## Because a minister missed a plane   {tone: wry, rate: normal, note: "the almost-farcical accident behind a founding moment"}

Which conference? Here we arrive at one of my favorite facts in this entire story, because it is so gloriously human, so far from the marble-and-frescoes version of history.

The foreign ministers of the six coal-and-steel nations agree to meet in the summer of 1955 to talk about relaunching Europe. And the meeting is held in Messina.

Messina. A port town on the northeastern tip of Sicily. If you were designing the birthplace of the European Economic Community — the thing that becomes the EU — you would not, in a thousand years, pick Messina. It's the far corner of Italy. It's inconvenient for everyone.

So why Messina? {pause: 1.2}

Because the Italian foreign minister, Gaetano Martino, was stuck there. There were regional assembly elections going on in Sicily, and Martino — who happened to be Sicilian, Messina was his birthplace — couldn't get away. He couldn't fly out to host a proper conference somewhere sensible.

So the mountain came to Martino. The founding conference of the European common market was held in a Sicilian port town, in June 1955, essentially because one man couldn't get on a plane. {pause: 1.0}

I dwell on this because it matters to how you should hear this whole episode. {tone: measured, rate: slow} The version in the textbooks is all destiny and vision. The truth is bankers with heresies, ministers stranded by local elections, and men rebuilding from humiliation. The peace of Europe was assembled by human beings, in real rooms, {pause: 1.0} for reasons that were sometimes as small as a missed flight. {pause: 1.0}

At Messina, the six agree: relaunch. Not an army. Not a constitution. A *market*. And they set up a committee to work out how — a committee chaired by a Belgian.

## The indispensable Belgian   {tone: measured, rate: slow}

His name is Paul-Henri Spaak. {pause: 0.6}

If Beyen supplies the heresy and Messina supplies the accident, Spaak supplies the *will*. He is the fixer, the closer, the man who makes rooms agree. He chairs the committee coming out of Messina. He drives its report. He'll chair the conference at Venice that follows. He'll oversee the negotiations that turn it all into treaties. He'll sign for Belgium in that room in Rome.

Spaak is the connective tissue of this whole episode — so when I say "the Spaak Committee," picture a man whose entire genius is holding quarreling delegations in a room until they stop quarreling.

And into Spaak's committee, in the second half of 1955, walks a delegation from a country that is, once again, the ghost at this feast. {pause: 1.0}

Britain. {pause: 0.8}

## The man they sent   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "this is a contested story; signal the ambiguity in the telling, don't resolve it"}

Now, the British had stayed out of the Coal and Steel Community. They'd been kept out of the Schuman plan deliberately, told after the fact. That was the pattern. So this time, at least, the six invite them in. Come to the Spaak Committee. Sit at the table. Help design the common market.

And Britain sends a man named Russell Bretherton. {pause: 0.6}

Consider who Bretherton was. An economist. A civil servant — an under-secretary at the Board of Trade. A serious, capable man. He was also, and I love this detail, an amateur entomologist — a collector and student of insects, of butterflies and beetles.

He was *not* a politician. Not a minister. Not a man who could commit his government to anything. He came to the most important economic negotiation of the century {pause: 0.8} with the standing of an observer. {pause: 0.8}

And here is the strange, haunting thing, preserved in his own archived reports. Bretherton wrote that Britain, sitting in that room, "had the power to guide the conclusions of this conference in almost any direction we like."

In almost any direction we like. {pause: 0.6}

He could see it. The man could *see* that Britain, at that table, in that moment, held enormous influence over the shape of the thing being born — could have steered the whole European project toward Britain's own preferences, a looser, freer, more Atlantic arrangement. {tone: wistful} And he had no authority to reach out and take that power. {pause: 1.0} He was there to watch. {pause: 1.0}

And in November 1955, the British Cabinet pulls him out. Britain walks away from the table. {pause: 0.6} Again. {pause: 1.4}

## What did Bretherton say?   {tone: measured, rate: normal, note: "openly a historical mystery; the narrator refuses to pretend certainty"}

Now — there is a famous story about how Bretherton left. A farewell speech, supposedly delivered to the committee on his way out. It goes something like this. He tells the assembled Europeans that the treaty they're negotiating has no chance — that they will never manage to negotiate it, and even if they negotiate it they'll never ratify it, and even if they ratify it it will never work. And then, the story goes, he stands, and says: {rate: slow} "au revoir, et bonne chance." {pause: 1.2} Goodbye, and good luck.

It's a wonderful line. {tone: wry} Dismissive, aristocratic, wrong in every particular — the perfect image of British hauteur missing the boat. Jacques Delors would brandish it decades later.

Here is my problem, and I want to be honest with you about it, because the honesty is the point. {tone: measured, rate: slow} We are not sure {pause: 0.8} he said it. {pause: 1.0}

The quote comes down to us essentially through a single French witness. Bretherton's own papers suggest he privately thought Britain could have *shaped* the whole thing — hardly the words of a man who thought it was doomed nonsense. And there's a dispute even about the manner of his leaving. Bretherton said he was *asked* to leave — pulled out by London. Spaak said he *walked* out. {pause: 0.6}

Which is it? Did he stride out with a witty insult, or was he quietly recalled by a Cabinet that had decided Europe wasn't for Britain?

I can't tell you for certain, and I'm not going to pretend I can. What I can tell you is that the *substance* isn't in doubt: Britain sent a man who could see the opportunity and couldn't take it, and then removed even him. Whether the famous farewell captures Bretherton's mind or only his Cabinet's — that we may never know. {pause: 1.0} Hold the ambiguity. It's more interesting than a clean answer.

## The men who stayed at the table   {tone: measured, rate: slow}

So Britain leaves, and the six stay, and now we should look at *who* stayed — because the men building this thing were, several of them, extraordinary.

Take the German who would eventually sign — Walter Hallstein. A law professor. And where do you suppose Walter Hallstein spent the end of the Second World War? {pause: 1.0}

In Mississippi. {pause: 1.0} As a prisoner of war. At a camp called Camp Como, in the American South, a captured German officer.

And what did he do there? He organized a *university*. A camp university, behind the wire, teaching law to his fellow prisoners. {pause: 0.8} Sit with that image for a moment — {pause: 1.2} a German POW in the Mississippi heat, running lectures on jurisprudence to other captured men. This is the person who will become the first President of the European Commission. A man who would later call national sovereignty "a doctrine of yesteryear."

He does not believe the nation-state is sacred. {tone: grave, rate: slow} He has watched the nation-state produce {pause: 1.0} the barbed wire he's standing behind. {pause: 1.2}

These are the men now bent over the technical work — Spaak holding them together, Beyen's horizontal idea as the frame, the Dutch and the Belgians and the Italians and the Germans and the French and Luxembourg, hammering out how you actually *build* a common market. Tariffs to be dismantled on a timetable. A common external wall against the outside world. Institutions to police it all.

And it might all have collapsed. It nearly did. {tone: tense} Because inside one of those delegations — the German one — {pause: 0.6} a war was going on.

## The heretic in the German cabinet   {tone: tense, rate: normal, note: "internal conflict, real stakes; this is where the treaty almost dies"}

Here's something the tidy version leaves out. {pause: 0.8} The single most successful economic mind in Europe {pause: 0.8} was *against* this treaty. {pause: 1.0}

Ludwig Erhard. The architect of the *Wirtschaftswunder* — the German economic miracle. The man who'd unleashed West Germany's astonishing recovery by *freeing* markets, tearing out controls, betting everything on competition and open trade. Erhard was Adenauer's Economics Minister. And Erhard looked at the proposed common market and hated it.

Why? Because to Erhard, a customs union was a *protectionist* device. Six countries dropping tariffs among themselves while raising a common wall against everyone else — that wasn't free trade, that was a fortress. A cozy little club. Erhard wanted something bigger and looser: a broad free-trade area across all of Western Europe, open to the world, open to Britain. Not this walled garden of six.

And he wasn't alone. Franz Josef Strauss stood with him. At a meeting at Saint-Cloud, near Paris, at the end of October 1956, the German position deadlocked — Erhard and Strauss pulling one way, the Europeanists pulling the other. {pause: 0.8}

{tone: dramatic} The father of the German economic miracle was trying to strangle the European common market in its cradle, {pause: 1.0} from *inside the German cabinet*. {pause: 1.0}

And there was only one man who could overrule him. {pause: 1.0}

## Adenauer chooses Europe over economics   {tone: grave, rate: slow}

Konrad Adenauer. The old Chancellor. {pause: 0.6}

Adenauer overrode his own Economics Minister. He decided that the *political* prize — Germany bound irreversibly into Europe, Germany made safe, Germany made a partner instead of a threat — was worth more than the economic argument Erhard was winning on the merits. He chose the fortress of six over the open free-trade area, because the fortress *bound Germany in*, and being bound in was the whole point.

And Adenauer did something else — something that tells you everything about the psychology of a defeated Germany eleven years after the war.

He told the French ambassador, in effect: Germany cannot lead in foreign policy. {pause: 0.8} Not after what we have done. France must lead. Germany will support. {pause: 1.2}

Think about what that is. That is the most powerful economy on the continent {pause: 0.8} *deliberately kneeling*. {pause: 1.0} Choosing subordination. And it was not weakness — it was strategy. Because a confident, demanding Germany would have terrified everyone and gotten nothing. A humble Germany, a Germany that said "you lead, we'll follow," let France extract better terms than it could ever have won from a Germany throwing its weight around. Adenauer's deference *was* his power.

So the German war ends with Adenauer overruling Erhard, choosing the political Europe over the economic one, and choosing to follow France. {pause: 0.6}

But even with Germany's internal fight settled, the whole negotiation was still stuck. And what broke it loose wasn't idealism at all. {tone: dramatic} It was catastrophe — {pause: 0.8} two catastrophes, on the same few days, a thousand miles away. {pause: 0.8}

## Suez   {tone: dramatic, rate: normal, note: "the hinge of the episode; two humiliations breaking the deadlock at once"}

The autumn of 1956. {pause: 0.6}

Two things happen almost simultaneously, and together they change the mind of Europe.

In Egypt, Gamal Abdel Nasser has nationalized the Suez Canal. And Britain and France — the old imperial powers, in secret collusion with Israel — invade to take it back. Late October, early November 1956. And it is a disaster. Not a military disaster — militarily they're winning — but a *political* one, total and humiliating. Because the United States turns on its own allies. Washington applies brutal financial pressure — threatens the pound, refuses to prop up sterling — and forces Britain and France into a shameful, scrambling withdrawal.

The lesson lands like a hammer, especially in Paris: France and Britain, acting alone, as great powers, in the world they used to run — could be *overruled by a phone call from Washington*. {pause: 1.2} They were not great powers anymore. {rate: slow} Not on their own. {pause: 1.0}

And at the very same moment — the very same days — the Soviet Union sends its tanks into Budapest and crushes the Hungarian uprising. Naked, brutal, on the world's front page.

So here is what Europe is looking at, in the first week of November 1956. To the east, {pause: 0.6} the Soviet Union murdering a nation's hope with tanks. To the west, {pause: 0.6} the United States humiliating France and Britain and reminding them they are clients now, not powers.

{tone: grave, rate: slow} Caught between the two giants, {pause: 0.8} small and exposed and alone. {pause: 1.4}

And on the 5th and 6th of November — *within days* of the Suez ceasefire, the humiliation still raw — Adenauer travels to Paris to meet the French Prime Minister, Guy Mollet. And in that meeting, under the shadow of those twin disasters, the two men clear away the last obstacles to the Treaties of Rome. {pause: 0.8}

Do you feel the grim logic of it? {tone: grave} The common market was not, in the end, sold to France by dreamers promising a better tomorrow. It was sold by Nasser and Eisenhower and the Soviet tanks in Budapest. France reached for Europe because Suez had just proven, in the cruelest possible way, that France alone was nothing.

{tone: grave, rate: very-slow} The peace of Europe was midwifed by humiliation. {pause: 1.4} Remember that when people tell you it was built on hope. {pause: 1.0}

## The bargain nobody would say out loud   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "the central ambiguity of the episode — voice both readings, resolve neither"}

Now, with the last obstacles clearing, we come to the bargain at the heart of the treaty — and to the second thing in this episode I cannot tell you for certain.

Why did France agree? Even after Suez, France was nervous. A common market meant German industry — efficient, terrifying German industry — competing freely against French factories. What did France get to make that bearable?

France got agriculture. {pause: 1.2}

The deal, roughly, was this. Germany wanted the industrial common market — open markets for its manufactured goods. France wanted protection and support for its huge, politically untouchable population of farmers. So the treaty gestured toward what would become the Common Agricultural Policy — a system, eventually, of guaranteed prices and subsidies that would pour money into French farms. The customs union for Germany's industry; the farm support for France's countryside. Industry balanced by agriculture.

And here is the ambiguity — the thing historians still argue about. {pause: 1.0}

Was that a clear, *shared* understanding — everyone at the table knowing exactly what had been agreed, a fair trade openly struck? {pause: 0.8} Or was it a *deliberate ambiguity* — a bargain left vague on purpose, the details of the farm policy left unwritten, so that each side could tell its own parliament whatever it needed to hear, and France could come back later and exploit the vagueness to extract far more than Germany ever thought it had promised? {pause: 1.2}

Both readings are alive in the record. Both. And I won't choose for you, because the historians haven't. {pause: 0.8}

But notice — either way — what it tells us about the *method*. Whether it was an honest bargain or a strategic fog, the durable heart of this treaty was a deal about money and markets that ordinary people never voted on and mostly never understood. That is not an accident. {tone: grave} That is the lesson of the last chapter, applied.

## The lesson of the corpse   {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "the thesis of the episode, stated plainly; let it unsettle"}

Because — hold that thought — remember why we started this episode in the wreckage of the European army.

The men building the common market had just watched the direct approach die. They had asked a parliament, out loud, to hand over the army — the naked substance of the state — and the parliament had refused to so much as discuss it.

So they learned a lesson, and it is the darkest, most consequential lesson in this whole series. {pause: 1.2}

{rate: very-slow} *Never again ask the people directly.* {pause: 1.6}

Don't put the sovereignty question on the table. Don't hold the referendum. Don't demand that a nation vote, yes or no, to become part of something larger. You will lose. You lost with the army and you will lose again.

Instead — hide the integration inside prosperity. Build a market. Lower the price of a car and a refrigerator and a holiday abroad. Let trade grow, let jobs follow, let a whole generation get comfortable inside a common European economy — and let the *loyalty* grow out of the comfort, quietly, without anyone ever having to stand up and vote on the principle of surrendering their nation. Build the market, and let the market build the Europeans. {pause: 0.6}

It is brilliant. It worked. It is arguably the most successful act of political engineering in modern history — Franco-German war made not merely unthinkable but, in Schuman's old phrase, materially impossible.

And it should unsettle you a little, even so. Because it means the thing that lasted — the spine of the entire European Union, still there today — was engineered by men who had decided, on purpose, {pause: 1.0} that the surest way to unite Europe was to make sure the people of Europe were *never quite asked*. {pause: 1.4}

Should that reassure you, or unsettle you? {pause: 1.2} Hold the question. We are going to need it.

## Val-Duchesse, and a second treaty with a secret   {tone: measured, rate: slow}

Through the winter of 1956 into the spring of 1957, the detailed negotiations run at a chateau outside Brussels — Val-Duchesse. Spaak presiding, the six grinding through the technical work, turning the political bargains into treaty text.

And there were two treaties, not one. {pause: 0.6}

The first is the big one — the European Economic Community. The common market. Beyen's horizontal customs union made real. That's the one that lasts, the one that becomes the EU.

The second was called Euratom — the European Atomic Energy Community. And its public face was gleaming and hopeful: the six pooling their efforts on *peaceful* nuclear power. The atom for civilization, not war.

But there was something buried underneath Euratom, {tone: tense} and the declassified documents show it. {pause: 1.0}

Under the cover of peaceful nuclear cooperation, France, West Germany, and Italy were quietly exploring *weapons* cooperation. A shared bomb, in effect, sheltered inside the civilian program. Adenauer, it is reported, wanted for Germany "as quickly as possible, the chance of producing our own nuclear weapons." {pause: 0.8}

{tone: grave, rate: slow} Let that sit. {pause: 1.2} Eleven years after the war, the men signing a peace treaty were, in one of its two halves, arranging the shared machinery {pause: 1.0} for a nuclear arsenal. {pause: 1.2}

That scheme would not survive. Within a year a man was going to return to power in France who found the very idea intolerable — who would insist that France's bomb, the *force de frappe*, be French and French alone, no sharing, no Europe. He would kill the Euratom weapons scheme in 1958. But we are getting ahead of ourselves. Remember that man is coming. More on him in a moment.

## The Room of the Horatii, again   {tone: reverent, rate: slow, note: "return to the opening image; let the signing land with weight"}

And so we come back to where we began. {pause: 0.6}

The 25th of March, 1957. Rome. The Capitoline Hill. The Sala degli Orazi e Curiazi — the Room of the Horatii and the Curiatii, the frescoes of the champions who fought to the death so their peoples wouldn't have to.

It is, as it happens, the feast of the Annunciation — the day the Christian calendar marks an announcement that changes everything. The men who chose the date would have known that too. Not one detail here was left to chance. And they are signing on the hill where the Roman Senate met, where triumphs were celebrated — the ceremonial heart of a vanished empire that had once, in its own brutal way, united this continent by the sword.

Now six nations do it with pens. {pause: 1.4}

Belgium, France, West Germany, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands. Spaak signs for Belgium. Adenauer and Hallstein for Germany. The Treaties of Rome. The European Economic Community, and Euratom.

There is no European army in these pages. No constitution. No Peoples' Chamber elected by universal suffrage — all of that died with the defence community in 1954. {pause: 0.8} What's here instead is quieter and, it turns out, far stronger. A timetable to dismantle tariffs. A common external wall. A promise about farms. A commission in Brussels to run it all.

{rate: very-slow} A market instead of an army. {pause: 1.6}

And out of the ruins of total defeat, the men who believed in Europe had built the thing that would actually endure — the spine that is still standing under the European Union today, more than half a century on. They reached for the state and lost. {pause: 0.8} They reached for the market, and won. {pause: 1.2}

## The prime minister of a country that doesn't exist   {tone: measured, rate: slow}

The treaties come into force on the 1st of January, 1958. And the new machine gets a government.

Walter Hallstein — our German law professor, our organizer of a prisoner-of-war university in Mississippi — becomes the first President of the European Commission.

And Hallstein takes the job *seriously*. {pause: 0.6} Almost dangerously seriously. He describes his Commission as something like "a kind of European prime minister's" office. He will receive the credentials of foreign ambassadors — the ceremony by which a nation's envoy presents himself to a *head of state* — as though Brussels were a capital and he its leader. {pause: 1.0}

He behaves, in short, like the prime minister of a country that does not yet exist. {pause: 1.2} A country he intends to *will* into existence, through the patient machinery of the market, exactly as the method prescribed. National sovereignty, remember, is to him a "doctrine of yesteryear." {pause: 0.8}

For a moment, in 1958, it looks unstoppable. The machine is built. It has a government. It has a man at its head who acts as though the united Europe were already real, and merely waiting to be finished. {pause: 1.0}

## The giant returns   {tone: tense, rate: slow, note: "the cliffhanger; turn the triumph cold; end pointing hard at Episode 5"}

And in that very same year — 1958 — a giant walks back into power in Paris. {pause: 1.0}

Charles de Gaulle. {pause: 1.2}

He does not believe in "Europe." {pause: 0.8} He believes in *France*. He has just watched, with cold fury, a scheme to hand France's future nuclear force to some shared European arrangement, and he regards the whole supranational apparatus — Hallstein's little court in Brussels, with its ambassadors and its credentials and its airs of statehood — as a fiction. A pretension. {pause: 0.6} A usurpation of things that belong, by right, to nations. To *his* nation. {pause: 0.8}

The engineers have just built their machine. They have given it a government, and a man who runs it like the prime minister of a Europe that isn't yet a country.

And now the most formidable nationalist of the twentieth century has returned to power in the largest of the six member states — a man who looks at Walter Hallstein sitting there "surrounded with all the trappings of sovereignty," {pause: 0.8} and decides that this will not stand. {pause: 1.2}

They built the machine. {pause: 1.0}

{tone: grave, rate: slow} Now the General is going to try to break it. {pause: 1.2} From the inside.
