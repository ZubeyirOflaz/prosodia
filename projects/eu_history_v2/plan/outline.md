# Ever Closer Union — Series Outline

*A Hardcore-History-style narrative history of how the European Union was forged out of the ruin of 1945. Single narrator. Source material: `projects/eu_history_v2/research/dossier.md` (citations preserved below).*

---

## THE DRIVING QUESTION (stated once, for the whole series)

**When a continent has proven it will destroy itself given the chance, can you engineer peace from the top down — chaining nations together with treaties, coal quotas, and shared currencies before their peoples ever agree to it — and does that engineered peace hold, or does the gap between what the architects built and what the voters wanted eventually come due?**

Every episode re-tests one facet of this: the peace was *designed*, deliberately, by a handful of men who mostly never won an election — and it worked, spectacularly, right up until the moment the designed thing collided with the people it was designed for.

**Split of Era 7 across the final two episodes (no overlap):**
- **Episode 7** owns: fall of the Wall → reunification–for–euro bargain → Maastricht signature and the referendum revolts (Denmark, France) → euro launch → Stability Pact breach → eastern "Big Bang" enlargement. (The *building* of the Union and its currency.)

**Workload flags (coverage is intentionally uneven):**
- **Episodes 1, 2, 4, 5 carry disproportionate weight** and are the depth of the series — this is where the founding drama lives and where the brief's center of gravity (the ruin of 1945 and what was built directly on it) sits. Episodes 1–5 are each meant to stand as strong hours.
- **Episode 3 is short and sharp** — a single failure, deliberately compressed to one arc so its shock lands.
- **Episodes 6–8 cover far more calendar time on far less runway per year.** This is deliberate: the founding decade gets luxurious pacing; the later half-century is compressed and accelerated as the series drives toward the finale. Episode 8 is a NARROWING, not a catch-all — it does not attempt to summarize the whole EU; it escalates around the driving question and ends on it.

## EPISODE 1 — "The Suicide of a Continent"
*Roughly 1914 → May 1945. The problem the whole series exists to answer.*

**Scope.** How Europe destroyed itself twice in thirty years, and the handful of dreamers who kept insisting, against all evidence, that the nation-state was the disease. Covers WWI and Verdun; Versailles and the war-guilt clause; the interwar federalist manifestos (Coudenhove-Kalergi's *Pan-Europa*, Briand's League speech); the 1940 Franco-British Union gambit; and the specific, concrete fear that would shape everything after — the Ruhr. **Boundary:** ends at V-E Day / the ruin itself. It sets up the *problem* and introduces Monnet as a method, not yet a solution. It does NOT touch the Marshall Plan or any postwar institution — that is Episode 2.

**Opening hook.** Verdun: ten months, ~300,000 dead, over ground that scarcely moved — land so poisoned it is still sealed off as the *Zone Rouge* a century later. Open in that dead zone and ask: what kind of civilization does this to itself, and then does it *again* twenty years later?

**Central tension (facet of the driving question).** Between the wars, *every* government applauded the idea of a united Europe and *not one* would surrender an inch of sovereignty to build it. The tension is the terrifying gap between universal agreement in principle and total paralysis in practice — and the dawning realization, by 1945, that maybe peace can't be voted into existence; maybe it has to be engineered by someone who stops asking permission. The unresolved "so what": the men who saw it coming were dismissed as cranks; being right in advance bought them nothing.

**Ground-level human anchor.** **Jean Monnet** — introduce him here as a method, not a hero. The cognac merchant's son who never went to university, walks into a Prime Minister's office at 26, and already believes the thing no one else does: states acting together can do what states acting separately cannot.

**Texture (raw material — placement/wording left to writer):**
- Monnet's father at the docks: *"Don't take any books with you. No one can think for you. Look out the window, talk to people."* Shaped a lifetime of persuading over dinner, not by memoranda. *(Monnet, Memoirs 1976/1978; jean-monnet.europa.eu; blog.cognac-expert.com)*
- 1914, aged 26 and medically unfit to fight, Monnet talks his way in to see PM Viviani and proposes pooling Allied shipping — becomes the Inter-Allied Shipping Committee, arguably the first real supranational pooling body. *(jean-monnet.europa.eu; EPRS_BRI(2017)614603; New World Encyclopedia)*
- Verdun's *Zone Rouge*: terrain still uninhabitable, sealed for unexploded ordnance and human remains. *(Philpott, Bloody Victory 2009; memorial-verdun.fr; Britannica)*
- Coudenhove-Kalergi: Austrian count father literally helped up off his horse in Japan by Mitsuko Aoyama, whom he married; their half-Japanese son publishes *Pan-Europa* at 29, pulls in Einstein and Freud. Hitler calls him a "cosmopolitan bastard" in *Mein Kampf* — a description that is, factually, an accurate summary of his heritage. *(Wikipedia; japansocietyboston.org; Mazower, Hitler's Empire 2008)*
- Briand, Sept 1929: 26 European delegations applaud the "federal link"; 68 ministers attend six sessions of enquiry; nothing is built; Briand dies March 1932 with the plan still on the table. *(europarl.europa.eu; Boyce 1980; Oxford Academic)*
- 16 June 1940, the Franco-British Union — full merger, shared citizenship, one war cabinet. **VERIFIED:** de Gaulle's dry retort to Monnet — *"Monsieur Monnet, voulez-vous marier le président Lebrun au roi George VI?"* ("Do you want to marry President Lebrun to King George VI?") — yet he agreed to carry it to Churchill for its morale value. Pétain and Weygand called it enslavement to England; France signed the armistice two days later. *(fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_franco-britannique; en Wikipedia Franco-British Union; Fondation Charles de Gaulle; History Guild)*
- The Ruhr in French internal documents: *"a symbol of German power and a source of French humiliation"* — the coal-and-steel heartland that fed the war machine twice in thirty years. Plant this; it is the lock Monnet will pick in Episode 2. *(CVCE.eu; ehs.org.uk; Wikipedia Ruhr Question)*
- Optional darker note: the Morgenthau Plan (Aug 1944) — de-industrialize Germany, flood the Ruhr and Saar mines, turn it pastoral — briefly initialed by Roosevelt and Churchill at Quebec. Shows what "serious people" thought the German problem required. *(dossier Era 1)*

**Named conflict / uncertainty.** The dead of WWII resist counting: global estimates run 70–85 million; Europe 35–40 million; the USSR alone perhaps 27 million, with Soviet and Western figures diverging. The narrator must give a *range*, not a false-precise number — and the fact that we cannot count the dead is itself part of the horror. *(National WWII Museum; Britannica; Wikipedia WWII Casualties; Statista)*

**Handover (suspended tension).** 1945: the continent is rubble, France is again terrified of the Ruhr, and the man who thinks peace can be *built* rather than declared has spent thirty years being proven right and ignored. Now, for the first time, the ruin is total enough that someone might finally listen. But first, someone has to give Europe the money to stand up at all — and that money will arrive from across an ocean, wrapped in a speech that almost nobody was supposed to hear.

## EPISODE 2 — "The Speech Nobody Was Supposed to Hear"
*Roughly June 1947 → July 1952. The founding act.*

**Scope.** The two things that got postwar Western Europe on its feet and then bound part of it together: the Marshall Plan (and the Iron Curtain it hardened), and the Schuman Declaration / ECSC — the moment Monnet's method stopped being theory and became a treaty. Covers Marshall's Harvard speech and its deliberate burial in America; Stalin's rejection and the crushing of Czechoslovakia; the Hague Congress and the toothless Council of Europe; and then the nine drafts at Houjarray, the Salon de l'Horloge, and the first genuine surrender of sovereignty in modern European history. **Boundary:** ends when the ECSC comes into force and Monnet takes the High Authority (July 1952). It does NOT cover the European army — that fight starts in Episode 3 and had, in fact, already begun (Korea, June 1950), which the handover flags.

**Opening hook.** 5 June 1947, Harvard: while George Marshall reads a speech that will rebuild a continent, the U.S. State Department is telling American reporters it's "nothing of any importance" and Truman is calling a rival press conference to pull them away — while Dean Acheson is quietly on the phone to the BBC, making sure Europe hears every word before America can bury it. The most consequential speech of the century, deliberately hidden from the country that gave it.

**Central tension (facet).** Here the "engineered from the top" thesis gets its first real proof — and its first moral cost. Schuman reads a declaration written in secret by an unelected planner; Adenauer, chancellor of a still-occupied country, is told before Britain is; the whole thing is deliberately kept from the one government (Britain's) that might dilute it. The tension: the founding act of a democratic union was profoundly *undemocratic* in method — a fait accompli — and it worked *because* it was. The "so what": if pooling coal and steel really did make Franco-German war "materially impossible," was the sleight of hand justified? And who gave these men the right?

**Ground-level human anchor.** **Jean Monnet** again — but now at the peak of his method, running a nine-day writing marathon in his own farmhouse to author a declaration another man will read. Secondary anchor: **Robert Schuman**, the Lorrainer who spoke German as his first language and served in the German army, staking his career to deliver it.

**Texture (raw material):**
- The buried speech: State Dept. "nothing of any importance"; Acheson feeds the full text to the BBC; Bevin catches it on the radio that evening and calls Bidault the next morning. *(Marshall Foundation; Harvard Gazette 2017; Wikipedia Marshall Plan)*
- Marshall in a "double-breasted gray sharkskin suit," delivering a speech with "virtually no details and no numbers" — a challenge to Europeans, not an American blueprint. *(Harvard Gazette 2017; Marshall Foundation)*
- **Stalin's 4 PM deadline:** Czechoslovakia's government votes *unanimously* to accept Marshall aid (7 Jul 1947); Stalin summons them and gives until 4 PM on 10 July to reverse it; President Beneš is incapacitated by a stroke; the ministers debate only *how to explain* the reversal. **VERIFIED:** Masaryk on returning — *"I went to Moscow as the Foreign Minister of an independent sovereign state. I returned as a lackey of the Soviet Government."* (Dark epilogue available: Masaryk found dead beneath his window, March 1948.) *(Embassy of the Czech Republic London; Wikipedia Jan Masaryk; International MassMedia Agency 2017)*
- Nine drafts in nine days at Houjarray by Monnet, Reuter, Uri, Hirsch, Clappier; on the 9th, Monnet writes *"final, to be sent to Schuman"* in blue ink and sends a courier to Scy-Chazelles; Schuman phones Sunday, says he'll deliver it Tuesday — asks for no revisions. *(strasbourg-europe.eu; EP ThinkTank May 2025; Federalist.eu)*
- **The founding of Europe was never photographed:** 9 May 1950, Salon de l'Horloge, 200+ journalists, and *no* photographer, radio, or TV present. Every image now used to "illustrate" the declaration was shot on 20 June 1950 at the opening of negotiations; Schuman had to re-record the declaration for posterity. *(Europeana 70th-anniversary exhibition; EP ThinkTank 2025)*
- Adenauer told before Britain; in his memoirs he "agreed to his proposal with all my heart"; on 13 June 1950 he tells the Bundestag the plan's importance is "above all political and not economic." Schuman's own line: war between France and Germany becomes "not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible." *(Federalist.eu; Schuman Declaration text)*
- Attlee's irony: refuses the ECSC because he "would not accept the economy being handed over to an authority that is utterly undemocratic and responsible to nobody" — having just *nationalized* British coal and steel, which is precisely what he can't hand to a supranational body. *(Wikipedia ECSC; Campaign for an Independent Britain)*
- The Hague Congress, May 1948: ~750 delegates under Churchill's honorary chairmanship split immediately — federalists (French, Belgians, Italians, Dutch) vs. intergovernmentalists (British, Scandinavians); Duncan Sandys brokers language nobody fully accepts; the result is the *consultative*, powerless Council of Europe — and the federalists start hunting for a better vehicle. *(Wikipedia Congress of Europe; CVCE)*

**Named conflict / uncertainty.** *Who actually "authored" the founding of Europe — Monnet or Schuman?* The record is clear that Monnet's team wrote the text and Schuman delivered it unrevised — but Schuman's defenders note he staked his career on a document that would otherwise have stayed a planning paper, and there's a real dispute over Bernard Clappier (Schuman's own chef de cabinet) as the quiet bridge who got Schuman to embrace it. Name the ambiguity; don't resolve it. *(Federalist.eu vs. strasbourg-europe.eu vs. Wikipedia Schuman Declaration)* — Also usable: whether **Churchill** ever meant Britain to be *inside* his "United States of Europe" (Zurich 1946) or only to sponsor it from the outside (the "three circles"). *(EU-rope.ideasoneurope.eu vs. National Churchill Museum vs. Intl. Churchill Society)*

**Handover (suspended tension).** The coal-and-steel gamble works — and immediately the Americans want more. Because on 25 June 1950, one month *before* the ECSC is even ratified, war breaks out in Korea, and Washington starts demanding the one thing no Frenchman can stomach five years after occupation: German soldiers, with German rifles, in a German army. Monnet's method has just triumphed. It is about to attempt the most audacious pooling of all — a single European army — and it is about to fail catastrophically.

## EPISODE 3 — "The Crime of the 30th of August"
*Roughly June 1950 → 1955. The great failure. (Deliberately short and sharp.)*

**Scope.** The one clean defeat in the founding story: the attempt to pool armies before pooling anything else, and how France killed its own creation. Covers the Korean-War pressure and Acheson's "single package"; the Pleven Plan; the European Defence Community; Spinelli slipping a *constitution* into a defence treaty (the European Political Community); and the procedural knife-in-the-dark of 30 August 1954. **Boundary:** a tight, single-arc episode — it does NOT wander into the customs-union relaunch (Episode 4). It compresses the ratification chronology of the other five countries into a montage; its whole weight is on why the *proposer* refused to vote.

**Opening hook.** A treaty that four years of work produced, signed by six nations, ratified by others — killed in an afternoon in Paris on 30 August 1954 *without a single substantive speech on its merits ever being put to a vote.* Not defeated. Never debated. A procedural motion decided that Europe's army was not even worth discussing. Open on that vote, and ask how the country that *invented* the plan became the country that assassinated it.

**Central tension (facet).** Sovereignty has a hard floor. Coal you can pool; an army is the state itself. This episode tests the top-down thesis to destruction: the architects reached past what a national parliament could swallow, and the parliament simply refused to *look*. The "so what": the same French republican tradition that had suffered most from German invasion could not bear to dissolve its own army into a "stateless" European one — and a man like de Gasperi died believing in a Europe that had just been killed behind his back.

**Ground-level human anchor.** **Altiero Spinelli** — the anti-Monnet: a Communist jailed by Mussolini for eighteen years, who wrote a federalist manifesto on a prison island (Ventotene) and, in 1951, talked de Gasperi into smuggling a demand for a European *constitution* into a *military* treaty (Article 38). His defeat here is personal; flag that he will return decades later (Episode 6/7 territory) — a thread across the whole series.

**Texture (raw material — distribute thinly; this is a lean episode):**
- The Pleven Plan wasn't Pleven's: Monnet drafted the memo and sent it to PM Pleven *four days* before Pleven presented it (memo dated 20 Oct 1950) — the same trick he'd pulled with Schuman. *(CVCE archive; Wikipedia EDC Treaty)*
- Spinelli's Article 38: a clause about a European army quietly containing a clause about a European *state* — "a further federal or confederal structure based on the separation of powers." *(federalists.eu; psyjournals.ru; Academia.edu)*
- The Ad Hoc Assembly — 99 members, six months — actually *delivered* the first-ever draft European constitution on time (March 1953), including a "Peoples' Chamber" elected by universal suffrage; it then sat unread on ministers' desks for 17 months before the procedural vote killed it. *(EP Historical Archives; Wikipedia EPC)*
- Dulles's "agonizing reappraisal" threat (Dec 1953) — diplomatese for a possible U.S. troop withdrawal — which *backfired*, hardening Gaullist and Communist opposition (bullying / proof it was a U.S. project). *(Oxford Academic, Diplomatic History)*
- **The procedural scalpel:** 30 Aug 1954, opponents file a *question préalable* — a motion that there is no reason even to open debate — and win it 319–264. The treaty dies unspoken. *(CVCE; alcideproject.eu; Wikipedia EDC Treaty)*
- 81-year-old Édouard Herriot — three-time former PM, a mainstream Radical, not a Communist or Gaullist — rises on 28 August to deliver what's called the treaty's "death warrant": proof the opposition was the republican center, not the fringe. *(Taylor & Francis; CVCE; Britannica)*
- De Gasperi dies in August 1954, weeks before the collapse — never knowing the project he'd helped launch had been killed. *(psyjournals.ru; CVCE)*
- Coda: **Monnet can't leave his own office** — announces in Nov 1954 he won't renew his ECSC mandate, starts packing, and his legal service tells him he legally *cannot* leave until a successor is named; he stays five more months and uses them to plan the Action Committee. Human, wry, and it sets up the relaunch. *(Jean Monnet, Wikipedia, quoting his memoirs)*

**Named conflict / uncertainty.** Raymond Aron is reported to have called the EDC fight the greatest ideological rupture in France since the Dreyfus Affair — but the precise book/article/speech has not been located, so frame it as "Aron is reported to have written." Also flag the minor date/count wobble: the vote is given as 319–264 with 31 abstentions on 30 Aug 1954 by most sources, but one NATO item dates it 29 Aug; the *outcome* is not in doubt. *(alcideproject.eu citing Aron; Wikipedia EDC; CVCE)*

**Handover (suspended tension).** The federalists overreached and lost everything — Europe as a *state* is dead, apparently for good. And yet the same wreckage contains a lesson. If you cannot pool the army — the raw nerve of sovereignty — perhaps you go the other way entirely: not deeper into politics but wider into the economy, where a market can grow the solidarity that an army could never impose. Beaten, the architects are about to hold a conference in a Sicilian port town nobody would have chosen — and quietly relaunch everything.

## EPISODE 4 — "The Relaunch: A Market Instead of an Army"
*Roughly 1955 → January 1958. The pivot that actually built the thing that lasted.*

**Scope.** How the movement rebuilt itself from total defeat into the Treaties of Rome — the EEC and Euratom — the institutions that are still the spine of the EU today. Covers Beyen's insight (a horizontal customs union, *against* Monnet's sector-by-sector method); the Messina Conference; the Spaak Committee and the British walkout; the Val-Duchesse negotiations; the CAP-for-customs-union bargain; Suez as the shock that broke the final deadlock; and the signing on the Capitoline Hill. **Boundary:** ends when the Treaties of Rome enter force and Hallstein takes the Commission (1 Jan 1958). It does NOT deal with de Gaulle (who returns to power in 1958) — his assault on this new machine is Episode 5.

**Opening hook.** The room where it's signed: 25 March 1957, the Sala degli Orazi e Curiazi on Rome's Capitoline Hill — the Room of the Horatii and Curiatii, named for a legendary battle between rival champions who fight to the death so their nations won't have to. Six nations sign a treaty in a room named for the ritualized substitution of single combat for total war. Open on that layered, deliberate symbolism — then cut to *why* it almost didn't happen, and why the conference that made it possible was held in a Sicilian port only because one minister couldn't get on a plane.

**Central tension (facet).** This is the episode where the top-down method finds its *durable* form — not by asking for more surrender of sovereignty, but by hiding integration inside prosperity, where nobody has to vote on the principle. The tension: the thing that lasted was built on a deliberate ambiguity (the CAP bargain) and a deliberate exclusion (Britain again), and on shocks abroad (Suez) rather than idealism at home. The "so what": Europe's most successful institution was engineered by men who had learned from the EDC that you must never again ask the people directly — you build the market, and let the market build the loyalty. It worked. Should that reassure us or unsettle us?

**Ground-level human anchor.** **Paul-Henri Spaak** — the indispensable fixer who chairs Messina's committee, drives the report, chairs Venice, oversees Val-Duchesse, and signs for Belgium. Strong secondary anchor: **Johan Willem Beyen**, the "forgotten architect" (ex-Unilever, ex-IMF) whose customs-union idea *overturned Monnet's* and won — a genuinely fresh face for a listener who thinks this is all one man's story.

**Texture (raw material — a rich, texture-heavy episode):**
- **Beyen vs. Monnet:** the relaunch was built on *rejecting* Monnet. Monnet's faith: integrate one sector at a time and momentum follows. Beyen since 1953: no — sectors will each protect themselves; you need a *horizontal* customs union where everyone's in the same boat at once. Beyen's April 1955 memo wins the argument at Messina — and **Monnet wasn't even at the conference.** *(Wikipedia Benelux memorandum / Messina / Jean Monnet)*
- **Messina was in Sicily by accident:** Italian FM Gaetano Martino was stuck in Sicily by regional-assembly elections; Messina was his birthplace; the EEC's birthplace was chosen partly because a minister couldn't fly out. *(Wikipedia Messina Conference; Gaetano Martino)*
- **The Bretherton problem (flag as contested — see below):** Britain sends Russell Bretherton — an economist and amateur entomologist, explicitly *not* a politician who could commit — to the Spaak Committee; his own archived reports say Britain "had the power to guide the conclusions of this conference in almost any direction we like," but he had no authority, and the Cabinet pulled him out in Nov 1955. The famous "you will never be able to negotiate it… au revoir et bonne chance" farewell may be embellished or misattributed. *(Wikipedia Bretherton / Spaak Committee; pjgoldsmith.com 2017)*
- **Hallstein's camp university:** the man who'll sign for Germany and become the first Commission President spent the end of WWII as a POW at Camp Como, Mississippi, where he organized a "camp university" to teach law to fellow prisoners; he'd later call national sovereignty a "doctrine of yesteryear." *(Wikipedia Walter Hallstein; EPRS_BRI(2019)637982)*
- **Suez made Europe possible:** late Oct–early Nov 1956, Britain and France invade Egypt and are forced into humiliating withdrawal by U.S. financial pressure while the USSR crushes Hungary — both at once. Adenauer and Mollet meet in Paris 5–6 Nov, within days of the ceasefire, and clear the last obstacles to Rome. *(CVCE "Suez Crisis and Hungarian Uprising"; history.state.gov FRUS 1955-57)*
- **Adenauer's deliberate subordination:** he tells the French ambassador Germany cannot lead in foreign policy because of the war — France must lead, Germany will support — which lets France extract better terms than a confident Germany would have conceded. And he overrode his *own* Economics Minister to do it. *(CEJISS CAP article; FRUS)*
- **Erhard fought the EEC from inside the German cabinet** — the architect of the German economic miracle wanted a broad free-trade area, not a protectionist customs union; he and Strauss deadlocked the Saint-Cloud meeting (30 Oct 1956); Adenauer had to override them. *(Wikipedia IGC on Common Market and Euratom; CVCE/FRUS)*
- **Euratom's buried secret:** the public frame was peaceful nuclear power, but declassified documents show France, West Germany, and Italy arranging weapons cooperation under Euratom's cover; Adenauer reportedly wanted "as quickly as possible, the chance of producing our own nuclear weapons." De Gaulle killed the scheme in 1958, insisting the *force de frappe* be purely French — a direct thread into Episode 5. *(Wikipedia French military nuclear programme; Atomic Heritage Foundation)*
- Signing details: 25 March 1957 was also the Catholic feast of the Annunciation; the Capitoline was where Rome's senate met and triumphs were celebrated. *(CVCE signing ceremony; historysphere.com)*

**Named conflict / uncertainty.** Two on offer: (1) *Did Bretherton walk out or was he asked to leave, and is the famous farewell quote real?* Bretherton said he was asked to leave; Spaak said he walked out; the quote comes from a single French witness (Deniau), was later brandished by Delors (1991), and Bretherton's own papers suggest he privately thought Britain could have shaped the whole thing — so the quote may express the Cabinet's mind, not his. (2) *Was the CAP bargain a shared understanding or a deliberate ambiguity France later exploited?* Both live. *(Wikipedia Spaak Committee/Bretherton; pjgoldsmith.com; CEJISS; EH.net)*

**Handover (suspended tension).** The machine is built, and it has a *government* — Walter Hallstein, a man who will receive foreign ambassadors' credentials as though he were a head of state, running a Commission he calls "a kind of European prime minister['s]" office. But in that same year, 1958, a giant returns to power in Paris — a man who believes in France, not "Europe," who has just been humiliated by the idea of a shared nuclear force, and who regards Hallstein's little supranational court with cold fury. The engineers built their machine. Now the most formidable nationalist of the century is going to try to break it — from the inside.

## EPISODE 5 — "The General Against the Machine"
*Roughly 1958 → 1969. The great collision — and the model works uneven pacing hard here.*

**Scope.** Charles de Gaulle versus the supranational project he could neither destroy nor abide: the twin British vetoes, the Élysée Treaty, the Empty Chair Crisis, and the Luxembourg Compromise that froze majority voting for two decades. Covers the paradox that de Gaulle built the EEC's most important machinery (the CAP that saved French farming) while sabotaging its political evolution and decapitating the man who embodied it. **Boundary:** ends with de Gaulle's midnight resignation (April 1969) and the Hague Summit that reopens the door he'd bolted. It does NOT cover the actual first enlargement (1973) — that's Episode 6; here Britain is only *blocked*.

**Opening hook.** A single sentence, delivered at one minute past midnight: "I will cease to exercise my functions as President of the Republic." De Gaulle, beaten in a referendum on regional reform he didn't have to hold, resigns in nine words — and within months the enlargement he had personally vetoed *twice* becomes possible. Open on the paradox: the man who most shaped the European Community spent a decade trying to stop it becoming anything more than a French tool, and only by *leaving* did he set it free.

**Central tension (facet).** This is the counter-argument to the whole series made flesh. If the driving question is whether peace can be engineered top-down past the peoples' consent, de Gaulle is the man who says: *the peoples are the nations, and the nations are real, and your "supranational" court is a fiction dressed in the trappings of sovereignty.* He tests the thesis not by losing but by *winning* — the Luxembourg Compromise hands every nation a veto and freezes the machine for twenty years. The "so what": was de Gaulle a reactionary blocking the inevitable, or the only honest man in the room — the one who insisted that a democracy of Europeans didn't yet exist, and you couldn't conjure one by protocol?

**Ground-level human anchor.** A rare *dual* anchor, and the episode is built on their antagonism: **Charles de Gaulle** and **Walter Hallstein** — the nationalist giant and the law professor who behaves like a prime minister of a country that doesn't exist yet. Follow both; the collision *is* the episode.

**Texture (raw material — carry this episode heavy; it can afford it):**
- **Rambouillet, then the knife:** 15–16 Dec 1962, Macmillan pleads his case to de Gaulle — "England isolated from the Continent since the Reformation… wishes to come out of its isolation" — and leaves thinking a deal is alive; five days later at Nassau he takes American Polaris from Kennedy (the very thing de Gaulle had been *refused*); on 14 Jan 1963 de Gaulle vetoes. Macmillan says he is "staggered and deeply wounded." He never saw it coming. *(Alastair J. Dunn / AJD History blog 2020; CVCE minutes)*
- De Gaulle's astonishingly candid public reasoning in that veto: if Britain joins, others follow, the market dissolves into a free-trade zone, and "in the end there would appear a colossal Atlantic Community under American dependence and leadership which would completely swallow up the European Community." A precise geopolitical prediction, stated aloud as a *reason*. *(de Gaulle press conf. 14 Jan 1963, JFK Library; aei.pitt.edu/5777; Atlantic Council)*
- The Élysée Treaty (Franco-German friendship) signed 22 Jan 1963 — the same *week* as the British veto. Not coincidental: de Gaulle's counter-Europe, built on a Paris–Bonn axis instead of an Atlantic one. *(dossier Era 5)*
- **Hallstein receiving ambassadors:** he takes the formal credentials of foreign ambassadors — a head-of-state ritual — and de Gaulle writes in his memoirs that Hallstein "sat, surrounded with all the trappings of sovereignty," "ardently wedded to the thesis of the super-State." *(Wikipedia Hallstein, citing de Gaulle's memoirs; Der Spiegel via eucmh.com)*
- **The provocation that lit the fuse:** 24 Mar 1965, Hallstein presents his CAP-financing package (which would give the Commission its own revenue and Parliament more budget power) to the *European Parliament* a full week before the Council of Ministers — deliberately building momentum before member states could kill it. Within three months France empties its chair. *(Wikipedia Hallstein Commission; CVCE)*
- **The Empty Chair Crisis:** 30 Jun 1965, Couve de Murville — chairing the Council as France holds the presidency — refuses all compromise and recalls France's Permanent Representative from Brussels. France simply stops showing up, for seven months. *(dossier Era 5)*
- **The Luxembourg Compromise as an agreement to disagree:** 29 Jan 1966, the text says where "very important interests" are at stake the Council will "endeavour to reach solutions which can be adopted by all." France reads it as: unanimity, i.e. a permanent veto. The other five read it as: try for consensus, then proceed by majority. *Both* interpretations are written into the same document and both delegations sign it. It's not a resolution — it's a formal record that the dispute is unresolved, and it paralyzes majority voting for nearly twenty years. *(Wikipedia/CVCE Luxembourg Compromise)*
- **Second veto, gift-wrapped:** 18 Nov 1967 Wilson devalues sterling; nine days later (27 Nov) de Gaulle vetoes Britain again, noting its economy "does not at present belong to Europe." The devaluation handed him the economic argument. *(MoneyWeek; UK in a Changing Europe)*
- **Hallstein, the only casualty:** after the Compromise, the Council refuses to renew his term; he departs 30 Jun 1967 — "the only person to lose his job over what happened." De Gaulle didn't just stall integration; he decapitated the man who embodied it. *(Wikipedia Hallstein Commission)*
- **Pompidou's triptych:** proposing the Hague Summit (July 1969), Pompidou frames it as "completion, deepening and enlargement" — in that exact order. "Completion" = lock in permanent CAP financing (a subsidy to French farmers) *before* Britain gets a seat. Gaullist realpolitik wearing the language of idealism. *(CVCE, Pompidou statement 1 Dec 1969; EBSCO Research Starters)*

**Named conflict / uncertainty.** Two genuine historiographical fights, both worth naming: (1) *Was the 1963 veto really about Nassau/Polaris, or had de Gaulle decided Britain wasn't ready before Nassau, using it as a convenient public pretext?* (Ludlow and others argue the latter.) (2) *Was the Empty Chair Crisis primarily about the CAP/budget money, or about majority voting and sovereignty — with the sovereignty framing as post-hoc cover?* Both are live and not mutually exclusive. *(Cambridge Core "De Gaulle's Race to the Bottom"; ETH Zurich; Academia.edu "Challenging French Leadership"; e-IR; Springer/MIT "Visions, Votes and Vetoes")*

**Handover (suspended tension).** At one minute past midnight the giant walks away, and the door he bolted swings open. Britain — twice-humiliated — is about to be let in. But the Europe Britain is about to join is not the confident engine of the 1950s; it's a machine de Gaulle froze, entering an era of oil shocks and stagnation, about to discover that the harder problem was never getting *in* — it was getting ordinary people to *want* to be there. And the first time Europe asks a whole nation directly, in 1972, the answer is going to be *no*.

## EPISODE 6 — "In, Out, and the Ambivalence of Nations"
*Roughly 1969 → 1988. Enlargement, stagnation, and the first cracks between the project and its publics. (Compressed pacing begins here.)*

**Scope.** Nearly two decades at accelerated pace: the first enlargement (Britain, Ireland, Denmark in; Norway's shock No); "Eurosclerosis" and the oil-shock decade; Thatcher's five-year rebate war; the southern enlargement (Greece, then Spain and Portugal, anchoring new democracies); and the Delors relaunch — the single-market "1992" programme, the Single European Act, and the Bruges backlash. **Boundary:** ends with Thatcher's Bruges Speech (Sept 1988), which lights the fuse of everything in Episode 8. It does NOT cover Maastricht or the euro — that's Episode 7. Referendums here (Norway 1972, UK 1975) are about *membership*, deliberately kept distinct from the *treaty* referendums of Episodes 7–8.

**Opening hook.** September 1972, a near-perfect map of a nation split in two: Oslo votes 67% Yes to join Europe; the fishing counties of the far north — Nordland, Troms, Finnmark — vote 70%+ No. Norway says no to Europe, and Prime Minister Trygve Bratteli, who had staked his government on Yes, keeps his promise and resigns. Open on the first time a whole people looked at the European project and turned it down — and the coastline where they did it.

**Central tension (facet).** The gap the whole series is about starts to *show*. The project's architects keep succeeding at the elite level — enlargement, the single market, treaty engineering — while ordinary Europeans keep signaling, in referendums and empty stadiums, that their hearts aren't in it. The tension: is ambivalence a temporary lag that prosperity will close, or a permanent feature that the top-down method can only paper over? The "so what": Delors converts even Britain's unions in an afternoon — and six weeks later Thatcher answers with a speech that will, decades on, take Britain out entirely. The engineered consensus is real, and it is brittle.

**Ground-level human anchor.** **Jacques Delors** — the French Socialist who arrives (1985) at an institution many think is dying and prescribes two cures at once: the single market for business and the right, the "social dimension" for labour and the left. Strong secondary human beat: **Trygve Bratteli** for the opening; **Margaret Thatcher** as the recurring antagonist whose arc bridges into Episode 8.

**Texture (raw material — pick and choose; more here than the runway needs, by design):**
- **The half-empty Wembley:** Heath's Jan-1973 "Fanfare for Europe" football match ("The Three" vs. "The Six") draws only ~36,500 into a 100,000 stadium — the empty seats become the image of British ambivalence. *(Wikipedia Accession of the UK; Euronews archive 2020)*
- **Norway's fishermen:** the near-perfect urban/rural, Oslo/coast split; Norges Fiskarlag and Norges Råfisklag campaign No over sovereign control of Norwegian waters; Norway will vote No *again* 22 years later for the same reasons. *(Wikipedia 1972 Norwegian referendum)*
- **Wilson suspends collective responsibility (1975):** to hold Labour together for the referendum he lets 7 of 23 cabinet ministers campaign against the government's own position; "Britain in Europe" outspends the No camp roughly £1.5m to under £150k. *(House of Commons Library CBP-7253; Wikipedia 1975 referendum)*
- **What Thatcher actually said:** not "I want my money back" but "We are not asking… for money. We are simply asking to have our own money back." She secured 1,000m ECU for 1984 and a permanent ~two-thirds abatement, treaty-locked "unchangeable without British agreement." (The handbag-slam is likely myth — see conflict.) *(TheConversation; Margaret Thatcher Foundation)*
- **Spain's fleet:** at accession Spain's fishing fleet was larger than *all* existing EEC members' fleets combined — a single fact that froze fisheries talks for years; the accession happened "without any official ceremony other than the raising of the Spanish and Portuguese flags in Brussels." *(Wikipedia 1986 enlargement)*
- **Greece vetoes its own partners:** after joining in 1981, Greece threatens to veto Spain and Portugal unless it gets Integrated Mediterranean Programmes; the gambit wins it ~$2 billion before it drops the veto. *(Wikipedia 1986 enlargement)* — pair with Karamanlis's "We belong to the West," EEC accession as democratic consolidation after the junta. *(Cambridge UP, Byzantine and Modern Greek Studies 2025)*
- **Delors turns the TUC (8 Sept 1988):** Britain's Eurosceptic unions give the French Socialist a *standing ovation* at Bournemouth; Ron Todd's verdict — "the only card game in town… is in a town called Brussels." *(History & Policy; ETUC)*
- **Bruges (20 Sept 1988), twelve days later:** Thatcher — "We have not successfully rolled back the frontiers of the state in Britain, only to see them re-imposed at a European level with a European super-state exercising a new dominance from Brussels." Delors refused to attend; Heath traveled to Brussels to rebut a sitting PM of his own party. *(Wikipedia Bruges speech; Margaret Thatcher Foundation)*
- **Cockfield "went native":** Thatcher sent him to Brussels as a reliable ally; he wrote the forensic ~300-measure White Paper (14 Jun 1985) so comprehensive it couldn't be blocked — and she felt betrayed. *(European Sources Online; CVCE)*

**Named conflict / uncertainty.** Two: (1) *What did Thatcher actually say/do about the rebate?* The "I want my money back" and the handbag-on-the-table are mythologized; her documented words are more measured, and no authoritative transcript of the Dublin 1979 exchange settles it. (2) *Was "Eurosclerosis" a real institutional failure or a neoliberal diagnosis?* Giersch's term blamed over-regulation; Cairn.info (2009) argues the 1970s actually saw significant institutional innovation and that the slump was driven by global shocks (oil, Nixon), with Krugman later noting France's employment eventually beat the U.S. *(Margaret Thatcher Foundation vs. secondary accounts; Giersch/mainstream vs. Cairn.info 2009 vs. Krugman)*

**Handover (suspended tension).** Delors has revived the machine and even seduced its critics — and Thatcher has just declared war on the whole idea. But everything so far has been argument. In November 1989, an actual wall is going to fall in Berlin, and the abstract question of "how much Europe" becomes suddenly, terrifyingly concrete: a single Germany, 80 million strong, at the heart of a continent that spent the century terrified of exactly that. The founders' original nightmare is about to reappear — and the price of taming it, again, will be paid in a currency that doesn't exist yet.

## EPISODE 7 — "The Price of a Country: Reunification and the Euro"
*Roughly 1989 → 2004. The last great top-down gamble.*

**Scope.** How the fall of the Wall forced the deepest integration of all: German reunification traded (explicitly or implicitly) for the single currency; the Maastricht Treaty that created the "European Union" and the referendum revolts that nearly killed it; the birth of the euro; the founders' fiscal rules broken by the founders themselves; and the "Big Bang" eastward enlargement that made the Union continental. **Boundary:** ends around 2004 with the euro in pockets, the Stability Pact already hollowed, and ten new states in — the Union at its high-water mark of confidence. It does NOT cover the eurozone crisis or Brexit — those are the reckoning of Episode 8. It builds the machine that Episode 8 will stress to breaking.

**Opening hook.** July 1990, a dacha in the Caucasus mountains near Stavropol: Helmut Kohl and Mikhail Gorbachev conduct part of the negotiation *outside, in the open air,* and Kohl walks away having bought back his country — capping a united Germany's army at 370,000 and pledging billions of Deutsche Marks to move Soviet troops home. Open on two men under an open sky deciding whether Germany can be one country again, and at what price to the rest of Europe.

**Central tension (facet).** The founders' deepest fear — a dominant Germany — returns, and the answer is the founders' oldest method, at maximum stakes: bind it in so tightly it cannot dominate. Mitterrand's reported logic — you get your country, we get your currency — is the Ruhr bargain of 1950 scaled to an entire monetary system. The tension: a currency union with no fiscal union, no shared budget, no common government — held together by rules its two largest members break within four years — is a bet that the *engineered* solidarity will hold when tested. Maastricht passes by a whisker of the French vote and is *rejected* by Denmark. The "so what": Europe built the most ambitious top-down structure in its history precisely as its peoples were most loudly signaling they hadn't been asked — and it did it anyway, on the theory that they'd come around. Would they?

**Ground-level human anchor.** **Helmut Kohl** — the physically enormous chancellor (6'4", "the Pear" in caricature) who understood German unity had to be *bought with European currency, literally*, and who wept openly when eastern enlargement talks were agreed. Strong secondary: **François Mitterrand**, the old man who remembered occupation and feared a Germany dominant "as in 1913."

**Texture (raw material):**
- **The Caucasus deal:** Kohl to Gorbachev's dacha (not the Kremlin), talks partly outdoors; caps the united German army at 370,000, pledges billions to relocate Soviet forces. *(GHI-DC "Kohl-Gorbachev Talks July 15–16 1990"; Christian Science Monitor 18 Jul 1990)*
- Kohl announces a 10-point reunification plan (28 Nov 1989) *without consulting allies* — the unilateralism that frightened Paris. *(dossier Era 7)*
- **France's near-death Maastricht vote (Sept 1992):** 50.8% Yes to 49.2% No — under 500,000 votes in an electorate of 37 million; Mitterrand had assumed an easy win. *(Wikipedia 1992 French Maastricht referendum; Britannica)*
- **Denmark forces the invention of "opt-outs":** No on 2 Jun 1992 (50.7%); rather than reopen the treaty for twelve, leaders devise the Edinburgh Agreement (Dec 1992) granting Denmark four opt-outs; Denmark then votes Yes (56.8%) in May 1993. The lesson leaders drew: *if a country votes wrong, redesign the question.* *(Wikipedia 1992 Danish referendum; Edinburgh Agreement; EUR-Lex)*
- **The euro almost had another name:** the ECU was the frontrunner but was sunk partly because "écu" is a medieval French coin — un-neutral, too Gallic for the Germans; Chirac called "euro" clumsy ("maladroit"); adopted at Madrid, 16 Dec 1995, precisely because it favored no language or nation. *(EU Council Library blog; Wikipedia History of the euro)*
- **New Year 2002, the queues at the ATMs:** 300+ million people wake to a new currency; many skip New Year parties to withdraw the first notes; within days 96–97% of eurozone ATMs dispense euros; the largest currency changeover in history — 15bn notes, 51bn coins replacing 9bn old notes and 107bn old coins. *(EUR-Lex review; ECB; VOA News)*
- **France and Germany kneecap their own rules (Nov 2003):** both breach the 3% deficit limit of the Stability and Growth Pact and vote to suspend enforcement; Schäuble later concedes the Pact "lost its bite" — telegraphing to every government that the rules were negotiable. *(Wikipedia SGP; SpringerLink; CEPR VoxEU)*
- **Big Bang (1 May 2004):** ten states join at once — the Union goes continental. *(dossier Era 7)*
- Optional human coda: Kohl weeping when eastern enlargement talks are agreed (Dec 1997), per Juncker. *(European Commission Medium post; Bundesregierung archive)*

**Named conflict / uncertainty.** Two, both genuinely unresolved: (1) *Did Mitterrand explicitly condition reunification on the euro, or was it an implicit understanding?* The strong version rests on declassified Mitterrand–Genscher minutes (Der Spiegel via MercoPress 2010) and aide Hubert Védrine's testimony, with Pöhl affirming the structural link — but Schäuble flatly denied any closed-door deal; historians split between "informal understanding" and "explicit transaction." (2) *Did Greece "cheat" its way into the euro (2001), or was it "let in" under rules Eurostat itself tolerated — and is the real scandal the separate Goldman Sachs swap?* Two distinct issues persistently conflated; the Goldman arrangement is far better documented than the entry-falsification charge. *(MercoPress/Der Spiegel; Voxeurop; Tandfonline Cold War History; VoegelinView — and — 2004 Greek Financial Audit; Bureau of Investigative Journalism 2012; INETECONOMICS)*

**Handover (suspended tension).** By 2004 the Union looks unstoppable: a shared currency in 300 million pockets, ten new members, a continent reunified without a shot. But it has built a currency with no country underneath it, written rules it won't enforce, and enlarged and deepened faster than any electorate ever endorsed. The whole edifice now rests on a single unspoken bet — that the engineered solidarity will hold when the first real storm hits. In 2008, the storm is coming. And the bill for sixty years of building peace *at* the peoples rather than *with* them is about to be presented.

## EPISODE 8 — "The Reckoning: Whatever It Takes"
*Roughly 2005 → 2020. The finale. NARROWS and escalates onto the driving question — not a catch-all.*

**Scope.** Deliberately narrow. Three collisions between what the architects built and what the peoples would accept, in escalating order: the rejection of the EU Constitution and its quiet resurrection as the Lisbon Treaty; the eurozone crisis that nearly destroyed the single currency and was saved by eight words from an unelected central banker; and Brexit — the first nation to reverse the whole thing. **Boundary:** the finale does NOT re-narrate Maastricht or the euro's creation (Episode 7) or the earlier membership referendums (Episode 6); it treats them as the debt now being called. It ends on the driving question itself, unresolved.

**Opening hook.** 26 July 2012, a London conference room. Italian and Spanish borrowing costs are climbing toward the level that bankrupted Greece; the euro — 300 million people's money — is days from unraveling. Mario Draghi, president of the European Central Bank, has *no new policy* to announce and hasn't cleared his words with the board. He says: "Within our mandate, the ECB is ready to do whatever it takes to preserve the euro. And believe me, it will be enough." Yields start falling within hours. Open on the moment the entire European project was saved not by a vote, not by a treaty, but by one unelected man's sentence — the top-down method in its purest and most naked form. Then ask what it means that it came to *that.*

**Central tension (facet — the whole series' question, now fully exposed).** Every earlier episode showed the gap between the architects and the peoples as a manageable lag. Here it comes due all at once. The Constitution is written by a convention of elites and rejected outright by French and Dutch voters — then re-packaged as "Lisbon" and pushed through without letting most peoples vote again (Ireland votes No, then is asked again until it says Yes). The euro is saved by a central banker's will, not democratic consent. And Britain, asked directly, votes to leave. The tension is the driving question with nowhere left to hide: *if peace and union can only be preserved by repeatedly overriding or re-asking the peoples who keep saying no, is it still their union — and if it isn't, how long can it last?* The "so what" is left open, because it genuinely is: the same top-down method that made Franco-German war "materially impossible" also made a democratic deficit structural, and the story ends with the ledger open.

**Ground-level human anchor.** **Mario Draghi** for the euro's rescue — the technocrat whose eight words were the whole intervention. Balanced against **David Cameron** for Brexit — the man who, gambling to settle his own party, asked the people the direct question the project had spent sixty years avoiding, and lost.

**Texture (raw material — tight; the finale earns its narrowness):**
- **The Constitution rejected, then reborn as Lisbon:** France rejects the Constitutional Treaty 55% No (29 May 2005), the Netherlands 61% No (1 Jun 2005); its substance returns as the Lisbon Treaty (signed 2007); Ireland rejects Lisbon (53.4% No, Jun 2008), is asked again, and votes Yes (67.1%, Oct 2009) — the Danish/Maastricht "re-ask" pattern, now on a constitution. Giscard d'Estaing, who chaired the drafting convention, was devastated by the rejection *in his own country*. *(Wikipedia Constitutional Treaty / Lisbon Treaty; dossier Era 7)*
- **Greece lights the fuse:** within days of taking office (Oct 2009) Papandreou reveals the real 2009 deficit — over 12%, later revised to 15.4%, against a stated 3.7%; first €110bn bailout (Apr–May 2010); the "Troika." *(Wikipedia Greek government-debt crisis timeline; Market Histories)*
- **Draghi's eight words** (as above) — no new policy, not pre-cleared; OMT only announced in September; the *words themselves* broke the panic. *(ECB verbatim transcript sp120726; Quartz 2017; Brookings)*
- **The Brexit bus:** "We send the EU £350 million a week — let's fund our NHS instead." The UK Statistics Authority said the figure was wrong (net was ~£250m/week); Cummings later called the slogan "one of the main reasons" Leave won; two years on, 42% of those who'd heard it still believed it. *(Wikipedia Vote Leave bus; Conversion Uplift; LBC)*
- Brexit arc, compressed: Cameron's Bloomberg promise (2013) → referendum 23 Jun 2016, 51.89% Leave → Cameron resigns within hours → Article 50 (Mar 2017) → UK formally leaves 31 Jan 2020. *(Wikipedia 2016 referendum; UK Parliament research)*

**Named conflict / uncertainty.** Reuse and sharpen the Greek-entry ambiguity from Episode 7's *forward* thread if desired — but the finale's real "uncertainty" is interpretive and should be voiced as the closing question, not a footnote: historians and citizens genuinely disagree whether the repeated pattern (reject → re-ask, or reject → re-package) is *pragmatic statesmanship saving people from their own worst impulses* or *a democratic deficit that Brexit finally punished* — and the series should refuse to settle it, because it isn't settled. *(supported by the whole referendum record across dossier Eras 6–7 and the Brexit sources above)*

**Final beat (no handover — this is the end).** Return to 1916 and the *Zone Rouge* from Episode 1: land still too poisoned to walk on, a century after the men who died there. Set against it the single, staggering fact the whole series was built to earn — that France and Germany, who bled that ground white three times in seventy years, have now not fired a shot at each other in eighty. Then hold the two truths together and refuse to reconcile them: the engineered peace *held* — it is the most successful peace project in the history of the world — *and* it was built over the heads of the people it was meant to serve, so relentlessly that one of its largest members finally walked out the door. The driving question, restated one last time and left open: when the men who built this are all dead, does a peace that was engineered rather than chosen belong to the people living inside it — and will they keep it?

## SERIES ARC (one line)

From a poisoned battlefield that proves a continent will destroy itself, through the handful of unelected men who engineered a peace nobody voted for and made it hold for eighty years, to the moment the peoples they never asked finally start asking back — ending not with an answer but with the ledger open.
