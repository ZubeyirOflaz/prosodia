## The smell of the ruin {tone: somber, rate: slow}
Picture the continent in the spring of 1945. {pause: 0.8} Not a map — a smell.
Brick dust, and cordite, and something worse underneath it. Cologne is a
photograph of a cathedral standing over a field of rubble that used to be a city.
Warsaw has been erased on purpose, block by block, by demolition crews.

In the space of thirty-one years — from the summer of 1914 to the spring of 1945
— the most powerful, wealthy, scientifically advanced civilization on Earth had
tried to kill itself, twice, and on the second attempt had very nearly succeeded.
{pause: 0.8} Tens of millions were dead. Whole cities were gone.

And here is the thing the people standing in that rubble actually believed — the
thing that is almost impossible to feel from where we sit now. They did not
believe it was over. {pause: 1.0} They believed it might happen *again*.

## Three times in one lifetime {tone: grave}
Because it had a pattern. Think like a Frenchman born in 1900. By the time you
are forty-five years old, Germany has invaded your country three times — 1870,
1914, 1940. {pause: 0.6} Three times, in a single human lifetime.

This is not history to you. This is a recurring event, like a flood that comes
every twenty years and is always worse than the last. So when the guns finally
stop in 1945, the question on the table in Paris and London is not the warm
question we would ask today, *how do we build a united Europe?* The question is
colder, and more frightening. {pause: 0.7} How do we stop the Germans from doing
this a fourth time?

## The bad medicine {tone: measured}
And the traditional answers were all bad. You could try what they tried after the
First World War — crush Germany, strip it, saddle it with reparations, keep it on
the floor. They had tried exactly that, at Versailles, in 1919. It produced a
humiliated, furious nation, mass unemployment, and a man named Hitler who rode
that fury straight into power.

So that medicine had been administered once, and the patient had not recovered.
{pause: 0.5} The patient had come back, and burned the house down.

You could try the opposite — be generous, rebuild Germany, hope it behaves. But a
rebuilt Germany is an industrial Germany, and industry is steel, and steel is
tanks and guns. Every road seemed to lead back to the same place: the Ruhr
valley, full of coal and steel, the dark heart of European war-making, firing up
again.

## The terror in the east {tone: tense}
Now layer on the new terror, the one rising in the east. By 1948 the Soviet Union
has swallowed half the continent. The Iron Curtain has come down — from the
Baltic to the Adriatic. Berlin is blockaded; the Western allies are flying coal
and food into the city around the clock, because Stalin has cut the roads.

Western Europe is broke, hungry, demoralized, and now sitting in the shadow of the
Red Army. And the Americans, who have the money and the bomb, are asking a pointed
question. They will help — but you Europeans have to stop being a collection of
squabbling, bankrupt, vengeful little states. The Marshall Plan comes with
dollars, and with a demand. {pause: 0.6} Cooperate, or we walk.

## The famous men give speeches {tone: wry}
Into this moment step a handful of people who will quietly bend the century. And
they are not, for the most part, the famous men. The famous men give speeches. In
1946, Winston Churchill stands up in Zurich and says something electrifying: "We
must build a kind of United States of Europe." A thrilling line. {pause: 0.5} And
then — nothing happens. Because Churchill means *they* should do it, the
continentals; Britain will cheer from across the Channel.

{tone: measured} The federalists hold a great congress at The Hague in 1948, full
of passion, and what they get for it is the Council of Europe — a debating chamber
in Strasbourg with no real power, where delegates can talk but cannot bind anyone
to anything. So the lesson, by 1949, is brutal. {pause: 0.6} Nations will not
simply vote away their sovereignty around a conference table. The big front door
is locked.

## The strange, indispensable man {tone: measured, note: "a turn toward intrigue; lean in, let curiosity build"}
Which brings us to the strange, indispensable man at the center of this story. His
name is Jean Monnet, and he is almost a joke as a candidate for "father of a
continent." He is not a president, or a general. He is not even an elected
politician — he will never, once in his life, win an election. He is a businessman
from Cognac, in southwest France. His family sells brandy.

But Monnet has a single, uncanny talent. He is a *fixer* — a man who appears at
the elbow of the powerful in moments of crisis and quietly reorganizes how things
work. He has a mind that does not think in terms of speeches, or borders, but in
terms of machinery. What concrete mechanism would make a thing happen — regardless
of what people said they wanted?

## Solidarity by plumbing {tone: measured}
And Monnet had drawn a radical conclusion from all the failure around him. If you
cannot get nations to surrender sovereignty in one grand gesture — and you cannot
— then stop asking them to. {pause: 0.5} Don't aim at the whole. Aim at one small,
specific, vital thing.

Take one sector of the economy — one that two enemies both depend on — and place
it under a shared authority that stands *above* the nations. Make their industries
so physically tangled together that pulling them apart to build weapons becomes
impossible. Solidarity not by treaty of friendship — those are worthless — but
solidarity by plumbing. {pause: 0.4} By welding the pipes together. And then, once
that one small thing works, you do the next thing, and the next. Build the United
States of Europe not with a constitution, but with — of all things — coal and
steel.

## The secret draft {tone: tense}
In the first months of 1950, Monnet and a tiny team lock themselves away and
begin, in total secrecy, to draft a proposal. They tell almost no one. They have
one shot, one audience that matters, and a window of weeks before the great powers
meet and the moment passes.

They are going to take the two materials that had armed every German invasion of
France — coal and steel — and propose handing control of them, French and German
together, to a body that neither government will fully control.

## They have one in mind {tone: dramatic, rate: slow}
Now imagine you are a French official who gets wind of this. Your country has been
invaded by Germany three times in living memory. The war ended *five years ago*.
The bodies are barely cold. {pause: 0.8} And someone is about to propose that
France voluntarily merge its war industries with Germany's.

They are going to need a politician brave enough — or reckless enough — to put his
name on it, and say it out loud. {pause: 0.8} They have one in mind.
