## The lock   {tone: curious, note: "cold open on a concrete object; unhurried; lead the listener into the thought experiment, don't announce it"}

Before you go to sleep tonight, you will almost certainly lock your door.

Most of us do it without a thought — a reflex at the end of the day, a turn of the wrist, and then we forget it. But stop on that small motion for a second, because it's a confession. That lock is a statement about what you believe about the people around you. Not that they're wicked. You probably like your neighbors. You'd help them jump a dead battery; they'd take in a package for you. And still — you lock the door. Because you know, in some quiet, unsentimental part of yourself, that between you and them there stands something you're counting on without ever naming it. A police force you could call. Courts that would hear you. A whole apparatus of law that makes it *not worth it* for anyone to come through that door in the night. The lock isn't really the thing keeping you safe. The lock is a token. The real thing is the enormous, invisible machine standing behind it.

So let me take the machine away. {pause: 0.8}

Not your neighbors — they stay. Not the houses, the streets, the tools, the knives in the kitchen drawer. All of that stays exactly where it is. But tonight, quietly, every authority above you dissolves. No police to call — there is no police. No court to appeal to. No government, no shared judge over anyone, nobody with the recognized right to step between you and the man across the fence and say *stop.* It's just you, your locked door, your neighbors, and one new piece of knowledge that everyone now shares: there is no one coming. Whatever happens on this street tonight, happens. There is no referee left in the world.

Now sit in your house and think. {pause: 1.0}

## Strike first   {tone: measured, note: "build the diffidence logic in the second person; make the listener feel THEMSELVES reason toward violence; the fear is rational, not evil"}

You are not a violent person. Hold onto that, because it's the whole point.

You have no desire to hurt anyone. You'd like to get through the night, keep what's yours, be left alone. But here is the thought that arrives, uninvited, some time after midnight. Your neighbor — the decent one, the one with the dead battery — is lying awake too. And he's thinking about *you.* He's thinking: my neighbor has food, has tools, has a family to protect, and there's no law anymore. What if he decides that the safest move, with no one to stop him, is to come take what I have before I can take what he has? What if he strikes first?

And you realize, with a cold feeling, that he's right to worry — because you've been having the exact same thought about *him.* {pause: 0.8}

Follow it all the way down, because this is the trap, and it's airtight. Even if you have no intention of attacking anyone, you cannot be *sure* your neighbor is as peaceful as you are. And he can't be sure about you. And you both know that the person who moves first — who strikes while the other one is still hoping it won't come to that — has an enormous advantage. So the peaceful thing to do, the thing you'd *want* to do, becomes the stupid thing to do. Because if you wait, and he doesn't, you're finished. The only rational move, for a reasonable person who simply wants to survive, is to get him before he gets you.

And every single person on the street is running that same calculation tonight. Not because they're monsters. Because they're *afraid,* and because fear, in a world with no referee, is the most reasonable thing there is. {pause: 0.8}

So — how long? Honestly. How long before you slide the knife off the counter and into your coat, and go check on your neighbor before he checks on you? {pause: 1.2}

That fear — not evil, not cruelty, just the clean cold logic of frightened people with no one above them — that fear is the whole problem. And the man who saw it most clearly gave the situation you're sitting in right now a name. He called it the *state of nature.* And he said that in it, human life falls into a condition he described in five words that have never stopped echoing: a war of every man against every man.

## Competition, diffidence, glory   {tone: lucid, note: "NAME the doctrine as payoff; define state of nature plainly; the three causes; war as disposition (weather analogy); the famous line lands here"}

His name was Thomas Hobbes, and we'll get to his life, because it matters enormously — this was not an armchair fantasy for him; he lived inside the collapse he's describing. But stay with the idea one more beat, because he built it with the precision of a geometry proof, and every piece of it is doing work.

The *state of nature,* for Hobbes, is not a real historical time. He's not claiming there was a date on the calendar when cavemen roamed around murdering each other. It's a thought experiment — exactly the one you just ran. It asks a single, stripped-down question: take away all government, all common power, everything we built — what are human beings, left to themselves? What's underneath the machine?

And his answer is that three things drive us to each other's throats. First, *competition* — we want the same scarce things, the food and the land and the tools, and there isn't enough to go around, so we fight to take. Second — and this is his deepest and most original insight — *diffidence.* That's an old word, and it doesn't mean shyness here; it means distrust, wariness, *fear.* Even the man who wants nothing of yours has to worry that you want what's his, and so he has reason to strike pre-emptively, to master others before they can master him. Fear alone, with no greed at all, is enough to start the war. And third, *glory* — we fight over reputation, over a word, a look, a slight; over being disrespected. A trifle, Hobbes says, is enough — a different opinion, and above all any sign of being undervalued. {pause: 0.8}

Now here's the subtle part, the part people miss when they picture the state of nature as constant, nonstop bloodshed. Hobbes says war doesn't mean everyone is actually fighting all the time. War, he says, is like weather. Foul weather doesn't mean it's raining every minute — it means that for a stretch of days, rain is always *possible,* the sky is set that way, and you'd be a fool to plan a journey without reckoning on it. That's what the war of all against all is: not perpetual battle, but the perpetual, *known disposition* to battle, with no assurance to the contrary. Everyone, always, one bad night from violence, and everyone knowing it.

And what does that do to a life? Listen to what he says vanishes — because it's not just safety that goes. There's no industry, he says, because no one can be sure of keeping what they produce, so why produce? No farming, no shipping, no building, no arts, no letters, no knowledge, no account of time. And — this is the ledger — no society. Just fear. And then the sentence, the one that people who have never read a word of philosophy still somehow carry around with them. In the state of nature, Hobbes wrote, the life of man is "solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short." {pause: 1.0}

Solitary. Poor. Nasty. Brutish. And short. That is what we are, he's telling you, once you strip the machine away. That is the floor.

## The animal we are not   {tone: measured, note: "STAGE THE CLASH vs Aristotle (Ep3 callback); steelman the natural-whole view, then Hobbes's demolition; society as artifact"}

Now, if that made something in you object — if a voice in the back of your head said *that's not right, people aren't like that, we naturally band together* — then congratulations, because you are Aristotle, and you've just walked into the oldest and deepest argument in this whole series.

Go back with me a few episodes, to the Greek who told us that man is by nature a political animal. For Aristotle, the city wasn't a machine bolted on top of frightened individuals. It was *natural* — as natural as the acorn becoming the oak. Human beings, he said, grow toward community the way a plant grows toward the light, because it's only inside a shared life, sharing in ruling and being ruled, that we become fully what we are. The person who could live outside the city, needing no one, was in Aristotle's chilling phrase either a beast or a god — but not a man. On this view, society isn't an escape from our nature. Society *is* our nature, coming into flower. That idea ruled the Western imagination for nearly two thousand years.

And give it its due, because it's beautiful and it's not obviously wrong. We *are* social creatures. Babies die without care. We ache in isolation. We fall in love, we make families, we sing together. Doesn't it feel truer to say we're built for togetherness than to say we're a mob of paranoid loners held apart only by the threat of the gallows? {pause: 0.8}

Hobbes says: no. And he says it with a bluntness that's still bracing. That warm picture, he thinks, is a fairy tale — and worse, a dangerous one, because it hides the truth we most need to see. Look again, he says, at how you actually behave. You lock your door — even against your friends. You travel armed, or you would, if the law weren't watching. When you ride out you carry a weapon; when you sleep you bolt the doors; even in your own house you lock your chests. And you do this in a country with police, and judges, and laws to punish theft! What opinion of your fellow human beings, Hobbes asks, do you confess by these actions, as much as I do by my words? You already know what people are. You've built your whole life around knowing it. You've just never been honest enough to say it out loud.

So for Hobbes, the city is not the oak that was always inside the acorn. The city is a machine. An artifact. Something frightened, self-interested individuals *build,* deliberately, out of nothing but calculation, because the alternative is the war. It is not natural to us. It is the greatest and most artificial thing we have ever made — precisely because it's the thing that saves us from our nature. And that single reversal, man as builder-of-society rather than creature-of-society, is the doorway into the entire modern age. Everything the rest of this series argues about — rights, consent, revolution, the whole modern conversation — happens on the far side of that door. Hobbes is the one who opened it.

But to understand *why* a man would look at humanity and see the war before he saw anything else, you have to understand where he was standing when he looked.

## The twin of fear   {tone: measured, note: "human anchor; the Armada birth story (hedge the wording as his own account); the Civil War; frame biography honestly"}

There's a story Hobbes told about his own birth, and whether he polished it over the years or not, it's the truest thing he ever said about himself.

He was born in the spring of 1588 — the year the Spanish Armada was bearing down on England, the year the whole country lay awake waiting for invasion, for the ships that might carry an army to burn it all down. And Hobbes liked to say — the exact words come down to us secondhand, through his own later account, so hold the phrasing lightly — that his mother was so frightened by the rumors of the coming Spanish fleet that she gave birth to him early, in her terror. That fear brought him into the world before his time. He put it, more or less, like this: that his mother brought forth twins that day — himself, and fear. That he and dread were born together. {pause: 0.8}

Take it as legend if you like. But it's the perfect key to the man, because Hobbes built an entire philosophy on the conviction that fear is not our weakness. Fear is our teacher. Fear is the one passion clear-eyed enough to lead us out of the war — because fear of death is the thing that finally makes us reasonable. Most thinkers treat fear as the enemy of good sense. Hobbes treats it as the beginning of it.

And then the fear he was born with came true, on a national scale, right in front of him. Because Hobbes lived to watch his own country do the unthinkable. He watched England fall into civil war — Parliament against the King, region against region, sect against sect, the whole intricate machine of a settled society coming apart in his hands. He watched neighbors take up arms against neighbors over religion and power. And he watched it build toward the single event that, for a man of his time and temperament, was very nearly the end of the world: in January of 1649, the people of England took their anointed king, Charles the First — the sovereign, the very keystone of the arch — and they cut off his head in public. {pause: 1.0}

You have to feel what that meant to someone like Hobbes. Not a policy dispute. Not a change of government. The deliberate, ceremonial destruction of the sovereign himself — the thing that was supposed to be *above* the fight, the referee, the one power that held everyone else in awe. And when that fell, what came pouring through the gap was exactly the war he'd theorized. Not weather anymore. The storm itself.

## The first who ran   {tone: measured, note: "the exile years; fled Paris 1640, returned 1651; frame as living the statelessness he theorized; Leviathan as disorder's answer"}

Hobbes did not stay to watch it up close. He ran.

In 1640, as the temperature rose and a Parliament gathered that he could see was going to come for men like him — men who had written in defense of royal power — Thomas Hobbes fled to Paris. And he was rather proud of the timing. By his own account he was the first of all who ran, the earliest to read the signs and get out. He would stay in exile for eleven years.

Think about what that means for the ideas. Here is a man writing about the terror of a world without a settled, protecting authority — and he's writing it as a refugee. Stateless. Cut off from home, watching from across the Channel as his country tore itself apart, living in the one condition his whole philosophy is built to dread. The state of nature was not an abstraction he cooked up in a comfortable study. It was the news from home.

And in Paris, in the middle of that exile, in 1651, he published the book. He called it *Leviathan* — after the great sea-monster of the Book of Job, the most powerful and terrifying creature God ever made, the beast no man can master. *Leviathan* is the answer to disorder. It's a man who has seen the machine of society smashed, sitting down to ask the most basic question there is: how do you build the machine in the first place? What could possibly make a mob of frightened, dangerous, self-interested people hold together at all?

And then — the detail that tells you he meant every word of his own theory. At the end of 1651, with the King dead and the royalists defeated and a new republican government, Cromwell's Commonwealth, in power in England, Thomas Hobbes went *home.* He submitted to the very regime that had won the war he'd fled. Made his peace with the victors. His old royalist friends in exile were scandalized. But Hobbes, as we'll see, had a theory that made his homecoming not a betrayal but a *duty* — and understanding why is understanding the coldest and most shocking thing he ever argued. First, though, we have to watch him build his way out of the war.

## Covenants and swords   {tone: lucid, note: "build the escape in layers; right of nature, first law (seek peace), laying down the right to all things; then 'covenants without the sword are but words'"}

So you're back in your dark house, knife in your coat, everyone on the street a threat. How do you get out?

Hobbes says the way out starts with fear itself, and with reason working on fear. Because even in the war, human beings can think. And reason looks at the war of all against all and says: this is intolerable, everyone loses, even the strong die in their beds eventually. Reason discovers a rule — Hobbes calls it the first and fundamental law of nature — and the rule is simply this: *seek peace.* Get out of the war if you possibly can.

But how? Here's the diagnosis. In the state of nature, Hobbes says, every person has a *right to everything* — literally everything, including one another's bodies. Not because it's granted, but because in the war, with no law, there's nothing to stop you claiming anything you can grab; your right extends as far as your strength. And that universal right to everything is exactly the problem. If everyone has a right to everything, then no one has a secure right to *anything.* My claim to my own life runs headlong into your claim to it. The right to everything is worth nothing.

So reason says: peace requires that you give something up. Each person must be willing to lay down this boundless right to all things — to step back from the grab — *on one condition.* And the condition is everything. You lay down your right only if, and as far as, everyone else lays down theirs too. A mutual stepping-back, all at once, each surrendering the same right in the same measure. Hobbes calls this mutual transfer of right a *covenant.* It's the founding act. It's the moment the machine could switch on. {pause: 0.8}

But now watch him do the thing that separates him from every dreamer who ever imagined a peaceful contract. Because you might think: fine, we all promise to lay down our arms, we shake hands, we're done. And Hobbes says — are you *serious?* A promise? Out here? Go back to the diffidence. If I disarm because you promised to disarm, but I can't be sure you actually will, then I'm the fool who trusted, and you're the one who strikes first. A promise that isn't enforced is just noise, and everyone knows it, so no one will disarm, so the covenant dies the instant it's made. And here Hobbes writes one of the hardest, most clear-eyed sentences in all of political thought. Covenants, he says — covenants without the sword — are but words. Words, and of no strength to secure a man at all. {pause: 1.0}

*Covenants, without the sword, are but words.* A contract that nothing enforces isn't a contract. It's a wish. So if the covenant is going to be worth more than the breath it's spoken with, there has to be a sword. There has to be some power — real, terrible, credible power — standing over all the parties, able to punish the one who breaks faith, so that keeping the covenant becomes the *rational* choice for everyone, the way breaking it was rational in the war. You don't escape the state of nature by everyone becoming trustworthy. You escape it by building something that makes trustworthiness *pay.*

And that something is the most consequential invention in the book.

## The mortal god   {tone: grave, note: "the sovereign; the frontispiece image (real, canonical); WHY absolute, WHY undivided (divided power = civil war, lived); legitimacy = protection alone"}

Here is the move. Every person in the covenant does more than lay down the right to all things. They hand it over. Each of them says to all the others: I authorize and give up my right of governing myself to this one will — to this man, or this assembly — on condition that you all do the same. Every one of them makes a single person, or a single body, the *author* of all their actions. They don't just agree to obey. They agree that the sovereign's will shall count *as their own.* The sovereign acts, and it's as if they each acted.

And out of that act, Hobbes says, something is born that has never existed before. A single artificial person, a single will, assembled out of the surrendered wills of a whole multitude. He calls it the *Leviathan.* And in one of the most famous images ever put on the cover of a book, he had it engraved: a colossal giant rising up over the land, crowned, holding a sword in one hand and the crook of spiritual authority in the other — and when you look closely at the giant's body, at its chest and arms, you see it's made of people. Hundreds of tiny individuals, packed together, all of them facing inward, toward the giant's head, *becoming* the giant. That's the commonwealth. That's us, having pooled ourselves into one enormous person who can do what no one of us could do alone: keep the peace. Above the giant runs a line from the Book of Job — there is no power on earth to compare with him. Hobbes calls this creature a *mortal god,* under the immortal God — the great artificial man we build to save ourselves from ourselves. {pause: 0.8}

And now come the two features that make Hobbes Hobbes — the two words that made his name a scandal. The sovereign he's just built must be *absolute,* and it must be *undivided.* And you have to understand why, because they are not the ravings of a man who loves tyranny. They are the desperate conclusions of a man who has watched the alternative burn his country to the ground.

Start with *undivided.* Why can't we split the sword — give some power to a king, some to a parliament, some to the courts, some to the church, and let them check one another? Because Hobbes has just watched exactly that experiment end in a river of blood. Divided sovereignty, to him, isn't a safeguard against tyranny. It's the seed of civil war. If the King has some of the ultimate authority and Parliament has some of it, then the day they disagree — and they will disagree — there is *no one above them* to settle it. Each claims to be right, each commands the subjects' obedience, and the subjects are torn in half. A divided sovereign isn't a balanced government. It's two swords in one house, waiting for the day they cross. And when they cross, you're right back in the state of nature, only now with armies. So the whole point of the sovereign is to be the *one* place the buck finally stops — the single, final judge over every dispute, including the dispute about who's in charge. Split that, and you've built nothing. {pause: 0.8}

And *absolute,* for the same iron reason. Suppose we say: the sovereign rules, *but* only within limits, and if the sovereign oversteps, we the people get to judge him and resist. It sounds reasonable — it sounds like every decent instinct you have. But Hobbes springs the trap. Who decides when the sovereign has overstepped? If the answer is *the sovereign decides,* then the limit is meaningless. And if the answer is *you decide* — each subject judging for himself whether the ruler has gone too far — then you have not limited the sovereign at all. You have simply put the final judgment back in the hands of every individual, which is precisely, *exactly,* the state of nature you were trying to escape. A limited sovereign is a contradiction. The moment there's a power that can judge and overrule the sovereign, *that* power is the real sovereign — and if that power is all of us, individually, then there is no sovereign, and the war is back. So there can be no appeal above him, no law he's bound by, no contract he can breach — because he never signed the covenant in the first place; the covenant was among the *subjects,* to create him. The sovereign is a party to nothing. He is the sword the parties made.

## Protection and nothing else   {tone: contemplative, note: "the protection-only legitimacy; the mutual relation of protection and obedience; the low, clean, godless foundation"}

Now — before that curdles into pure horror, I want to show you the strange, cold beauty of it, because there's something genuinely radical hiding inside all this apparent worship of power.

Ask the oldest question in this series: what makes authority *legitimate?* What gives a ruler the *right* to rule you, and not just the might? For a thousand years the answers reached upward — because God ordained him, because he descends from the right bloodline, because he governs justly according to a natural law written into the cosmos. Authority came down from heaven. Remember Aquinas, a few episodes back, with his great ladder of law running from the human magistrate all the way up to the eternal reason of God. Legitimacy was a thing you inherited from *above.*

Hobbes throws every bit of that away. His sovereign needs no divine right, no noble blood, no cosmic sanction, no natural law grading his performance. The legitimacy of the sovereign rests on one thing and one thing only: *protection.* He is legitimate because, and only because, and exactly as long as, he keeps you safe. The whole reason you built him, the entire content of the deal, was to escape the war — so his authority is nothing more than the answer to that need. Hobbes says it flatly: the obligation of subjects to the sovereign lasts as long, and no longer, than the power lasts by which he is able to protect them. Protection and obedience are two ends of one rope. He shields you; you obey him. The day he can no longer shield you — the day the war comes back despite him — the rope is cut, the deal is void, and you're free, because you're back in the state of nature and must fend for yourself again. {pause: 0.8}

Do you see how clean it is? How *modern?* Hobbes has built the entire edifice of political authority out of nothing but self-interest. No God required. No natural hierarchy. No claim that the ruler is wiser or holier or better than you. Just this: frightened people, doing math, deciding that any master is better than the war, and building one. It is the leanest possible answer to the question of legitimacy — power is justified when, and because, it delivers safety, full stop. And that answer is still, right now, doing enormous work in how you think about your own government, whether you know it or not. When you say a state that can't keep its people safe has failed at its one basic job — that's Hobbes talking. He handed us that idea.

But the same cold logic that makes the theory so clean is about to make it monstrous. Because if protection is the *only* thing that legitimates power — then it doesn't much matter *how* the protector got the job.

## The conqueror's right   {tone: tense, note: "institution vs acquisition; the usurper legitimate the moment he protects; commit-then-complicate; recoil; tie to Hobbes going home to the Commonwealth"}

Hobbes says there are two ways a sovereign comes to be. And the second one is where the listener recoils.

The first he calls sovereignty by *institution* — and that's the story we've just told. People, terrified of one another, come together and mutually agree to set a power over themselves. They build the Leviathan by consent, out of fear of *each other.* Clean origin, or as clean as it gets.

But the second way he calls sovereignty by *acquisition.* And this is when the sovereign gets his power by *force.* A conqueror sweeps in. A usurper seizes the state. An army wins, and stands over you, sword drawn, and offers you the plainest deal in the world: submit, and live. Obey me, and I will not kill you. And you — being a reasonable person who would rather live — you submit. {pause: 0.8}

And here is the blade. Hobbes says: those two sovereigns are *equally legitimate.* Exactly equally. There is no difference in their right — none — because the foundation of both is identical. Look closely, he says: in both cases you're consenting out of *fear.* When you institute a sovereign, you consent because you're afraid of your neighbors. When you submit to a conqueror, you consent because you're afraid of *him.* But it's consent either way — a rational choice to accept a master rather than face death — and fear-driven consent is the only kind there ever is. So the conqueror who protects you has every bit as much right to your obedience as the king your ancestors freely chose. Might, the moment it provides protection and receives your submission, simply *becomes* right. {pause: 1.0}

Feel how that lands. Because your whole moral intuition wants to scream that there's a difference — that the legitimate king and the armed usurper are not the same thing, that one has a *right* and the other only has *power.* And Hobbes looks at you and says: what difference, exactly? If the usurper keeps the peace, ends the war, protects your life — he is doing the one job that legitimates *anyone.* And if you say he's illegitimate because he took power by force, then look back honestly at how *every* state that has ever existed actually began. Not with a pretty covenant on a village green. With a conquest. With force, congealed over time into something we later dressed up as legitimate. Hobbes just refuses the costume. Protection is the coin. Whoever pays it, rules by right.

And *now* you see why Thomas Hobbes could go home. The King was dead; the royalists had lost; Cromwell's Commonwealth held the power and kept the peace. By Hobbes's own theory, the moment the new regime could actually protect him, it had a rightful claim to his obedience — and clinging to a defeated king who could no longer protect anyone was not loyalty, it was folly. So he made his peace with the victors and sailed home, and his scandalized royalist friends called it betrayal, and Hobbes, if you follow his logic, had simply done exactly what his philosophy required. He'd honored the only master that matters: not the man on the throne, but the power that keeps you alive. {pause: 0.8}

If that unsettles you — good. It should. And it turns out it unsettled Hobbes too, in one specific place — a place where his own iron logic springs a leak that he could not, or would not, seal.

## The one thing you cannot sell   {tone: contemplative, note: "the Achilles' heel; true liberties of subjects; inalienable self-defense; the condemned criminal; KEEP UNRESOLVED — contested since his day"}

Everything we've built points one direction: you give it *all* up. Every right, laid down at the sovereign's feet, no appeal, no limit, no take-backs. Absolute means absolute.

Except. There's a hole in the wall. And Hobbes himself dug it. {pause: 0.8}

Because push the logic of the covenant back to its root and ask: *why* did you enter it in the first place? To survive. To escape violent death. Self-preservation is the entire engine — it's the whole reason a rational person would ever hand power to a Leviathan at all. And that means self-preservation is the one thing you *cannot* be surrendering when you covenant, because surrendering it would defeat the only purpose of covenanting. You cannot rationally give away the very thing you're trying to protect. It would be like signing a contract whose sole purpose is your safety, in which clause one is *and I agree to let you kill me.* Incoherent.

So Hobbes admits — grants it outright — that there are what he calls the *true liberties of subjects.* Rights that survive even inside the absolute state, that no covenant can strip away, because no rational person could ever have transferred them. And the deepest of them is the right to defend your own life. If a man comes to kill you — even lawfully, even the sovereign's own officer — you are not obligated to hold still for it. You may resist. Hobbes says it plainly: if the sovereign commands a man to kill himself, or not to resist those who assault him, that man has the liberty to disobey. He goes further, and it's genuinely startling coming from the great theorist of absolute obedience: a criminal being led to a *just* execution — a man the sovereign has every right to put to death — may *still* fight the guards, still struggle, still try to live. Not because the sentence is unjust. It's perfectly just. But because no man can be obligated to walk calmly to his own death. The condemned man's right to resist and the executioner's right to kill him both stand, at once, in full force. {pause: 1.0}

Now stop and see what he's just done to his own building. He spent the whole book telling you that the sovereign's power must be *absolute* — total, unlimited, unresistable — because any crack, any retained right of resistance, lets the war back in. And then, at the foundation, he admits there's a right the sovereign can *never* touch, a right every subject keeps, a right to say *no* to the state at the ultimate moment. People have called this the Achilles' heel of the whole system — the fatal weak point in the invulnerable warrior. Because if you keep the right to resist the executioner, then the sovereign's power was never really absolute, was it? There's a limit after all. And once there's *one* limit, a limit written into the very logic of self-preservation, why not others? What about resisting when the sovereign starves you? Endangers you? Sends you to a war that'll get you killed? Where, exactly, does the right of self-defense stop?

I'm not going to resolve that for you, because it has never been resolved. This tension — between Hobbes the theorist of absolute power and Hobbes who could not deny the individual's inalienable right to save his own skin — has been argued over since his own lifetime, and the argument is still live. Some read the true liberties as a trivial exception that leaves the sovereign essentially all-powerful. Others read them as a hidden bomb in the basement, a seed of individual rights that will grow up to blow absolutism apart. What matters, for tonight, is that Hobbes — the hardest, coldest builder of state power in the whole tradition — could not build a wall high enough to keep the frightened, self-preserving individual entirely inside it. Something in us, even in his system, will not be governed all the way down.

## Is it the only door   {tone: contemplative, note: "the central live question; commit-then-complicate on absolute+undivided; the vise; who protects you from the protector"}

So let's put the real question on the table, the one this whole episode has been circling. To escape the war of all against all — must we do what Hobbes says? Must the sovereign be *absolute,* and *undivided?* Is unlimited power really the only alternative to chaos?

And I want to be fair to him first, genuinely fair, because it's easy to dismiss Hobbes as a bootlicker and it's a mistake. Let me commit you to his side for a moment. You've felt the war — you sat in that dark house, and you know the pull to strike first is *real,* and you know that a world with no final referee is a world that eats itself. And Hobbes's central warnings are not paranoid. Divided sovereignty really did tear England apart; when two powers each claim the last word, the disagreement really does have nowhere to go but the battlefield. And a general right of resistance — everyone free to decide for himself when the ruler has gone too far — really can slide into permanent rebellion, every faction certain it's the one being wronged. Hobbes watched *exactly* that happen. He's not imagining the danger. He buried friends in it. {pause: 0.8}

So hold his position honestly. Given the horror of the war, isn't a hard master better than no master? Isn't *any* order better than the abyss? Say yes for a second and mean it.

Now let me change the one thing that makes it break. You've agreed that any master beats the war. But look at what you've agreed to. You've built a sovereign who is absolute, unlimited, answerable to no one, above every law, who cannot break his contract because he never made one, whom you may never resist except at the very knife's edge of your own execution. You built him to protect you. But you've given him so much power that *nothing now protects you from him.* The sovereign can tax you into ruin, silence you, imprison you, send you to die in his wars — and short of literally trying to kill you on the spot, you owe him obedience, because the alternative is the war. Hobbes's answer to *who protects you from your neighbor* is airtight. His answer to *who protects you from the protector* is: no one, and you'd better hope he's decent, because you've disarmed yourself against him forever. {pause: 1.0}

There's the vise, and I don't think Hobbes ever fully escaped it. On one side, the war — real, total, unbearable. On the other side, a cure that hands one man or one assembly a power so complete that if it turns predatory, you have traded a war of all against all for a war of all against *one,* and lost even the right to fight back. Hobbes stares at those two options and says: take the sovereign, every time, because the war is worse than any tyrant. And the question that will drive the *next* three centuries of this argument is whether he was right that those are the only two doors. Whether it's really *absolute power or chaos* — or whether there's a third thing he couldn't see, or wouldn't: a government strong enough to keep the peace and yet *limited,* bound, held accountable, resistable, without collapsing back into the war.

## The cleared ground, rebuilt   {tone: grave, note: "ENDING — step from referee to advocate; bookend the lock; recast as beginning; handover to Locke (Ep10) — the trade was a fraud, we walk in already owning something"}

Let me come back to the lock on your door. {pause: 0.8}

We started there — with that small nightly confession that you're counting on an invisible machine to keep the peace. And what Hobbes has given you, across this whole argument, is the most honest account anyone had ever written of *why that machine has to exist.* Strip it away, and the war comes. That much I think he simply got right, and I don't want to be cute about it: the people who sneer at Hobbes have usually never had to live a single night in the state of nature, and he did — he watched his country fall into it. He is right that order is not natural and not free, that it's a thing we build against our own worst logic, and that a power capable of keeping the peace is the most precious artifact human beings possess. Anyone who forgets that is a child playing in the ruins of something braver people built. So — where do I come down? I come down with Hobbes on the *problem,* completely. The war is real. The lock confesses a truth.

But I come down against him on the *price.* Because I don't believe those are the only two doors. I don't believe the choice is really absolute, unlimited, unaccountable power on one side and the abyss on the other — and I think Hobbes half-knew it, because he's the one who left that crack in the wall, that stubborn right of the individual to say *no* even to the sovereign, even at the end. He built a fortress of absolute power and then couldn't quite brick up the last window. Something in the frightened individual survives, uncovenanted, un-surrendered — and I think that something is the beginning of the real answer he couldn't reach. {pause: 0.8}

And here is where the argument gets handed on — because someone was about to reach for exactly that. Hobbes's subjects trade away *everything.* Every right, every claim, every scrap of self-governance, all of it poured into the Leviathan, and back you get one thing only: your bare life, protected. That's the deal. Total surrender, for safety.

A generation later, an English doctor — a quiet, careful man who would also spend years in political exile, watching his own country convulse — looked at that deal and said: *it's a fraud.* We never agreed to that. We *couldn't* have, and we didn't, and no rational person ever would. Because we do not walk into society naked and desperate, with nothing but our fear, willing to trade it all for a master. We walk in *already owning things* — a life, a liberty, and something we made with our own hands that no king and no covenant and no Leviathan ever gave us, and that therefore no sovereign gets to simply take. He called them *rights.* Natural rights. Things you carry into the deal, and keep, and can hold up against the sovereign himself. And if that's true — if we come to the table already owning something — then the whole calculation changes. Then a government that violates what you brought with you hasn't just governed badly. It has broken the deal. And you might, just might, have the right to take your power back. {pause: 0.8}

Hobbes rebuilt order on the ground Machiavelli cleared — a whole political world raised up out of nothing but fear and reason, with no help from heaven. It's a magnificent, terrible building, and half the modern world lives in it still. Next time, we meet the man who walked into that building, looked at the absolute sovereign towering in the middle of it, and started quietly pulling out the foundation stones — the man who took Hobbes's own tools, the state of nature and the contract, and used them to argue for the exact opposite conclusion: not absolute power, but limited government; not obedience unto death, but the right to resist. His name was John Locke. And the argument they never had, across the years that separated them, is the argument we are still having about you, and your rights, and the lock on your door. {pause: 0.6} Stay with me.
