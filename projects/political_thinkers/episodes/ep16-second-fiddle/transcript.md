## Below the street   {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "cold open on the descent itself — no city, no date, no person until the room has landed; concrete, unhurried; the word 'murder' is the payoff of the beat"}

Through a covered passage off the main road, barely wide enough for one person at a time, and into a court. Not a street: a small yard, closed on all four sides by the backs of houses, with one privy in the corner serving every family in it.

And then down. Because the room you have come to look at is below the level of the road.

Three or four steps, and the floor. Earth, or broken brick over earth. One window at pavement height, if there is a window. When the weather turns, water comes up through the floor and stands there, and when it goes down it leaves the walls wet, and the walls stay wet, because nothing in that room has ever been dry. Bedding on the ground. Several people. A family, and very often a lodger the family has taken in, because rent has to come from somewhere. No clean water — water comes from a standpipe or a pump shared by the whole district, and what it has run past to get there does not bear thinking about.

This is Manchester, an evening in the early eighteen-forties. And above the roofline, over the entire city, is the coal smoke of the most concentrated industrial district on the face of the earth.

Two people have just come down those steps. One is a young man in expensive clothes. The other is a woman who knows the way — she is Irish, she works in a factory, and she has brought him to a part of the city he could not have found on his own, and would not be let into if he turned up by himself.

The people in that room are not idle. That is the thing to hold onto. They work. The adults are in the mills, and the children are in the mills, and in the morning they will get up off that floor and walk to a building full of machines and stay in it for twelve hours, and come back, and sleep on that floor again.

And they die early. Not of anything exotic or interesting. Fever. Dysentery. The lungs. The children especially — the children die at a rate that, in districts like this one, is simply expected, factored in, unremarkable. A working man in that city can expect to be dead a great many years before the gentleman who owns the mill he works in. Decades before.

Now hold that room in your mind and put something next to it.

Two miles away in the same city, at the same hour, there is more capital assembled than in any comparable space in human history. There are engineers who could drain that court. There are surveyors who know exactly where the water is coming from. There is a corporation with the legal power to act, and there are private fortunes large enough to pay for it out of pocket without noticeably changing anyone's dinner. The knowledge exists. The money exists. The technology exists. None of it arrives.

So a child in that cellar dies of a disease that a drain would have prevented.

And I would like you to actually answer the next question, not just receive it. {pause: 0.6}

Who killed that child?

Nobody, obviously. That is the honest answer at the level of persons. Nobody struck her. No law was broken. The landlord let a room at the market rate. The mill paid the going wage. The corporation followed its procedures. The parents did the best they could with what they had. Search that entire chain of human beings for a murderer and you will not find one, and every single person in it can tell you, truthfully, that they were doing what everyone in their position does.

Fine. Then what do you call it? {pause: 1.4}

The young man on the steps in the good clothes had an answer, and it is one of the most aggressive sentences anyone produced in that entire century. He said: this is murder. Not *like* murder. Not murder in a manner of speaking. Murder. He called it social murder.

## A killing with no killer   {tone: curious, note: "the Sandel drill on 'social murder' — let the listener commit to the objection, then steelman it from the REAL counting-house (firm unnamed until the next beat), then vary the case so the intuition breaks"}

Sit with that word, because everything else in this episode grows out of what you decide about it.

Your first instinct is probably that it is a stunt. A young radical reaching for the strongest noun available, because "unfortunate" and "regrettable" and "a problem of sanitation" are not going to make anybody put down their newspaper. Murder is a serious accusation with a serious structure. It needs an agent. It needs intent, or something close to it. It needs a person who did a thing, knowing what the thing would do. Take those away and you no longer have a crime, you have a misfortune — and calling a misfortune a murder is not analysis, it is shouting.

If that is roughly where you are, keep it, because it deserves its strongest possible defender. And the strongest defender is not somebody I have to invent for you.

The young man taking notes on those steps had a day job. He spent his working hours in a Manchester counting-house — a cotton firm, and we will come to whose — and the whole arithmetic of the trade ran through that office in its raw form. The order book. The price of raw cotton off the boat. The freight. The wage bill. He did not have to imagine what the mill owner would say in his own defence, or invent a reasonable version of him to argue with. That defence came in with the post.

And it goes like this.

The firm did not build that cellar. It does not own it. Nobody in that office has ever seen it. It pays the wage the market pays — and if you find that wage disgraceful, then sit in that chair for a moment and see what you can actually do from it. Pay a third more than the mill down the road pays, and the competitor who doesn't will be selling cloth cheaper by the end of the year. Then you are undersold. Then you are not a philanthropist, you are a bankrupt. And the four hundred people on your floor have no wages at all, which is worse than low wages by a distance nobody in a warm room seems able to imagine. The drains, meanwhile, are not yours either; they are the corporation's business, and the corporation answers to ratepayers who do not want the rate raised. You employ those people. You have given them the only thing standing between them and the workhouse, which is rather more than the gentlemen who write pamphlets about it have given them. And as for the child — children die everywhere, in every century, in every village in Europe, and they were dying long before there was a cotton mill in Lancashire.

That is the case for the defence, and notice that almost every clause in it might be *true.* Notice something else, too. The man who is about to call this murder understood that case better than most of his critics ever would, because he was inside those numbers, not reading about them.

So let me change one thing, and see whether your intuition survives it.

Grant him everything. Grant that the manufacturer individually cannot do otherwise. Grant the same to the landlord, who bought a bad house in a bad district and would lose money improving it. Grant it to the corporation, and to the ratepayer, and to the parents, and to the lodger. Suppose every single person in the chain is telling the truth: each one acting rationally, lawfully, within their rights, doing what any sane person in their seat would do.

What you now have in front of you is an arrangement that reliably kills a predictable number of children every year, that could be stopped, and that contains no guilty party whatsoever. {pause: 1.0}

Two ways out. One: nobody is responsible. It is weather. It is the price of progress. The deaths are real and the blame is nowhere, and the correct response is regret. Two: responsibility belongs to something that is not a person — to the arrangement itself, to the pattern, to the structure of ownership and rent and wage that produces this result no matter who is standing in the individual slots.

And if the first one turns your stomach — if you cannot say out loud that a system which kills children on schedule is nobody's fault — then you have just signed up to the second, and you should notice what you have signed.

There is a second half to the charge, and it is the half that makes the word "murder" more than a metaphor. Murder needs knowledge. And knowledge is exactly what that society had. This was the most documented misery in the world to that point. Royal commissions. Parliamentary evidence. Sanitary reports. Doctors publishing on the fever districts. Inspectors filing every six months. Nobody in respectable England could claim they did not know what those conditions did to a human body, because it was in print, in official volumes, on the shelves of the clubs. So the accusation has a precise shape: the knowledge is present, the power to act is present, the deaths are foreseeable, and the deaths continue. Every element of murder is there. The only thing missing is the concentration of it all into one pair of hands.

Which is why he refuses to let the diffusion count as an excuse. Spreading a decision across ten thousand people, he says, does not dissolve it. It just makes it harder to prosecute.

That second answer — the one where a *thing*, an arrangement, a way of organising ownership, can be the perpetrator — is the one modern politics ended up living inside. It is behind every argument about public health and poverty, behind corporate manslaughter law, behind every inquest that traces a death back to a budget line and a decision made by a committee that never met the deceased. The phrase itself keeps coming back, dusted off and used again, every time a preventable death turns out to have had a cost-saving behind it. It has been doing that for a hundred and eighty years.

And the man who put it into circulation was twenty-four years old.

The book is called The Condition of the Working Class in England. It came out in 1845, written from what he had seen with his own eyes in the company of a woman who lived it. This matters for everything that follows: it was written *before* the great partnership. Before the joint manuscripts, before the Manifesto, before the forty years. It is not the junior partner's contribution to somebody else's project. It is his, alone, and it is very good, and it helped push a certain unemployed German philosopher away from arguing about consciousness and towards the study of economics — which is to say, it helped make the other man's life's work possible.

His name was Friedrich Engels.

## The son of the firm   {tone: measured, note: "the paradox pays a second time — the counting-house from the last beat turns out to have his name on it; Mary Burns as the real named carrier, guiding role hedged as reconstruction"}

So: whose name was over the door of that counting-house?

His.

He was born in 1820 in the Rhineland, the son of a wealthy textile manufacturer, into exactly the class he would spend his life indicting. And when the family wanted the boy to learn the trade properly, they sent him to the place where the trade was being invented — Manchester, to the firm his father had an interest in. Ermen and Engels.

Which means the arithmetic I put to you a few minutes ago, the whole case for the defence, was not a position Engels had to reason his way into from the outside. It was the family business. The competitor down the valley, the wage bill, the ruin waiting for the man who moves first — that was what he was sent to England to learn, and he drew an income from it for decades of his adult life. The man who wrote that the English bourgeoisie was committing social murder went into the office on Monday morning and helped a cotton firm turn a profit.

It is very easy to score points off that, and almost everyone has. The cheap reading is hypocrisy, and it costs nothing to say. The interesting reading is that this is the single most useful vantage point anyone in the socialist tradition ever occupied. He was not a tourist in industrial capitalism, and not a philosopher who had read about mills. He could tell you what a pound of yarn cost to produce and what a spinner was paid to produce it, because both numbers ran through that office in the ordinary course of business. There is a reason his descriptions of the machinery of trade do not read like a moralist's.

And he never pretended otherwise, and he never gave the money up. That income did two things for the rest of the century: it kept Friedrich Engels comfortable, and it kept Karl Marx alive. For something on the order of forty years, cotton income kept a stateless writer's family fed and his coat out of pawn. The most famous indictment of industrial capitalism ever written was paid for, in the most literal sense, by industrial capitalism.

Now the woman on the steps, because she deserves better than a footnote and is not going to get much more than one.

Her name was Mary Burns. She was Irish, she worked in a factory, and she was Engels's companion for years. She is also — and this is what his biographers reasonably reconstruct, rather than something we have on the record from anyone who was there — the reason a well-dressed young foreigner got into places that were closed to well-dressed young foreigners. She knew the passages. She could vouch for him. On any sensible reading of how that book got written, without her it does not exist in the form we have it.

And that is very nearly all we know. She left no writing of her own. What survives of her comes to us sideways, through the letters of men, mentioned in passing. We do not have her account of what she showed him, or what she thought he was doing, or what she made of the book. The record simply does not contain it. Which is its own small illustration of the thing the book is about: the crucial witness in the great work of testimony about the working class is a working-class woman we cannot hear.

Then comes the partnership. The two men meet properly, discover they have arrived at the same place from different directions, and produce a joint body of work over four decades — the Manifesto in 1848, a mountain of correspondence, the whole apparatus of a movement. And within that partnership Engels adopted a description of himself that has shaped how everybody has read him ever since.

He said he was the second fiddle.

That can be heard three ways. It can be modesty, of the ordinary generous kind. It can be simple accuracy — Marx was, by any measure, the deeper and stranger mind. Or it can be, without any conscious cunning on his part, the most effective piece of positioning in the history of political thought. Because nobody audits the accompanist. A second fiddle is beyond suspicion. If the label sticks, then everything the movement believes gets credited to the first violin, and the man who actually wrote the sentences that a million people memorised disappears into the orchestra.

So the question this episode exists to put to you is this, and I am not going to resolve it for a long while yet.

Was Engels the indispensable partner — the one who made an impossible thinker legible, who turned a chaos of unfinished manuscripts into a living movement, without whom you would very likely never have heard the name Marx at all? {pause: 0.8} Or was he the man who took a subtle, historically specific, deliberately unfinished critique and flattened it into a rigid science of iron laws — and in doing so laid part of the road the twentieth century then drove down? {pause: 1.2}

Hold both. They may both be true. That is what makes it a problem rather than a scandal.

## What was left on the table   {tone: measured, note: "the inheritance; deal the defenders' best card early and fairly — the systematising began while Marx was alive"}

March 1883. Highgate cemetery, north London. About a dozen people at the grave of a stateless journalist whose great work was two-thirds unpublished. Engels gives the address, and says the name will endure through the ages, and so will the work.

Then he goes home to the actual situation, which is this.

Volume one of Capital exists as a book. Volumes two and three exist as manuscripts and notebooks in the handwriting of a man who wrote for thirty years for nobody's eyes but his own — a hand almost nobody living could read, in German, with the drafts overlapping and contradicting each other and the arrangement unsettled. Somebody has to decide what goes in, in what order, and what the theory therefore says. That somebody spends much of the next twelve years doing it, and volume two and then volume three come out into the world with Marx's name on the spine and Engels's decisions inside.

And there is a second problem, larger than the manuscripts. A movement is forming — parties, unions, congresses, workers' associations across Europe — around a body of work that virtually none of its adherents could read. A thousand pages of German dialectical vocabulary braided with English factory statistics is not a text you hand to a compositor in Leipzig at the end of a ten-hour day. If the ideas are going to travel, someone has to make them portable.

Here I have to be careful with the chronology, because it is the strongest card the defenders hold, and it should not be buried at the end.

The making of "Marxism" did not begin at that graveside. It began while Marx was alive and reading. The book that started it, Anti-Dühring, is from 1878 — a long, combative demolition of a rival German socialist that took the opportunity to lay out an entire worldview: philosophy, natural science, political economy, history, the whole thing in one structure. Marx knew about it. Marx read it. Marx contributed a chapter to it. Whatever was being built there, it was built at his elbow, with his approval and his hand in it.

And then, in 1880, at the urging of Marx's son-in-law Paul Lafargue, three chapters of that enormous polemic were pulled out and published on their own as a pamphlet. It was short. It was clear. It was translated everywhere. It became the best-selling popularization of Marxism of the entire era — which means that for most of the people who ever called themselves Marxists in the nineteenth century, this little book, and not Capital, was where they actually met the theory.

Its title tells you what it does. Socialism: Utopian and Scientific.

And its first job is to draw a border.

## The line he drew   {tone: lucid, note: "the hinge; intuition first, let the listener commit to the obvious decent move, then break it on METHOD; utopians named lightly as foil only; define scientific by its opposite; checkpoint after the two discoveries; then the ledger of what the word buys and costs"}

Come back to the cellar, because the problem should come before the doctrine.

You have seen the room. You have decided this is intolerable and that it is not nobody's fault. So: what do you *do?*

The obvious move — and feel how obvious it is — is to work out what a decent arrangement would look like. Sit down and think it through properly, in detail: how the work would be organised, how housing would be built, how children would be educated, who would decide what. Make the design good enough that any reasonable person reading it can see it is better, more rational, more just than the mess we currently have. Then show it to people. Publish it. Persuade the men with money — some of them are decent and some of them are bored, and it only takes one. Build a model community that actually functions, so the thing stops being an argument and becomes a place you can visit. And let the obvious superiority of the working example do the rest.

That is not a straw man, and it was not a fringe position. Serious, formidable men gave their fortunes and their reputations to it — Robert Owen, Henri de Saint-Simon, Charles Fourier — and I am deliberately telling you almost nothing about them, because they are far too good to be background and they are getting an episode of their own. For today they are only what Engels used them for.

But before his verdict, answer honestly: does the plan-and-persuade route sound like what a decent, intelligent person ought to do about that cellar? {pause: 1.2}

Of course it does. It is the most reasonable thing in the world.

Engels says it is hopeless. And the reason he gives is why this episode matters — because it is not the reason you would expect.

He does not say they were soft-hearted, or naive about human nature, or bad designers. He goes out of his way to honour them. He calls them brilliant critics of the new order, men who saw the injustice of industrial society earlier and more sharply than anyone. Their descriptions he keeps. Their analysis of what was wrong, he keeps.

What he attacks is *where they looked for the answer.*

They looked, he says, in men's brains. In "men's better insights into eternal truth, reason and justice." Each of them sat down and consulted his own reason about what a just society would be, deduced an ideal from first principles, and then set about getting it invented and preached into existence.

And watch what follows, because Engels drives it home with real cruelty. If the answer comes out of your own head — then whose head? Owen's reason and Fourier's reason and Saint-Simon's reason produced three completely different and mutually incompatible societies: a village of cooperative mills, a fantastical community organised around the orchestration of human desire, and an industrial technocracy run by experts. All three were sincere. All three were derived from eternal reason and justice. And there is no way on earth to arbitrate between them, because there is no court of appeal above one clever man's intuition. What comes out, Engels says, is a mish-mash.

Then the sentence that does the killing. If socialism is a truth of reason — something a sufficiently clear-thinking person can deduce from justice itself — then it could have been discovered in any century, by any clever person, anywhere. Plato could have had it. A monk in the twelfth century could have had it. So why did it turn up when it did? Why did this idea occur to dozens of people, independently, all within a few decades, all in the countries where steam-driven industry was arriving? On their own theory, that is a coincidence. A cosmic accident of timing.

Engels says nothing about it is an accident. And the timing they cannot explain is precisely the thing that needed explaining.

Now his alternative, properly — because the word he chose is a trap and almost everyone falls into it.

"Scientific" here does not mean laboratory. It does not mean equations, or experiments, or the kind of proof you get in physics. Take it as the exact opposite of "utopian," and you will have it. Utopian: the answer is *invented* — thought up, held out, preached, waited for. Scientific: the answer is *found* — read off a process already under way in the world whether anybody approves of it or not.

Two discoveries make that possible, and Engels credits both of them, explicitly and generously, to Marx.

The first is the materialist conception of history. The motor of historical change, he says, lies not in men's brains but in changes in the modes of production and exchange, and in the class conflicts those changes generate. The family in that cellar is not there because nobody has yet thought of a better arrangement. Plenty of people had thought of better arrangements. They are there because of what cotton is, how it is spun, who owns the machinery, and what that ownership does to everyone standing near it. Change how a society produces, and everything else — its laws, its politics, its morals — shifts to follow, whatever anyone intended.

The second is surplus value: the mechanism showing how, inside a contract that is entirely free and entirely legal, wealth is systematically extracted from the person who does the work. Not fraud. Not theft. The normal, honest operation of the wage relation.

Checkpoint, because that was the load-bearing step. What he is really claiming is this: socialism is not a *proposal.* It is a *diagnosis.* Capitalism has already, on its own, made production social — thousands of hands in one integrated process, tied to world markets, planned in enormous detail inside the firm — while ownership has stayed private and individual. The thing has outgrown its own shell. You do not have to invent the next society; you have to read the strain in this one. {pause: 0.8}

That is the move. Now what it buys, and what it costs, because both are enormous.

What it buys is agency. If socialism is a good idea, then you are a supplicant: you must persuade the rich, and hope. If socialism is where this society is already heading, then the workers in that mill are not objects of anybody's pity — they are the agents of the transition, the people whose collective strength is the mechanism by which it happens. You do not write to a benefactor. You organise. And it buys something even more powerful: confidence. The certainty that you are not merely right but *early* — that history is coming your way and the other side is arguing with the tide. That is a formidable thing to hand a movement of poor people who have been told all their lives that nothing can be done. It built the largest political movement of the modern age, and the pamphlet in their pockets was this one.

And the cost. Three, and they compound.

The first is that a border drawn like that is drawn to *win.* "Pre-scientific" is not a rebuttal; it is a filing category. Once the border exists, the man who disagrees with you about method is not a rival with a case to answer — he is a leftover from an earlier stage, explained rather than met. Remember that, because the entire far side of that line, the builders and the anarchists both, spends the next fifty years shouting from a place the theory has defined as the past.

The second is that a theory which reads the future off the present has trouble taking evidence. If socialism is the readable tendency of capitalism's own contradictions, then a defeat is not a refutation, it is a delay. A revolution that does not come is a matter of timing. There is no result that counts against it. And a proposition immune to disappointment is a strange sort of science.

The third is the word itself. Science was the prestige of that age the way technology is the prestige of ours, and to call your politics scientific in 1880 was to place it, deliberately, outside the space where things get argued about.

One thing in fairness before I move on, because the case against him should not be lazy. Marx mocked the utopians too. The contempt for sentimental blueprint-drawing is right there in the Manifesto of 1848, and it is not Engels's private invention. But the *word* is his. The two-column table — dreamers here, science there — is his. And words like that keep working long after the man who chose them is dead.

## The state that dies out   {tone: measured, note: "problem first: you have won and you are holding the instrument; then Engels's three-part answer in his exact formulations; flag Saint-Simon behind the crisp phrasing; hard question to the listener; forward hook to the anarchists WITHOUT the detour; honour Ep 15's boundary on the twentieth century"}

Say it works. Say history does what he says it will do, and one morning it is over — the revolution has happened, the mills belong to everyone, and you are the one in the ministry.

You are now holding the state.

And on this theory, that is an awkward thing to be holding. Because the state, in this tradition, was never a neutral referee. It is the instrument by which one class holds down another — the courts that enforce the contract, the police who protect the property, the whole apparatus that made the arrangement in the cellar lawful. That is the thing now in your hands. Staffed by people. With buildings, budgets, files, a payroll, and a settled sense of its own necessity.

So the obvious question, and the anarchists are going to ask it very loudly indeed: how does that ever end?

Engels answers in three moves. First, the transition: the workers take state power and use it — deliberately, as an instrument — to socialise the means of production. Second, the consequence: with class divisions gone, the material basis for one group dominating another goes with them. And third, the destination, which he states in a form that got quoted for the next hundred years.

The State is not "abolished." It dies out.

That distinction is doing real work. Abolishing is an act. Someone decides, on a date, to dismantle the thing — and someone has to have the power to do the dismantling, which means you have not escaped the problem, you have only renamed it. Engels is describing something else: not a demolition but an atrophy. The state, he says, becomes *superfluous.* It has functions; if the conditions that generated those functions disappear, the functions disappear, and an organ with no function withers. What remains is not rule but management — and now the line everybody knows: the government of persons is replaced by the administration of things, and by the conduct of processes of production. Roads still need building. Grain still needs moving. But nobody is being governed; things are being administered.

Two honest notes on that sentence. The idea in it is not Engels's. It belongs to Saint-Simon, the French count who thought politics as such was obsolete and that society should be run by the people who actually knew how to run things. What Engels supplied was the phrasing — the hard, portable, endlessly quotable version — and it is his wording that lodged in the political vocabulary of the world. Keep those two layers apart, because the tidy sentence has been doing the arguing on Saint-Simon's behalf for a very long time.

And Engels puts a horizon behind it that explains why any of this was worth dying for. He calls it the ascent of man from the kingdom of necessity to the kingdom of freedom. Everything up to now — the whole of recorded history — is humanity under compulsion, arranging itself around scarcity and the fear of hunger. What comes after is the first period in which the species decides its own affairs deliberately, rather than being pushed.

Now the hard question, and it is yours. {pause: 0.6}

Name an institution — any institution, in any country, in any century — that dissolved itself on the grounds that it had become superfluous. {pause: 1.4}

Take his side first, properly, because there is a real reply. He is not making a naive prediction about the nobility of officials. He is saying the state is not a thing, it is a *function* — and you cannot reason about the persistence of a function from the stubbornness of the men who currently perform it. A fire brigade in a world without fire does not have to be persuaded to disband; it simply stops being a fire brigade. That is the claim. It stands or falls on whether political power really is nothing but a function of class antagonism, with no life, no appetite and no self-interest of its own.

And that is the exact point at which a whole tradition refuses to follow him — and I am flagging it, not chasing it, because those people have an episode coming. The anarchists said: your instrument will not wither, because instruments have staff. Take the machinery of domination, hand it to the friends of the people, and in twenty years you will have domination with better slogans and a new class of rulers on top.

One more thing, and then I leave it, because I made a promise last time. Most of the propositions in this series were never tested; they are arguments about what would happen if. This one got tested, repeatedly, at enormous human cost, in the twentieth century. I said last episode that those regimes deserve a proper hearing of their own instead of a paragraph bolted onto somebody else's story, and I meant it. So all I will put on the record here is the bare fact, which is not in dispute: nowhere, in any state founded on this theory, did the state wither. Not once, not anywhere, not even slightly. What that proves about Engels's argument — whether the conditions were never met, or the argument was wrong — is exactly the kind of question that needs its own hour.

## Things that have a date   {tone: curious, note: "on-ramp from the familiar — the household as 'nature'; then the historicising move; flag the superseded anthropology honestly and separate framework from move; use it as the defenders' card"}

Some arrangements do not feel like arrangements. They feel like the floor.

Take the household you probably grew up assuming was the normal one. One man, one woman, married, with the property and the name passing down to their children, and authority inside the house running in a fairly predictable direction. Whatever you think of it, it does not present itself as *policy.* It does not feel like something that was decided. It feels like the way human beings come — the raw material politics has to work with, rather than something politics could ever be about.

In 1884, a year after Marx's death, Engels published a book arguing that it has a date.

The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State came out of Marx's own notebooks. In his last years Marx had been reading an American anthropologist, Lewis Henry Morgan, whose Ancient Society appeared in 1877. He filled notebooks on it. Engels took those notes and made a book. And the architecture of that book is right there in its title, which is not a list of three topics but a single claim: these three things came into existence together, out of the same change in how human beings produced and held wealth. The family in its familiar form, private property, and the state are one historical package.

And once you have said that, you have said something with a fuse on it. Because anything with an origin can have an end. Put an institution inside history and it stops being the floor and becomes a stage — and stages are, by definition, things you can be at the end of.

He applies this to the position of women with a phrase that went a long way. The shift to monogamous marriage under male authority, tied to the inheritance of property, he calls the world-historical defeat of the female sex. Not a fact of nature. Not the working-out of anybody's essence. A defeat — an event, with causes, in time, connected to who came to own the herds and the land and who needed certainty about whose children were whose. Which means the subordination of women is not something to be endured or excused; it is something that happened, and things that happen can be reversed. That book became a foundational text for socialist feminism and, later, for a great deal of Marxist feminism, and its influence on how the twentieth century argued about the family is difficult to overstate.

Now the honest flag, and I am not going to mumble it.

The anthropology the book is built on has not held up. Morgan's ladder of fixed evolutionary stages, climbed in order by every society, is not what anthropology teaches today. Nor is the rung Engels leaned on hardest: an original epoch of mother-right, a primitive matriarchy that came before property and was then overthrown. That is largely superseded — and it is not a small caveat about details. If you go to the book for a factual account of prehistory, you will be misled.

But separate two things, because they came apart and only one of them died. The ladder is gone. The *move* survived — the insistence that the family is an economic institution with a history rather than a fact of biology, and that where a woman stands in a household has something to do with who owns what. You can reject every stage in Engels's sequence and still find you have absorbed his question, and most people reading this century have.

And keep this book in mind for the next ten minutes, because it complicates the charge sheet I am about to read out. The man accused of freezing history into a set of iron laws is also the man who took the most naturalised institution in human life and put a date on it. There is more motion in Engels than his prosecutors always allow.

## The Engels problem   {tone: tense, note: "the centrepiece; charge at full strength, defence at full strength; both real scholarship; leave it UNRESOLVED; land the general point about interpreters"}

Which brings us to the accusation, and it is not a slur from his enemies on the right. It comes from inside — from people who care about Marx, and who think that what you have in your head when you hear the word "Marxism" is substantially the work of the second fiddle.

The charge, at full strength.

Marx wrote a critique. Specifically, a critique of political economy: an interrogation of one historically particular society, unfinished on purpose, restless, riddled with qualifications, at war with the idea of a system. Engels built a worldview. Anti-Dühring does not confine itself to capitalism; it covers philosophy, history, natural science and economics as one connected doctrine, and it is teachable, quotable, orderly, and complete.

And then he went further, in notebooks published long after his death as Dialectics of Nature. There he extended the dialectic out of human history and into nature itself — matter, physics, chemistry, the development of life, all of it moving by contradiction according to general laws. Marx never did that. Whatever Marx thought he was doing, he was not writing a physics.

Out of that material came the thing later called dialectical materialism: the official philosophy of the Second International — the federation of socialist parties that ran European socialism up to 1914 — and after that of the Soviet state. A total system, with laws, with textbooks, with examinations, with correct answers.

The prosecution is real scholarship, and it was close to an orthodoxy in the later twentieth century. Norman Levine wrote a book with the title The Tragic Deception: Marx contra Engels. Terrell Carver, in Marx and Engels: The Intellectual Relationship, in 1983, worked through the differences between the two men's philosophical commitments in detail. Their case is that Engels converted a supple, historically-bounded critique into positivist determinism — laws of nature and laws of history spoken in the same breath, as if the fall of capitalism were on a par with the boiling point of water. And their sharpest point is about what such a theory does once it reaches politics. If history has laws in the way physics has laws, then a party that knows the laws knows the future. And if you know the future, then someone who disagrees with you is not holding a different view; he is holding an error — objectively, demonstrably, the way a man who denies gravity is holding an error. Levine put the accusation in a phrase you do not forget: he called Engels the pimp of positivism.

Feel the weight of that distinction, because it is the whole fight. "Here is a way to interrogate any society: look at who owns the means of production and see what follows" — that is a method, and a method can be used well or badly, and it leaves you free. "Here is the law of all development, in nature and history alike, and we are its custodians" — that is a catechism. And a catechism, sooner or later, requires examiners.

Now the defence, and it gets the same force, because it is also real scholarship and it has some very hard facts on its side.

First: Marx was not a bystander. He read Anti-Dühring — the very book where the systematising happens — and he wrote a chapter of it. The natural-science project was known to him and, as far as the evidence goes, he approved of it. If this is a distortion, the distorted man was in the room, reading the proofs, contributing material. There is no moment where the tomb is robbed.

Second: they worked together for forty years — jointly authored texts, thousands of letters, shared drafts, arguments settled in conversation and never written down. So where exactly do you cut the seam? The premise of the prosecution is that a pure Marx exists underneath, recoverable if you scrape the Engels off. And you should ask what that surgery is really doing. It is a very convenient operation for anyone who wants to keep Marx while disowning everything done in his name — the original preserved, the fall of the church blamed on the first disciple. That is a suspiciously tidy story about a very untidy relationship, and the defenders say it overstates a rift for effect.

Third, and bluntest: without the popularizer there is nothing to betray. No volume two, no volume three — those exist because a man spent twelve years reading impossible handwriting and making decisions. No pamphlet short enough for a working man to finish in an evening. Very possibly no movement, and no reason for you to know either of their names. You cannot simultaneously hold that Engels made Marx into a world-historical force and that Marx would have been better off without him.

And fourth — this is the thing I told you last time to keep hold of. The supposed apostle of iron laws spent his last years writing letters that say very nearly the opposite. Pressed by younger followers on whether it all really reduces to economics, Engels answered, in his own hand, that the economic factor is only *ultimately* decisive — that the political and legal and ideological forms react back on the base and shape it in turn, and that neither he nor Marx had ever claimed more than that. He went further, and grumbled that it was the young Marxists themselves who had overstressed the economic side, as though he could see the caricature hardening and was trying to head it off. The man Levine calls the pimp of positivism spent his last breath denying the determinism the whole charge is built on. Whether that is the real doctrine finally said plainly, or an old man softening a harder line he had taught for forty years, is exactly what no one can settle — so it goes on the scales, not on the verdict.

So which is it? {pause: 1.2}

I am not going to close that for you, because it is genuinely open — this is a live argument among people who have spent their careers in the manuscripts, and anyone who tells you it is settled is selling you a side. You are going to have to hold it.

But one thing in it seems to me true whatever the verdict, and it is bigger than these two men. Every body of thought you have ever encountered reached you through an interpreter. Somebody decided which manuscript to publish and which to leave in the box. Somebody decided the order of the chapters, and what the difficult passage meant, and which single sentence went on the front of the pamphlet. Those decisions are not neutral, they are usually invisible, and they are made by people whose names do not go on the spine. Engels is simply the clearest case in the history of political thought — the one where we can see the editor's hands.

You have never read a thinker. You have read an edition. That is worth remembering the next time you are told what somebody's philosophy says.

## Back down the steps   {tone: contemplative, note: "ENDING — bookend on the cellar and 'social murder'; step from referee to advocate; the verdict is narrow and falls on the WORD; give the defence its last real point; recast as a beginning"}

Let me finish where we started, in the court off the main road, on the steps going down.

Two people are standing there in the early eighteen-forties. One of them lives in this city and works in a mill, and we do not have her words. The other is twenty-four, dressed in the profits of the trade that built the place, and he is writing it all down.

I have refereed this for the better part of an hour. Here is where I come down.

I think what the young man did on those steps is the better act, and the book he made out of it the greater achievement. And that is not a sentimental preference. What he did there was look — at things an entire respectable society had arranged, with real care, not to see — and then refuse the vocabulary that made it nobody's fault. That takes no philosophy of nature. It takes eyes and nerve, and the willingness to have the word "murder" thrown back in your face by everyone you grew up with. And it is the part of the whole enormous edifice that has never really needed defending. Every abstraction that came afterwards — the modes of production, the laws of development, the withering state — rests on the flooded floor of that room. Take the cellar away and historical materialism is an ingenious machine with nothing inside it.

And the charge that sticks is a narrow one, which makes it worse, not better.

It is not that he popularised. Popularising is honourable work, and the contempt intellectuals reserve for it is usually just snobbery with a bibliography. If you believe something matters, making it portable is not a betrayal of it. Nor is it that he systematised. Somebody had to arrange those notebooks; the alternative was silence.

It is the word. Calling it *scientific* was a claim to stand outside argument, and he applied that claim to propositions that were arguable then and are arguable now — that class is the master key to history, that the state has no life of its own, that the tendency he read in the present would deliver the future. Those are strong, serious, defensible theses. They are not the boiling point of water. And once the border was drawn with that word on his side of it, the movement inherited something dangerous: not a set of tools for asking hard questions about power, but a set of answers with the prestige of science stamped on the outside, and a category ready-made for anyone who dissented. Pre-scientific. Not a refutation. A filing decision. Whether the road from there to the twentieth century is short or long, that habit of mind was waiting at the near end of it.

And yet — the defence gets the last real point, and it is his own book that makes it. The man accused of turning history into iron law is the man who told us that the family, property and the state are all temporary; who insisted that the most natural-seeming thing in your life has a date on it and can therefore end. That is not the mind of a determinist reciting inevitabilities. That is somebody handing you a crowbar.

One last thing, about the label he gave himself.

He called himself the second fiddle, and that phrase has done him harm in both directions. It hid how much of what the world means by "Marxism" is actually his — the border, the word, the two-column table, the arrangement of volumes two and three, the pamphlet in the pocket. And it flattered the assumption that whatever is his must therefore be the lesser part, which is exactly wrong about the book he wrote at twenty-four, before he had a famous friend at all. What is genuinely unusual, and I think genuinely admirable, is that a man with those gifts spent forty years organising his life, his time and his cotton income so somebody else could do the work. That is rarer than brilliance. It should not be mistaken for smallness.

But do not let me close it. {pause: 0.8}

The Engels problem is not solved, and you are the one holding it now. And you will find it is not really a question about two Germans in the nineteenth century. It is the question of what happens to any powerful idea when it is handed to the person who has to make it usable — which is what happens to every powerful idea, always, including the ones you currently believe. The next time you meet an argument that presents itself as beyond argument — as method, as consensus, as simply what the evidence shows, as *science* — you now know the two questions to ask. Who packaged this. And what did the packaging make unaskable.

## Across the Channel   {tone: measured, note: "HANDOVER — the two worlds on the far side of the line as forward hooks only; the anarchists' warning is VINDICATED by the century, stated with restraint; then pivot hard to utilitarianism and end on the monster inside it"}

Engels drew a line across the movement, and drew it to win. Dreamers on one side, science on the other.

Two whole worlds were standing on the far side of it. There were the ones who wanted to *build* the new society now — to design it, fund it, demonstrate it, rather than wait for history to hand it over — and one of them was a mill owner who spent most of a great fortune proving that a mill did not have to be run the way competition insisted it must. And there were the ones who said Engels's plan contained a catastrophe in its second step: seize the state, then wait for it to wither. They said it would not wither, and they said what would grow there instead. The twentieth century gave them a hearing they would not have wanted. Both of those worlds are coming, and neither is going to be filed under pre-scientific.

But first this series leaves Germany altogether.

Because while Berlin argued about spirit and history, and Manchester argued about surplus value, Britain had been quietly running an entirely different answer to the misery in that cellar. Not class. Not spirit. Not the laws of historical development. A sum. Add up the pleasure, subtract the suffering, count everybody's equally, and choose whichever arrangement produces the larger total. The greatest happiness of the greatest number.

It is the most practical idea in this entire series. It has no metaphysics in it at all. It actually built the drains — it cleared yards like that one, it wrote the public health acts, it reformed the prisons — and it is very probably how you personally decide most political questions, whether or not you have ever heard the word for it.

It is also the one idea we have come to that will look you in the face and tell you, with perfect arithmetic and no embarrassment whatsoever, that your suffering is worth it.

That is next time.
