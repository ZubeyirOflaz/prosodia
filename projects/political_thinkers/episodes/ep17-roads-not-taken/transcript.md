## The appointment nobody kept   {tone: wistful, rate: slow, note: "a repeated ritual compressed over years, not a walked-into scene; dry, unhurried, faintly absurd — the pathos must arrive without being announced"}

Every day at noon, a man goes home to wait for a visitor.

He is a clerk. A commercial traveller, some years, in the cloth and colonial-goods trade, which he detests and calls organized fraud. He is not young. He has never married. He lives in rented rooms, alone, and he keeps them tidy, and at midday he stops whatever he is doing and goes back to them, because midday is when the visitor might come.

Nobody comes.

So he waits, and then the afternoon starts, and he goes back to the ledgers and the invoices and the commissions. And the next day at noon he is at home again. And the next. This goes on — if the story is true, and I will come back to whether it is — for years. Some accounts have it running to the end of his life.

Now. What is he waiting for?

He is waiting for a rich man.

Not a friend, not a relative, not a patron of the arts. A specific kind of stranger: somebody with capital and a spare conscience, who has read what this clerk has published, and who has understood it, and who has come to write the cheque. Because the clerk has designed something. It is all worked out — in his desk there are papers giving the dimensions of the building, the number of people who will live in it, how their work will be divided, how their meals and their evenings and their love affairs will be arranged. He does not need an army. He does not need a parliament, a party, a strike, or a single drop of blood. He needs one building, on one piece of land, put up once and properly. Because once it exists, once there is a functioning example standing in the world where people can walk up and look at it, the argument is over. Human beings are not stupid. They will see that this is better. And they will copy it, and copy it again, and inside a generation the old order will be gone — not overthrown, simply abandoned, the way you abandon a bad tool when a good one is lying next to it.

That is the entire plan. Build one, and the world converts by inspection.

And the money never arrives. He dies in his rooms in 1837, and the building is never built, and his name — Charles Fourier — comes down to us mostly as a punchline.

That noon vigil deserves better than pity, though, because the easy thing is to be moved by it and move on. Treat it as a diagnosis instead. That man is not merely lonely and eccentric. He is being perfectly consistent. If your theory says the just society wins by demonstration, then waiting for a benefactor is not pathetic — it is the *method*. The vigil is the strategy, executed exactly as designed.

And he is not alone in it. In those same decades there is a Welsh mill manager who does get the money — his own — and does build the thing, twice, and the second time it comes apart in about two years and takes four-fifths of his fortune with it. There is a French count who fought at Yorktown, made a fortune, lost it, tried to shoot himself when nobody would fund him, and survived with one eye. Brilliant, humane, serious men, all of them, all working on the same problem from different angles.

And within a few decades of their deaths, all three had been filed away — by their own side, by people who agreed with them about nearly everything that mattered — under a single dismissive word. Dreamers. Utopians. Pre-scientific.

I spent last episode with the man who did most of that filing. Today I want to go and look at the people on the other side of his line — the ones who thought there was another way out, and were beaten. Both kinds of them. Because there were two roads that lost, not one, and the second road lost a fight in a hotel in the Hague in 1872 and then spent the twentieth century being proved right.

## One mill, four hundred people   {tone: lucid, note: "the live question set as a concrete choice; put the three roads to the listener BEFORE any name is attached; this is the spine — establish the handrail here and keep it"}

Let me put something concrete in front of you and keep it there for the whole hour, because six thinkers is a lot of names and the names are not the point. The argument is the point.

One mill. Four hundred people in it, spinning cotton, twelve hours a day. Children among them. The wages are what wages are in that district, which is to say they are enough to keep a family in one damp room and not much more. And one owner, who did not build the housing and does not own the drains and is competing with the mill in the next valley, and who will tell you — with a good deal of arithmetic to back him up — that he cannot pay more without going under.

You have already decided that this is intolerable. Take that as given. Everybody in this episode has decided it. That is not the disagreement.

The disagreement is: what do you *do* about that mill?

And there are three answers, and I want you to pick one before I tell you whose it is.

The first: build the alternative. Don't argue, don't fight — demonstrate. Design the good arrangement in detail, get it funded, put it up, run it, and make it work so visibly well that the ordinary mills start to look like what they are. Convert the world by example.

The second: take the state. The mill is one node in a whole system of law, courts, police, and property, and you will not out-compete a system by building a pretty village inside it. Win political power, use it to socialize the means of production, and drive the change from the top with the whole weight of the state behind it.

The third: destroy the state, and let the workers in that mill take it themselves. Not through a government, not through a party, not through a law passed in a capital city — from below, by the four hundred, federating with the mill in the next valley and the next, until the coordination happens horizontally and there is no centre left to capture.

Choose. {pause: 1.4}

I suspect one of those felt obviously correct to you and at least one felt obviously naive. Hold on to which was which, because every one of the three was held, passionately, by people who agreed entirely about the mill — and the fighting between them was, at times, more vicious than the fighting against the owner. That is the strange, sad, revealing thing about this part of the story. When people agree about the diagnosis, the argument about method becomes a civil war.

The second road is the one you know. That is Marx and Engels, and I spent two episodes on it, and I am not going to re-explain it today. Today is the first road and the third. Build it, or smash it.

## The mill that worked   {tone: measured, note: "Owen: lead with the reform that PAID, then the character doctrine, then complicate the doctrine, then New Harmony's collapse; hedge the money figure and the fear-of-unemployment reading"}

Start with the man who actually had the mill.

In 1800 a Welsh draper's son in his late twenties takes over the management of a large cotton-spinning works at New Lanark, on the Clyde in Scotland. He is not a philanthropist parachuting in. He is a working manufacturer, and by the standards of the age a spectacularly successful one, and everything he does at New Lanark he does while keeping the books in the black. That last part is not a detail. It is the argument.

What he does is this. He shortens the working day. He stops employing very young children in the mills and puts them in school instead. He builds decent housing and gets the sanitation working. He opens a company store that sells good-quality goods at close to cost, instead of the usual arrangement where the company shop is a second way of taking the wage back off you. He gets rid of the routine brutality — the beatings, the fines, the petty tyranny of the overseer — and replaces it with a system of inspection and encouragement that his critics found faintly ridiculous.

And in 1816 he opens a building at New Lanark and gives it a name that tells you his entire philosophy in one phrase: the New Institution for the Formation of Character. The following year, an infant school — among the first in Britain. Small children, in a school, being taught kindly, in a cotton village, in 1817.

New Lanark became a place of pilgrimage. Reformers came. Aristocrats came. Statesmen from across Europe came, in numbers, to stand in a profitable Scottish mill and see that the misery was optional.

The man is Robert Owen, and underneath all of it there is a single proposition, and I want to state it carefully because it is the most quietly radical thing in this episode.

Character is made, not chosen.

A person's character, Owen argued, is always formed *for* him. Without a single exception. By circumstance — by what he was fed and shown and taught, especially in the earliest years, before he had any say in the matter at all. Nobody selects their own dispositions off a shelf. He set the case out in a series of essays published between 1813 and 1816, and the title is the whole thesis in one breath: A New View of Society, or Essays on the Principle of the Formation of the Human Character.

Follow that where it goes, because it goes somewhere hard. If the drunk, the thief, the brutal man, the woman the parish calls incorrigible — if those characters were manufactured by conditions none of them chose, then punishing them is not justice. It is a category error. You are penalising a product for the process that made it. The whole apparatus of blame and correction and moral improvement that the nineteenth century loved so much is aimed at the wrong object. Don't reform the man. Change the conditions that produce the man.

Feel how good that is. It is the root of nearly every humane social policy of the next two centuries — early-years education, public housing, the entire idea that you attack crime by attacking the conditions that breed it. When you hear someone say that a child's outcome is set long before school, you are hearing Owen.

Let me change one thing, and see whether it survives.

If character is *entirely* formed for you — if there is no exception, not a single one, as Owen says — then there is no one at home. Nobody is responsible for anything they do. And that cuts both ways, because it does not only excuse the thief. It also means the four hundred people in that mill are not agents, they are outputs. They cannot be the authors of their own liberation, because on this theory nobody is the author of anything. Somebody has to stand outside the process and design the better conditions.

Who? {pause: 0.8}

Owen. Obviously Owen. A benevolent, immensely capable man who knows what the conditions ought to be, arranging the environment for people who cannot be expected to arrange it themselves. The most humane employer of the age is also, if you look at the structure of the thing rather than the kindness of it, running a very well-lit experiment on four hundred people who were not consulted. And notice what this rules out. Nothing in Owen's system requires the workers to organise, demand, strike, or vote. Their part is to be improved. That is the deep flaw in the whole build-the-model road, and it is not a flaw of the heart. It is structural. The plan has no place in it for the people the plan is for.

Which brings us to Indiana.

In 1825 Owen buys a town. It is called Harmonie, in Indiana, twenty-odd thousand acres of cleared, working land with buildings already on it, and he buys it from a German religious community — the Harmonists, or Rappites, after their leader George Rapp — who had built the place up and were moving on. He renames it New Harmony, and he means it to be the demonstration: not a well-run mill this time but a full community of equality and common property, self-governing, the thing itself rather than a step toward it.

He sank something in the region of four-fifths of his fortune into it. Sources give figures around forty thousand pounds, and they vary, so hold the number loosely and the proportion firmly: most of what he had.

It lasted about two years.

The causes are not mysterious and they are almost funny in their ordinariness. It filled up, fast, with enthusiasts — and enthusiasts are wonderful and they do not necessarily know how to run a dairy. Critics said, and some of the residents said, that a fair number of the arrivals had come for the free bread. There was no shared discipline, because there was no shared anything: no common faith, no common trade, no common history, just a common opinion about how society ought to be. The community wrote constitution after constitution in quick succession, which is what a body does when it cannot agree and hopes that procedure will substitute for agreement. And Owen himself detonated a large part of it on the fourth of July, 1826, with a public address he called the Declaration of Mental Independence, in which he named the three great evils holding humanity back: private property, organized religion, and marriage. Whatever you think of that as analysis, as community management in Indiana in 1826 it was not a masterstroke.

By 1827 it was over as an experiment in common property.

And the detail that cuts deepest is this one, which I do not think Owen ever really answered. The Rappites — the devout, disciplined, hierarchical religious community he had bought the land from — had made that same land flourish. Same soil. Same climate. Same buildings. What they had that Owen's people did not was not a better design. It was God, and a leader they obeyed, and a reason to stay when it stopped being fun.

There is a sharp reading of the whole affair which I want to give you as a reading rather than as a fact, because it is an interpretation and it should be labelled as one. It goes: at New Lanark, Owen's system worked because underneath the kindness there was a lever. Those workers turned up, and kept the rules, and sent their children to the school, in a world where the alternative to that mill was destitution. Remove the lever — as it was removed in Indiana, where everyone had a share and nobody could be dismissed into starvation — and you find out how much of your beautiful cooperation was cooperation and how much of it was the fear of unemployment wearing a smile. If that is right, it is a brutal thing to say about the best employer of the nineteenth century. It may be right.

Two more things about Owen and then I will move, because he did not stop.

The first: after New Harmony destroyed his business reputation, he became a founding force in two movements that have long outlived his blueprints. The British cooperative movement — shops and exchanges owned by the people who use them, at one point trading goods against notes denominated in labour time rather than money. And trade unionism: in 1834 the short-lived, wildly ambitious Grand National Consolidated Trades Union. The man whose model community failed turned out to be the ancestor of institutions that are still standing.

The second is a small correction, because you will hear the stronger version and the stronger version is not reliable. Owen is often said to have coined the word "socialism." What can actually be supported is that the word *became current* among his followers — in the Owenite press in the late 1820s, and in the association he founded in the 1830s. Near the birth of the word, then. Not quite the father of it.

## The two three-thousands   {tone: curious, note: "Saint-Simon: open with the Parable as a live comparison put to the listener; then the operatic life compressed; then technocracy, its appeal, and its unanswered question; call back to Ep 16's flag on 'administration of things' without re-lecturing"}

The second builder starts from a different question entirely. Not "how should people live" but "who is actually holding this country up?"

He put it as a thought experiment, in 1819, in a journal of his own, and it is the best thing he ever wrote. Two subtractions. Compare them.

First, suppose France were to lose, suddenly, its three thousand leading scientists, artists, engineers, bankers, farmers and skilled craftsmen. The people who know how to build a bridge, run a foundry, get a harvest in, keep a ledger, treat a fever. Three thousand of them, gone overnight.

Second, suppose France instead lost the king's brother, the great officers of the crown, the ministers, the courtiers, the bishops, and the thirty thousand richest idle proprietors in the kingdom. Every one of them.

Which loss cripples the nation? {pause: 1.2}

It is not close, is it? The first would be a catastrophe you could feel for a generation — bridges unbuilt, harvests lost, nobody who knows how the machine works. The second would be a magnificent funeral, a great deal of solemn music, and then France would go to work on Monday morning exactly as before.

That is the Parable, and the man who published it was prosecuted for it — and acquitted. His name was Claude-Henri de Rouvroy, Comte de Saint-Simon, and if the argument sounds like an attack on the aristocracy from below, look at who is making it. He was born into the high French nobility. His family claimed descent from Charlemagne. He is telling you that his own caste is ornamental.

His is the most operatic life in this episode, and I will spend three sentences on it. He crossed the Atlantic as a young officer and fought in the American War of Independence under Lafayette, and was present at Yorktown. During the Revolution he made a fortune speculating in confiscated church and émigré land, and then lost most of it. And in 1823, poor, ignored, unable to find anyone to fund the reorganisation of society, he shot himself with a pistol — and failed, and lived, and lost an eye. There are more dramatic versions of that scene in circulation, with a count of shots attached; what survives as solid is the failed attempt and the eye. He died two years later, in poverty, with a following of exactly a handful of young men.

The idea next, because the idea won.

Saint-Simon's organising concept is *industry* — meaning not factories specifically but all purposeful productive activity: growing, making, building, healing, financing, knowing. The old society was organised around war and conquest, and its ruling class was a warrior class with a priesthood attached. The new society is organised around production. And a society organised around production is being ruled by the wrong people — by men whose only qualification is inheritance, presiding over a world whose actual operations they could not describe, let alone perform.

So: hand it to the competent. Scientists, engineers, industrialists, the people who produce and the people who know. And once you have done that, something interesting happens to politics itself. Politics is the art of contested command — who gives orders, in whose interest, by what right. But if the question in front of you is how to drain a marsh or route a railway or feed a city, that is not a matter of command at all. It is a matter of *getting it right*, which is a technical question with a technical answer. Governing men gives way to administering things.

Two footnotes on that famous formula, and I will keep them short because I flagged this last episode. The idea in it is genuinely Saint-Simon's. The hard, portable sentence everybody quotes is Engels's wording. Keep the layers apart; the tidy version has been arguing on Saint-Simon's behalf for a century and a half.

Watch what he has just done to the mill.

Owen wanted the owner to become better. Saint-Simon wants the owner to become *irrelevant* — not expropriated in a revolution, just quietly replaced by someone who understands spinning machinery, because the mill is a technical object and should be run by people with the relevant competence, in the general interest, as part of a plan. No class war. No barricades. Just the gradual, obvious triumph of expertise over inheritance.

That is startlingly modern. This is the least discussed man in this episode and arguably the one whose road we actually took. Every central bank staffed by economists rather than politicians, every regulatory agency, every argument that a decision is too technical to be left to a vote, every time you have heard someone say — perhaps you have said it — that the trouble with democracy is that the voters do not understand the issue and the experts do: that is Saint-Simon's road, and we have been walking down it for two hundred years.

Which means the objection to him is not a museum piece either. Two questions, and neither has a good answer in his system.

First: who decides what counts as a technical question? Because the move from "this is contested" to "this is technical" is itself the whole of politics. Somebody performs that move, and whoever performs it has taken a decision out of the arena and put it in an office.

Second, harder: what happens to the person whose interests the plan scores as inefficient? An expert administration is genuinely better at getting things right — and "right" is a judgement that has to come from somewhere, and it will not come from the people being administered, because on this model they are not competent to supply it. Owen's flaw returns in a suit. The four hundred in that mill still have no part to play except to be arranged well.

To be fair to him, he seems to have felt the hole. At the very end of his life, in 1825, he published a book called Nouveau Christianisme — the New Christianity — which recast the whole cold technical programme as a kind of secular religion whose supreme commandment was to improve the condition of the poorest class. A moral engine, bolted on late, by a dying man who could see that competence alone does not tell you what to be competent about.

One more thing, for the genealogy. His young secretary — who worked out his early ideas in Saint-Simon's orbit before the two of them broke bitterly in 1824 — was called Auguste Comte, and he went on to found positivist sociology. So the science of society, as a discipline, has one of its roots in this man's parlour. His followers turned the doctrine into an actual sect after his death. And Engels, when he came to bury the utopians, wrote about Saint-Simon with real warmth. He is the ancestor of technocracy, of planning, and of a good deal of what is now simply called management.

## The orchestration of desire   {tone: warm, note: "Fourier: the question nobody else in the episode asks; attractive labour worked seriously; then the cosmology as colour, framed as his own extravagance; then the joint-stock twist and the feminism credit hedged"}

Back to the man waiting at noon, because he asked a question the other five never really ask, and it may be the question that has aged best.

Everyone else in this episode is arguing about who should own the mill and who should run it. Charles Fourier looks at the mill and asks something else. He asks whether the *work* has to be like that.

Not who profits from the twelve hours. Whether the twelve hours have to be miserable.

His answer is that work is wretched because it is organised by idiots, and it is organised by idiots because the entire civilisation is built on a mistake about human beings. The mistake is repression. Fourier held that we come equipped with twelve fundamental passions — the five senses, then a set of affective drives like friendship, love, ambition and family feeling, and then a group of what he called distributive passions, of which my favourite by a distance is the butterfly: the passion for variety, for flitting, for not doing the same thing all afternoon. Combine those twelve in all their proportions and you get, by his arithmetic, eight hundred and ten distinct types of human character.

Every moral and religious system he knew of treated those drives as the enemy — things to be disciplined, denied, ground down. Fourier says they are the raw material. They are what God actually gave us, and a rational society would not fight them; it would *orchestrate* them, arranging the work and the households and the affections so that following your inclination and serving the common good turn out to be the same act.

Applied to labour, that gives you his best idea, and he gave it a name: *travail attrayant* — attractive labour. Work that pulls you rather than drives you. The mechanism is short shifts and constant rotation. Nobody spends twelve hours doing one thing; you do a couple of hours of this in company you like, and then something else with someone else, and you belong to several working groups organised around what you actually enjoy, and the butterfly passion is satisfied instead of being slowly killed. He extends it with a straight face to the jobs nobody wants: children, he observes, love filth and noise and being disgusting, so the community's dirty work should be handed to organised bands of children — he called them the Little Hordes — who will do it as play, in uniform, with honours.

And the container for all of this is a place. He called the community a phalanx: about one thousand six hundred and twenty people, which is twice eight hundred and ten, so that every character type is present twice and can be matched with its complement. They live in one enormous building, the phalanstère — a communal palace, with covered arcaded streets so you can walk from your rooms to the workshops in winter without going outside. Which is, incidentally, one of the great architectural ideas of the nineteenth century, and it has been rebuilt in a hundred forms since by people who never heard his name.

Then there is the other Fourier, and I am not going to hide him, because the extravagance is genuinely part of the man and it is also the reason he became a joke.

He embedded the whole social scheme in an immense mystical cosmology in which history and the planet itself are in movement toward an age he called Harmony. When Harmony arrives, he predicted, the earth will ripen. The North Pole will grow milder than the Mediterranean. New and benign species will appear — anti-lions, and anti-whales to tow ships. Human beings will acquire a useful tail with an eye on the end of it. Lifespans will lengthen. And the seas will lose their salt and turn into a kind of lemonade.

Those passages exist. They are his. He meant them, in whatever spirit a man like that means anything, and later socialists trying to be taken seriously in respectable company found him excruciating — one embarrassing citation away from having their entire politics laughed out of the room. It is worth saying that the twentieth century went the other way: the Surrealists adored him, and so did some very serious critics, precisely because he refused to keep desire out of politics.

But do not let the lemonade do your thinking. Look at what is underneath the cosmology, because two things there are hard-edged and strange.

The first is that Fourier's utopia is not egalitarian. At all. The phalanx keeps private property. The apartments are not the same; the returns are not the same. It pays dividends to three claimants — capital, labour, and talent — which is to say he designed a joint-stock utopia, deliberately, so that a rich man reading the prospectus would want to invest in it. That is the noon vigil again, showing up in the architecture. The whole design is shaped by the need for a benefactor.

The second is what he did with women. Fourier is commonly credited with coining the word "feminism," in 1837 — the credit is standard, it is disputed by some scholars, so take it as commonly credited rather than settled — and with the claim that you can measure the level of any civilisation by the degree to which it has emancipated its women. He also wrote a book about the erotic arrangements of Harmony, and the free play of desire within it, which was so far outside what could be printed that it stayed in manuscript for a hundred and fifty years. It was finally published in 1967.

His afterlife was in America, oddly, not France. In the 1840s a follower named Albert Brisbane and the newspaperman Horace Greeley made Fourierism a real movement in the United States, and dozens of phalanxes were founded. Most were brief. The North American Phalanx in New Jersey ran for years. And Brook Farm, the famous Transcendentalist community outside Boston, converted to Fourierism — and then burned down, which is the kind of detail you could not put in a novel.

He never got his building. But the question he asked — is the misery of work a fact about work, or a fact about how we chose to arrange it — is the one item from the entire utopian inventory that has not aged a day.

## The word that buried them   {tone: measured, note: "Engels's line REFERENCED not re-explained (Ep 16 did the work); then the utopians' best reply at full strength, which is genuinely prescient; then what actually survived"}

So: the burial. I described it last time from the other side.

In 1880 a pamphlet appeared with the title Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, and it became the best-selling popularization of Marxism of the era — which means that for most people who called themselves socialists in the nineteenth century, this short book, and not the thousand pages of Capital, was where they met the theory. I worked through its argument last episode and I am not going to run it again. The compressed version: Engels honours these three men extravagantly as critics — nobody saw the injustice of the new industrial order earlier or more sharply — and then destroys them on method. They looked for the answer, he says, in men's brains. In better insights into eternal truth, reason and justice. Each of them consulted his own reason, deduced an ideal, and set about getting it preached and funded into existence.

And his killing blow is the one you can now feel the force of, because you have just met all three. Owen's reason produced a cooperative industrial village with an infant school. Saint-Simon's reason produced a planned technocracy run by engineers and bankers. Fourier's reason produced a joint-stock palace of one thousand six hundred and twenty people organised around the orchestration of desire, in a world where the sea has turned to lemonade. All three were sincere. All three were derived from eternal reason and justice. All three are incompatible. And there is no court of appeal above one clever man's intuition, so what the method produces is a mish-mash.

That lands. I do not think there is a serious answer to it as stated.

But the builders have a reply, and it almost never gets made, and it is better than their reputation. Let me make it for them, in the strongest form I can.

First: the demonstration is not a wish. It is evidence. New Lanark was not a drawing; it was a working mill, in the world, with a payroll and a profit margin, and it settled a question that pamphlets cannot settle — whether a humane factory can survive in a competitive market. The answer turned out to be yes, at least there, at least then, at least under that management. When Engels calls this "utopian," he is using a word that means *impossible* about the one project in his story that was actually built and actually worked. There is something odd about a science that files the working prototype under fantasy.

Second, and this is the sharp one: if you refuse on principle to say what you are building, who fills in the blank?

That is the builders' real question to Marx, and it is not naive, it is prescient. The scientific road forbids blueprints. Blueprints are pre-scientific; history is not built to order; the new society will emerge from the conditions and cannot be drawn in advance. Very well. Then the morning after the revolution, there is a country to run and no design for running it — and the design will get made anyway, in a hurry, under emergency conditions, by whoever happens to be holding the levers, and it will be permanent by the time anybody notices. A refusal to specify is not modesty. It is a delegation. You have handed the drawing to the men in the room at the time.

Hold that thought for about twenty minutes, because it is going to come back with a Russian accent.

And then, in fairness to Engels, the ledger. The models did not spread. New Harmony collapsed. The phalanxes mostly failed. No community-by-example ever converted a nation, and the mill in the next valley did not reform itself because New Lanark was profitable — it went on paying what it paid. The build-it road was tested, repeatedly, by serious men with real money, and as a strategy for changing a society it did not work.

What did survive is not what any of them planned. The cooperative shop. The credit union. The trade union. Infant education. The kibbutz, the commune, the intentional community in all its forms, from the seriously impressive to the extremely silly. Company towns, for good and ill. Modern arguments about how to design work so it does not hollow people out. The utopians lost the argument about method and won an enormous number of small, permanent things — which is arguably the fate of every dreamer who is any good.

To the other road, then. And this one does not lose an argument. This one loses a vote.

## The theft that isn't stealing   {tone: lucid, note: "Proudhon: the rent case as the intuition first, then the slogan, then the correction that it means unearned income; the ugliness named plainly; the Marx clash staged as a real duel"}

A concrete case, and it will take fifteen seconds.

Two people. One of them owns a workshop — inherited it, or bought it with money that came from somewhere — and does not work in it, does not manage it, does not visit. The other works in it, twelve hours a day, and makes things. At the end of the month, a portion of the value of what the second person made is transferred to the first person. Not as wages. Not as payment for any act the first person performed. Purely because of what they own.

Is that theft? {pause: 1.0}

Careful, because whichever way you jumped, you jumped fast, and there is a distinction underneath that most people miss and that the man who wrote the famous line did not miss at all.

The line is: property is theft. La propriété, c'est le vol. It arrives in 1840, in a book called What Is Property?, and it made its author instantly notorious and has been misunderstood ever since — including, enthusiastically, by people who agree with it.

Because he does not mean your house. He does not mean your tools, your workshop, your plot, your books. He draws a hard line between two things that the law lazily calls by one name. There is *possession*: what you actually use — the home you live in, the land you farm, the tools in your hands. He wants to protect that. He wants more people to have it. And then there is *property* in the sense he is attacking: ownership as a legal power to draw an income from other people's labour without working — rent, interest, the return on a title. That is the target. Not having things. Charging for permission.

And he does not stop there, which is why the bumper sticker misrepresents him. In the very same book he also writes that property is liberty, and that property is impossible. Deliberately, all three, because he is working dialectically and setting the paradox up as an argument, not a slogan. Marx admired the force of the phrase. Proudhon meant it as an opening move, and got a catchphrase instead, which is what happens when you are the first person to say something in six words.

In that same book he does one more thing. He takes a word that had meant, for fifty years, roughly what "chaos" means — the insult you threw at people who wanted no order at all — and he applies it to himself, approvingly. He is the first person on record to call himself an anarchist.

His name was Pierre-Joseph Proudhon, and of the six people in this episode he is the only one who was actually of the class he was writing about. Owen was a manufacturer. Saint-Simon was a count. Fourier was a clerk, Bakunin a nobleman, Kropotkin a prince. Proudhon was born poor in Besançon — Fourier's town — and worked as a printer and compositor, setting other people's words in metal, largely self-taught, reading the books as he set them. When the revolution of 1848 came he was elected to the Constituent Assembly, and there is something worth pausing on in the image of that chamber: a room full of lawyers and journalists and professors debating the condition of the working class, with one actual artisan sitting in it, who found the experience of parliamentary politics roughly as edifying as you would expect. He tried to launch a mutual credit bank — the Bank of the People — which failed. He was imprisoned from 1849 to 1852 for attacking Louis-Napoléon, and later spent years exiled in Belgium.

His positive programme is called mutualism, and it is the most concrete thing anyone in this episode designed, so put it against the mill.

The four hundred do not wait for a benefactor and do not seize the state. They form an association and they own the mill themselves — the people who work it, holding it, running it, deciding. They exchange what they make with other associations by free contract, at prices reflecting labour time. And the thing that makes it possible rather than a fantasy is credit: a mutual bank, owned by its members, lending at somewhere near zero interest, not for profit. Because that is the real barrier, and Proudhon the printer knew it in his hands. Workers do not lack the ability to run the workshop. They lack capital, and capital is expensive precisely because it is scarce and owned. Make credit nearly free and the whole edifice of unearned income loses its foundation — no revolution required, no state required, just a different set of institutions built alongside the old ones until the old ones are hollow.

And politically he wanted the same shape at every scale: in a book of 1863 he argued for a bottom-up federation of autonomous communes and associations, coordinating by contract, with authority pushed downward and outward and never gathered into a sovereign centre. He wrote a famous passage cataloguing what it means to be governed — watched, inspected, spied upon, regulated, taxed, stamped, licensed, and so on down a long and mounting list, by creatures who have neither the right nor the wisdom nor the virtue to do it. I am giving you the sense of it rather than pretending to a definitive translation, because that passage exists in several English versions and I would rather paraphrase honestly than quote confidently.

Two things about this man have to be said, and they do not sit comfortably together.

The first is the ugly one. Proudhon held antisemitic views — they are there in his private notebooks, and they are vicious. And he was an outspoken misogynist, who ranked women as inferior, opposed their participation in public life, and fought bitterly with the feminists of his own day. Those are not slips, and they are not the standards of the time obscuring an otherwise clean record: contemporaries argued with him about both. I am not going to airbrush that to make the argument about rent easier to like, and I am also not going to pretend it refutes the argument about rent. It does neither. It sits in the record as part of the honest portrait of a man who could see one form of domination with total clarity and was blind, aggressively blind, to two others.

The second is what happened when he met Marx.

They knew each other in Paris. Marx was, at first, an admirer, and tried to bring him into a correspondence network of radicals. And Proudhon wrote back with a warning that I think is the single most quotable thing any of these six ever said to any other: that they should be careful not to make themselves the leaders of a new intolerance, the apostles of a new religion — even were it the religion of logic, the religion of reason.

That was May 1846. That October, Proudhon published a large book called The System of Economic Contradictions, or The Philosophy of Poverty. And Marx destroyed it, in a book whose title inverts his — The Poverty of Philosophy, 1847. Less than a year, then, from the warning to the demolition.

It is a demolition, and it is not fair, and it is largely right. Marx's charge has two prongs. The first is that Proudhon's Hegelianism is amateur — that he had picked up the vocabulary of contradiction and dialectic without understanding what it was for, and was using it as decoration. The second is the serious one, and it is the objection every scheme like Proudhon's has faced since. You want to keep production for exchange — commodities, markets, prices, contracts, competing associations — and you want to abolish the consequences of production for exchange. But the exploitation is not bolted on to the market as an extra; it grows out of it. Free the credit, equalise the start, and within two decades some associations will be more productive than others, some will accumulate, some will fail, some will hire, and you will have rebuilt the thing you removed, with better manners. Proudhon wants the machine without the exhaust.

That is a real objection and I do not think mutualism ever answered it. But look at what the exchange produced. Out of that pamphlet war comes the permanent fracture of the socialist movement into two families that have hated each other ever since: the ones who will use the state and the ones who will not. Libertarian against authoritarian — their words for each other, not mine. Everything that happens in the next thirty years is a continuation of the fight between a German philosopher and a French printer about whether you can get there without conquering anything.

And the man who turned that fracture into a war was a Russian aristocrat who had a talent, unmatched in the century, for being in the room when the shooting started.

## Dresden, and the road east   {tone: dramatic, note: "Bakunin's life compressed hard — one vivid run, then the theory; keep it fast, this beat is momentum not analysis"}

May 1849. Dresden. There is a rising in the city, barricades in the streets, and among the men organising the defence — advising on where to put the guns, reportedly suggesting they hang the city's Madonna painting on the barricades on the theory that the Prussians would not fire on a masterpiece — is a large, shaggy, magnetic Russian nobleman and former artillery officer. Also on those barricades, ringing the alarm bells and later writing about it at length, is the composer Richard Wagner, which is one of those historical coincidences that sounds invented and is not.

The rising fails. The Russian is captured. He is sentenced to death by Saxony, handed to Austria, sentenced to death again, and then handed on to the country that actually wanted him. His own. He goes into the Peter and Paul Fortress in Saint Petersburg, where, by most accounts, his teeth fall out from scurvy, and where he writes a long, strange, self-abasing confession addressed to Tsar Nicholas the First — a document that has embarrassed his admirers ever since, and whose sincerity remains debated, with most readers concluding it was tactical. He is sent to Siberia.

And then he does the thing that made him a legend. In 1861 he escapes — not west, because west is impossible, but east. Down the Amur, onto a ship, to Japan, across the Pacific to the United States, across the United States, across the Atlantic, and back into Europe, all the way around the world, to resume the revolution. He arrives in London and walks in on his astonished acquaintances like a man returning from lunch.

Mikhail Bakunin. Chaotic, charismatic, financially incontinent, incapable of finishing a book, and in person one of the most compelling human beings anybody in nineteenth-century radical politics ever met. The precise personal opposite of the disciplined scholar in the British Museum Reading Room. And he agreed with that scholar about almost everything.

He agreed that capitalism had to be destroyed. He agreed that it would take a revolution, and that the means of production had to be owned in common. His version of that is called collectivist anarchism: the land and the workplaces held in common by federated workers' associations and communes, coordinating from the bottom up, with what each person receives still tied to the labour they contribute. And he extended the rebellion further than Marx ever bothered to, all the way up: in a manuscript of 1871 he wrote the line that everybody quotes — that if God really existed, it would be necessary to abolish him. There are several translations of it. The sentiment is not in doubt.

One thing, and one thing only, divided him from Marx. The state.

## The vote in the Hague   {tone: tense, note: "the CLIMAX — run the drill as a real historical vote rather than a hypothetical ministry (Ep 16 already used 'you are holding the state'); steelman Marx fully, then let Bakunin answer; then the prophecy in the real wording with the 'red bureaucracy' tag flagged as possibly a later gloss"}

The International Workingmen's Association is founded in London in 1864 — the First International, the first serious attempt to build one organisation out of the workers' movements of every European country. And by the end of the 1860s it has two centres of gravity inside it, and they are incompatible.

One line says: organise politically. Build workers' parties. Contest elections where you can, prepare insurrection where you must, and aim at state power — because the state is the concentrated instrument of class rule and the working class needs to hold it, use it to socialise the means of production, and then watch it become unnecessary and die out.

The other line says: never touch it. Do not build parties, do not stand candidates, do not aim at the capital city. Organise the workers directly, at the workplace and in the commune, federate outward, and when the moment comes, destroy the state instead of inheriting it.

At the congress in the Hague, in the first week of September 1872, this gets settled by a vote. And I want you to have the vote, because it is a better instrument than any hypothetical I could build.

You are a delegate. You are not a philosopher; you are a man who has been beaten by police in your own country and has come a long way at your own expense. Here is the case for the first line, and I am going to make it properly, because it is the strongest argument in this episode and it usually gets caricatured.

You are proposing to abolish the state. Fine. Look out of the window. In every capital in Europe there is an army, and the men who command it are the sons of the men who own the mills, and they have artillery, telegraphs, railways, and no scruples whatsoever about using all three on unarmed people — as everybody in that hall in 1872 knew, in detail, from very recent memory. Now suppose your revolution succeeds in a city. Tomorrow morning the counter-revolution begins, funded from abroad, with professional officers. What will you meet it with? A federation of communes that has resolved, in principle, not to have a central command? You will be crushed inside a season, and afterwards the reprisals will fall on the very people you were trying to free, and the movement will be set back twenty years. The transitional state is not a betrayal of the revolution. It is the only thing that lets the revolution survive its first winter. And do not tell me about the dangers of power while your enemies are the only people organised enough to use it.

Also — and this is the accusation Marx pressed hardest at the Hague, against Bakunin's Alliance of Socialist Democracy — the man arguing for a movement without hierarchy has been running a secret society inside your organisation. A disciplined clandestine network of loyalists, with himself at its centre, operating inside the International. Whatever else that is, it is not federalism from below.

That is the case. Would you vote for it? {pause: 1.4}

The Marx faction won. Bakunin was expelled, along with James Guillaume. And to make sure the General Council could never fall into the hands of the other side, it was moved — to New York, three thousand miles from every organised worker in Europe, where it did what an institution does when you put it beyond the reach of its own membership. It died. The First International, the great hope of the movement, was effectively finished, and inside a fortnight the anarchists had reconstituted themselves at their own congress at Saint-Imier in Switzerland. That was not a slow drift apart. It was immediate. The split between the red and the black dates from that room.

So: Bakunin lost. Decisively, procedurally, and permanently. He died four years later.

And now the reason we are talking about him at all.

In a book of 1873 called Statism and Anarchy, and in related writings around it, Bakunin sat down and worked out — in advance, with no benefit of hindsight whatsoever, forty-four years before there was a single socialist state anywhere on earth — what the winning side's plan would actually produce.

His argument has three moves and they are very clean.

The first is a question about words. The proposal is a dictatorship of the proletariat. But a dictatorship is rule, and rule is something exercised *over* somebody. So if the proletariat is the ruling class — whom does it rule? There must be another class beneath it. Otherwise the phrase is empty.

The second is about who actually does the ruling. Not the proletariat, obviously. Forty million people do not administer anything; a few thousand people administer things on their behalf. So the state will be run by a small minority — the educated, the capable, the ex-workers who have become organisers and the intellectuals who have become theorists — and these people will be raised above the class they came from and given the machinery of a state to hold. And Bakunin's claim about what happens next is the sentence to carry out of this episode. Once elevated to state power, he says, they will cease to be workers — and they will look down at the whole common workers' world from the heights of the state.

They will not be corrupted by luxury, particularly. They will be corrupted by *position*. They will come to see the country from where they are standing, which is above it.

The third move is a general law about the thing itself: no dictatorship, he says, can have any other aim than its own self-perpetuation, and it can beget only slavery in the people who tolerate it. A "people's state," on this reading, is not a contradiction that resolves itself — it is a disguise. The domination of the many by a privileged few, wearing the name of the many.

There is a two-word tag that usually gets attached to this: red bureaucracy. It is a very good tag and it may not be his. I have not been able to place that exact phrase in his translated texts, and it may well be a later gloss by admirers summarising him — so I will not put it in his mouth. What is solidly his is everything I just described, in his own words, in a book you can read. And there is an image that goes with it, in one or another translation: that a stick is still a stick, and it makes no difference to the man being beaten whether the beating is administered in the name of the aristocracy, or the church, or the people.

Put the two lines of argument side by side, because they are not symmetrical in the way you might expect.

Marx's case is about the *outside*: without a state you will be destroyed by your enemies. Bakunin's case is about the *inside*: with a state you will be destroyed by yourselves. Marx is talking about the first winter. Bakunin is talking about the second generation. Both of them are right about the danger they are describing. Neither of them has an answer to the other's.

## The prince who counted the birds   {tone: curious, note: "Kropotkin as the third road's constructive face; the biology argument at full strength, then the honest puncture; then the Russian coda that closes both anarchism and the episode's irony"}

Which brings me to the third anarchist, and the strangest CV in the entire series.

He was born in 1842 into the Russian aristocracy — a prince, from a family that traced itself to the old princes of Smolensk — and he refused to use the title. He is universally called the anarchist prince; what is firmly documented is the refusal to use it, so let us leave the exact ceremony of renunciation alone. As a boy he was placed in the elite Corps of Pages and served as personal page to Tsar Alexander the Second, which means he spent part of his adolescence standing at the shoulder of the most powerful man in Europe.

Then, having every door in the empire open to him, he asked to be posted to Siberia.

And in five years there he became a genuinely first-rate scientist — not an amateur, not a gentleman naturalist, one of the serious geographers of the century. From his expeditions he worked out a new account of the structural lines of the mountain ranges of East Asia, arguing that the region was essentially a high plateau whose ranges ran on axes quite different from the ones on the existing maps. His orographic map of eastern Siberia was not superseded until 1950. He also did foundational work on the ice ages, demonstrating that continental ice sheets, and not floating floes, had once covered the northern plains.

In 1871 the Russian Geographical Society offered him its secretaryship. It is the position a young geographer in that empire would have organised his whole life around. He declined it — because, he said in substance, he had no right to the pleasures of science while the people around him lacked bread. That is the hinge of his life. The scientist becomes a revolutionary in the act of turning down the job.

Arrested in 1874, he went into the Peter and Paul Fortress — the same fortress as Bakunin, twenty years later — and his health broke there, and he was moved to a prison military hospital in Saint Petersburg. From which, in the summer of 1876, he escaped: by his own account in his memoirs, on a signal from a violinist stationed outside, then a run for the gate and a waiting carriage. He was in exile for the next forty-one years — Switzerland, from which he was expelled; France, where he did three years in prison; and then some three decades in England, where he became the gentlest and most respectable revolutionary in Europe, admired by people who agreed with nothing he said.

Peter Kropotkin. And he does two things to the third road that change it entirely.

The first is that he pushes past Bakunin on the economics. Bakunin's federated communes still tied what you received to what you contributed — so much labour, so much return. Kropotkin says: abolish that too. That is the move that turns collectivist anarchism into anarchist-communism. Abolish the wage in every form, including labour notes. Distribution should be according to *need*, with free access to the common store of goods.

His reason for this is better than the slogan suggests, and it is not sentimental. It is that the accounting is impossible in principle. Try to calculate what you personally have contributed to the value of anything you make. You are using tools somebody else designed, in a building somebody else built, on land cleared by people three centuries dead, with knowledge accumulated by thousands of anonymous predecessors, delivered by roads and ports and a language you did not invent. All wealth is a cumulative, collective inheritance. The idea of paying each worker their exact individual due is not unjust so much as *incoherent* — it pretends to a precision that does not exist. So stop pretending, and start from need. He set it out in 1892, in a book called The Conquest of Bread, which remains the clearest account anybody in this tradition wrote of how a society might actually feed itself the morning after.

The second thing he does is the big one, and it is the reason anarchism has an argument about human nature at all.

Everybody who dismisses anarchism does it in one move, and you have probably made the move yourself. Take away the police and the courts and the state, and human beings will simply prey on each other, because that is what human beings are — competitive, self-interested animals, and the state is the cage that makes civilisation possible. And in the late nineteenth century that intuition had acquired scientific armour plating: Darwin, or rather Darwin's popularisers, understood as having proved that nature is a war of all against all. Thomas Henry Huxley put it most memorably in an essay of 1888, describing the natural world as a gladiators' show — a continuous, universal struggle in which the strong survive by defeating the weak.

Kropotkin had spent five years watching animals in Siberia, and he thought this was empirically wrong.

Not sentimental — wrong, as a description of what is out there. What he had seen in the harshest environment on the continent was not primarily a war of each against all. It was cooperation: birds migrating and feeding in coordinated flocks, animals warning each other, herds and colonies and mutual defence, and above all the plain fact that in a Siberian winter the enemy is not other members of your species, it is the cold — and against the cold, the animals that make it are the ones that cooperate. He collected this into a series of essays in the 1890s and then a book, in 1902, called Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution. Cooperation within a species, he argued, is as real an evolutionary force as competition, and frequently the decisive one.

And then he draws the political conclusion, and it is elegant. If sociability is not a fragile veneer over a violent nature but an *instinct* — something bred into us over millions of years because it works — then society does not need a state to hold it together. Human beings have been generating their own institutions of mutual support for as long as there have been human beings: the village, the commune, the guild, the mutual aid society, the union. The state did not create order. The state arrived late, captured those institutions, hollowed them out, and took the credit.

He put the practical half in another book, in 1899, arguing against specialisation and against the split between brain-work and hand-work: decentralised production, industry mixed with agriculture, intensive small-scale farming, small workshops spread through the countryside, everyone doing varied work. An ancestor of a great deal of later thinking about localism, appropriate technology, and ecological economics.

There is an honest problem with all of this, and it is not small.

The leap from the zoology to the politics is a value judgement, not a finding. Suppose every observation in Mutual Aid is correct. Suppose cooperation is pervasive in nature, well documented, evolutionarily fundamental. Nothing about the necessity of police follows either way, because human institutions are not settled by what ants do. Competition is also pervasive in nature. Infanticide is pervasive in nature. You can find a warrant in the animal kingdom for almost any political arrangement you have already decided you want, and critics have made the reasonable point that Kropotkin's evidence was selected by a man who knew what he was going to conclude before he went to look.

That is fair. But notice who else it convicts. Huxley's gladiators' show was doing exactly the same work in the other direction — it was the scientific dressing on an entire generation's belief that the poor were poor by natural law and the successful were nature's own selection. Social Darwinism was the most politically useful biology of the nineteenth century, and nobody demanded that *it* justify the leap from zoology to policy. Kropotkin's real achievement is not that he proved anarchism from birds. It is that he broke the monopoly. He made "human beings are naturally competitive" a claim that has to be argued for rather than assumed — and that argument is still running, in every conversation you have ever had about whether people are basically decent.

And then the end of his life delivers something no theory could have arranged.

In 1916, in the middle of the First World War, Kropotkin put his name to a manifesto. It is known as the Manifesto of the Sixteen, and the count is wrong. There were fifteen signatories. The phantom sixteenth is "Hussein Dey" — not a person at all, but an Algerian town where one of the signatories lived, misread as a name. The manifesto backed an Allied victory over Germany, on the grounds that German militarism had to be defeated before anything else was possible. It detonated the anarchist movement. Malatesta, Emma Goldman, Rudolf Rocker, Alexander Berkman — the leading figures of the tradition repudiated it and him, and he spent his last years isolated from the movement he had spent forty years building. The apostle of mutual aid, isolated by a fight about a war. There is no way to make that anything other than sad.

In June 1917 he went home. Forty-one years of exile, and a huge crowd at the station to meet him. He was offered a post in the Provisional Government and declined it.

And then he lived to see it.

After October, Kropotkin watched the Bolsheviks build exactly the thing Bakunin had described in 1873 — the centralisation, the party monopoly, the bureaucracy, the terror — and he condemned it, and kept condemning it. He met Lenin, in a meeting usually dated to the spring of 1919, and he wrote him letters. One, in March 1920, warned in substance that a party dictatorship and bureaucratic centralism would ruin the revolution and bury socialism itself. Another, that December, protested the taking of hostages. I am giving you the substance rather than the exact wording, because the documents exist in translations I have not checked line by line; the substance is not in dispute.

He died of pneumonia at Dmitrov in February 1921, and his funeral in Moscow drew an enormous procession through the streets — the last great anarchist demonstration in Soviet Russia. Anarchist banners, in Moscow, in 1921, permitted once, and never again.

## Lost the fight, won the argument   {tone: grave, note: "land the irony, then refuse to let it settle; steelman vindication and its refusal at equal strength; the question stays open"}

So. Look at the shape of the thing.

In 1872, in a hotel in the Hague, the men who said "seize the state and it will wither away" defeated the men who said "seize the state and it will devour you." They won the vote, they won the movement, they won the century, and the losing side was written into history as a collection of bomb-throwing romantics who could not organise a meeting.

And forty-five years later the winning side seized a state. And the state did not wither. It grew, it hardened, it acquired a professional apparatus, and the men who ran it looked down at the whole common workers' world from the heights of the state — which is not my phrase, it is Bakunin's, published in 1873, before there was anything in the world for it to describe.

That is about as good as prophecy gets in political thought. Political theory almost never produces a falsifiable prediction; this was one, it was specific, it named the mechanism rather than just the outcome, it was made by the losing side in a fight about that exact question, and it came true on a scale that cost tens of millions of lives.

So does that vindicate anarchism? {pause: 1.2}

Take it seriously first, because there is a real case. Bakunin was not lucky. He did not guess. He identified a mechanism — that administration requires administrators, that administrators acquire interests, that a group given the machinery of a state will not dissolve itself, and that the sincerity of their intentions at the outset is irrelevant to any of it. Engels answered that objection, last episode, by saying that the state is not a thing but a *function*, and a function with nothing to do simply stops — a fire brigade in a world without fire does not need to be talked into disbanding. Bakunin's reply, in advance, was that a state is not a fire brigade. It is people, in buildings, with budgets and files and a settled conviction of their own necessity. And the twentieth century did not produce a single counter-example. Not one state founded on that theory ever withered, anywhere, even slightly.

Now the other side, and it is stronger than the anarchists like to admit.

Being right about your opponent's failure is not the same thing as being right. A doctor who correctly predicts that the operation will kill the patient has not thereby demonstrated that his own treatment would have worked. Anarchism has never held a large, hostile, industrial country against a determined enemy, because on the occasions it came close it was destroyed — usually by other revolutionaries, sometimes by the very Bolsheviks Kropotkin was writing letters to, which is precisely what Marx predicted would happen to a movement that refused to organise power. You do not get credit for having avoided a failure you were never in a position to commit.

And take the two anarchists in this episode with the sympathy switched off for a moment. The great enemy of all hierarchy stood accused, with some evidence, of running a secret society with himself at the top — a charge he never satisfactorily answered. The great theorist of mutual aid ended his life expelled from the movement he founded, over a fight about which empire to support in a war. Anti-authoritarians produce authorities. Federations produce factions. Something in the human material keeps generating the thing the theory says is unnecessary, and it is not obvious that the something is the state.

Which leaves the possibility that neither side gets to claim this. Not "Bakunin was right" and not "Marx was right," but something worse: that the problem is real and neither road goes around it. That any group that concentrates enough power to defeat an entrenched order will, by the act of concentrating it, become the kind of group that does not hand it back — and that a group which refuses to concentrate it will be beaten by one that does. If that is true, then the argument in the Hague was not between a right answer and a wrong one. It was between two ways of losing, and everybody in that room was arguing in the dark.

I am not going to close that. It is the most important open question in this entire episode and I do not think a hundred and fifty years have closed it either.

## Noon, again   {tone: contemplative, note: "ENDING — bookend on the vigil and the mill; step from referee to advocate with a NARROW verdict; recast as beginning; then the pivot"}

Let me go back to the man waiting at noon.

He is in his rooms. It is midday. He has a plan in his desk for a building that will house one thousand six hundred and twenty people in a way that makes their work a pleasure, and he is waiting for someone with money to knock on the door, and nobody ever does, and he dies waiting.

For a long time I read that as the epitaph of the whole first road: reason draws a beautiful thing, and the world declines to build it. And it is that. But I have come to think there is something else in the image, which is that he was not wrong about very much. He was wrong about the benefactor. He was right that work is organised stupidly, right that desire is not the enemy of a good society, right that a civilisation can be measured by what it does to its women, and right — most of all — that somebody ought to be able to say what they are actually building.

Here is where I come down, and I will keep it narrow, because a survey does not earn a grand verdict.

The utopians lost on method and they were owed a better funeral than they got. Engels was right that you cannot deduce a society from your own reason and then wait for a cheque. But the road that buried them inherited their unsolved problem and made it worse, because at least Owen and Fourier had *drawings*. A movement that forbids blueprints on principle does not thereby avoid designing anything; it simply guarantees that the design will be improvised, in an emergency, by whoever is holding the building when the shooting stops. That is not a small point. It is very close to being the whole history of the twentieth century in one sentence.

And on the anarchists — the specific thing Bakunin saw, I think he saw clearly and first, and the people who called him reckless owed him an apology they were mostly not alive to give. What he did not have is the other half: a way to defend a revolution without building the thing that ruins it. He identified the disease brilliantly. He did not have the cure, and he sometimes wrote as though naming the disease were the same as curing it.

Which brings me back to the mill, and the four hundred people in it, because I promised you it would come back.

Look at what each road actually offers them. Owen says: your owner will be persuaded, and your conditions will improve, and your children will be educated, and you should be grateful and well-behaved while it happens. Saint-Simon says: your owner will be replaced by a competent administrator, and the mill will be run properly at last, by people who know what they are doing, which is not you. Fourier says: come and live in my building, and the work will feel entirely different — as soon as a rich man pays for it. Marx says: your class will take the state and hold it in trust for you, and the men who hold it will hand it back when the time comes. Proudhon says: buy the mill, own it together, get your credit from a bank you own, and do not wait for anyone. Bakunin says: take it, now, and federate with the next valley, and if you build a government to protect what you have taken you will lose it to the government.

Every one of those is somebody's idea of freedom for those four hundred people. And notice that only two of the six put the four hundred in charge of the process rather than in receipt of it — which is a useful thing to check, permanently, about anybody who tells you what would be good for people like you.

There is one more thing all six of them share, and it is the reason this arc ends here.

Every road in this episode assumes the same master question. Who owns the means of production. Get the ownership right — by demonstration, by expertise, by conquest, by mutual credit, by federation — and justice follows. They disagree about the route with a violence that broke the movement in half, and they never disagree about the destination, or about what the fundamental question of politics is.

The argument is still yours to carry, and you will find it is not really about cotton mills. It is live every time somebody tells you that a decision is too technical for a vote — that is Saint-Simon. Every time somebody says that if you want a better institution you should go and build one rather than complain — that is Owen. Every time a movement wins power and explains that the emergency measures are temporary — that is the Hague, and you already know how the vote went.

## The road that kept the diagnosis   {tone: measured, note: "HANDOVER — the fourth road out of Marx: reform. Name the reformist wing that took the diagnosis and refused the barricade; hook to Bernstein without spending his reveals"}

There is one more road out of the argument this episode has been mapping, and it is the one most of the people listening actually live on.

Go back to the fork we started from. Marx said: capture the state, and ride the laws of history to the other side. The anarchists said: smash the state now, before it devours the revolution that built it. Both of those are revolutionary answers. Both take it for granted that the existing order has to be broken before anything better can stand on the ground.

But there was a fourth group, inside Marx's own movement, who looked at the same mill and the same misery, accepted a great deal of the same diagnosis — and then asked a heretical question. What if you did not have to break it? What if the vote, the union, the eight-hour law, the factory inspector we met a few episodes ago, could be *added up* — not into a revolution, but into a slow, unglamorous transformation that arrived without a single barricade?

To the revolutionaries, that was surrender wearing the mask of strategy. To the people who believed it, it was the only version of socialism that ever actually put bread on a living worker's table. Reform or revolution — the ballot or the barricade — would split the left for a hundred years, and one side of that quarrel, quietly, patiently, would go on to build the world a great many of us grew up inside.

And the man who lit the fuse on that quarrel was no outsider taking pot-shots at Marx. He had been Engels's own protégé — the executor of his will, trusted with his papers — turning, in grief and in good faith, against the prediction at the heart of the whole system.

His name was Eduard Bernstein. And he is next time.
