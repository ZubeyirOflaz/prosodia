## The knock   {tone: tense, note: "cold open on the scene; low and close, a real emergency in the room; do not tip that this is a thought experiment until it lands"}

There is a knock at your door. {pause: 0.8}

It is late. Hiding in the back of your house, behind a locked door, is a friend — someone who ran to you an hour ago, terrified, and asked you to keep them safe. You did. You took them in. And now there is a man on your porch, and you know exactly who he is and exactly what he wants. He is going to kill your friend. He asks you, plainly, standing in your doorway: *is your friend inside?*

You could lie. Of course you could lie. You could look him in the eye and say, *no, they went that way, toward the river,* and send him off into the dark, and save an innocent life with one small, obvious, decent falsehood. {pause: 1.0}

You'd do it. I'd do it. Nearly every human being who has ever lived would do it, and would be right to, and would sleep fine. The lie costs nothing and it saves everything. Where is the person cold enough to say otherwise?

## The scandalous no   {tone: measured, note: "reveal Kant's answer flatly, without editorializing yet; let the listener recoil first"}

I'll tell you where.

Late in his life — 1797, an old man in a Prussian town most of Europe had never heard of — one of the most careful thinkers who ever lived sat down and wrote a short, stubborn essay with a title that sounds like a joke: *On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy.* From *love of humanity.* And his answer to the murderer at the door was — no.

Not *no, it's complicated.* Not *no, but I understand.* Just — no. You may not lie. Not even to him. Not even to save your friend. Because the moral law does not carry an exception clause that reads *unless things get bad enough.* Tell the truth, he says, or say nothing — but you may not speak a lie, whatever comes of it. {pause: 1.0}

Now, most people who hear that conclude one of two things. Either the man was a monster, or the man was a fool — some bloodless logician who cared more for the tidiness of his rules than for the actual friend bleeding out in the actual back room. And I want to be honest with you: philosophers have argued about that essay ever since, and plenty of them think he got the case itself wrong, or at least stated it far too harshly. You are allowed to think he blew it. I sometimes think he blew it.

But here is what I don't want you to do. I don't want you to laugh him off. Because underneath that scandalous, almost unbearable *no* is one of the most powerful ideas in the history of politics — and if you dismiss the man too fast, you will miss it entirely.

## What kind of mind is this   {tone: curious, note: "pivot from the shock to genuine intrigue; introduce the human anchor lightly, warmly"}

So sit with the strangeness of it for a second. What kind of mind builds a whole moral universe out of rules that hold even when the rules seem to produce disaster? What is he *seeing* that the rest of us, with our sensible lie, are not?

The man's name is Immanuel Kant, and almost everything about his life is the opposite of the fire in that question. He was born in 1724 in Königsberg, a port city on the far edge of Prussia, and — as far as we can tell — he essentially never left it. No adventures. No love affairs anyone recorded. No war stories. A lifelong bachelor who lived by a schedule so exact that, the legend goes, the townspeople of Königsberg could set their clocks by his afternoon walk. Rise, coffee, write, lecture, dine, walk. The same route, the same hour, every day, for decades.

That's the caricature — the clockwork professor, the walking metronome. And I have to flag it: some of these stories are the kind that grow in the telling, and we should hold them a little loosely. But there's a truth the caricature points at, and it's this. This was a man who believed, down to the marrow, that beneath all the noise and mess of human life there is *order* — a structure you can find if you're rigorous enough, a law underneath the chaos. He looked for it in the stars. He looked for it in the mind. And he looked for it, most fiercely of all, in the question of how one free person may rightfully rule another.

There's one crack in the routine, and it's telling. The story that comes down to us is that the one thing that ever broke Kant's famous walk — the one book that made him forget the hour and stay in his chair — was a novel about how to raise a child, by a wild, weeping, half-mad Genevan named Jean-Jacques Rousseau. Hold onto that. We'll need it.

## The caring state   {tone: warm, note: "INTUITION-FIRST for the political question; build the paternalist state as GENUINELY attractive; the listener should want it before it's complicated"}

But let's leave the man for a moment and go to the argument — the one that still runs straight through your life.

Imagine a state that truly loves you. {pause: 0.6}

Not a tyranny. Not Big Brother. Picture, instead, the wisest, kindest government you can — one that wants nothing on earth except for you and everyone around you to be happy, healthy, and safe. It has the data. It knows that people who save for retirement end up better off, so it makes you save. It knows the foods that will kill you young, so it steers you off them. It knows the risky bets that ruin lives, so it quietly closes those doors. It is, in every case, right — it genuinely knows better than you what will make your life go well, and it acts on that knowledge, gently, for your own good.

Would you take it? {pause: 1.2}

Be honest, because the pull here is real. A government like that would prevent an enormous amount of suffering. It would save people from their own worst impulses — and we all have them. Half the arguments in modern politics are, quietly, arguments about *this* — about how much a good state should protect us from ourselves. When you hear "they should ban that," "someone should make people do this," "it's for their own good" — that is the caring state, and it is one of the most natural, decent instincts a person can have. Rulers have made exactly this promise for as long as there have been rulers: *trust me, I'll take care of you.*

## The father who is a despot   {tone: measured, note: "COMMIT, THEN COMPLICATE; vary one thing — you are an adult; let the discomfort surface before naming it"}

So let's grant it. Let's say you took the deal. The caring state moves in, and it delivers — you are, measurably, better off. Healthier. Richer at sixty. Safer.

Now change one thing. Just one. {pause: 0.6}

*You disagree with it.* Not because you're stupid or self-destructive — you've simply weighed your own life and decided you'd rather spend the money now than save it, or run the risk than avoid it, or eat the thing that will shorten your days by a year because those days are yours and that's the bargain you'd make. You are a competent adult, and you have looked at the very same facts, and you have chosen differently.

And the caring state says: no. We know better. And it overrides you — for your own good.

Feel what just happened. {pause: 1.0} A moment ago this was love. Now it's something else. Because there is a name for the arrangement in which one party decides what is good for you and imposes it, no matter what you yourself have concluded — and that name is not *citizen.* It's *child.* The loving state, the instant it overrides your own judgment about your own life, has stopped treating you as an adult and started treating you as a minor in its care.

Kant saw this with a clarity that still stings. A government built on benevolence, he wrote — a government that treats its people the way a loving father treats his children, deciding for them how they ought to be happy — is *"the greatest despotism thinkable."* {pause: 0.8} Not the kindest mistake. The greatest despotism. Worse, in a way, than the brutal tyrant — because the brutal tyrant at least leaves you your own mind, while the loving one climbs inside and takes even that.

## The one thing you're owed   {tone: lucid, note: "NAME AS PAYOFF; introduce the single innate right; define freedom plainly and by its opposite"}

Which brings us to the thing you just felt, and its name.

What went wrong with the caring state wasn't that it was cruel. It was *nice.* What went wrong is that it gave you everything — except the one thing Kant thought a person is actually owed. And here is the whole of his politics compressed into a single, austere claim, so let me say it slowly.

There is exactly *one* right you are born with. One. Not a long charter of entitlements — one innate right, and everything else in political life is built out of it. And that one right is *freedom.* {pause: 0.8}

But be careful, because he does not mean what you probably mean by the word. He is not talking about doing whatever you want. He defines it with almost mathematical precision — and it's worth hearing in something close to his own words. Freedom, he says, is *independence from being constrained by another person's choice.* Independence from another's *will.* The right, as he puts it, that belongs to every human being by virtue of their humanity — and no other.

Anchor it by its opposite, because that's where it gets vivid. The opposite of freedom, for Kant, is not danger, and it is not poverty, and it is not unhappiness. The opposite of freedom is being *at the disposal of another person's will* — being a thing that someone else steers. The slave is the pure case: not necessarily beaten, not necessarily starving, but *owned* — an instrument of somebody else's choosing. And the loving state, quietly, had made you a gentler version of exactly that. Well cared for. Well fed. And steered.

So what you are owed, before happiness, before welfare, before safety — is to not be steered. To be, in the deepest sense, your own.

## When my freedom meets yours   {tone: measured, note: "build the Universal Principle of Right in LAYERS; name the difficulty; carry one running example"}

Now, this is the hardest turn in the whole story, and I'm going to take it slowly, because it's where Kant stops being a critic and becomes a builder — where he answers the question the last four episodes have been circling. Stay with me.

Because the obvious problem with making freedom the whole point is that my freedom keeps crashing into yours. If freedom means *nobody may constrain my choice,* then the mugger is free to take your wallet and you are free to keep it, and we're back in the war of all against all we spent whole episodes trying to escape. Freedom for everyone, unlimited, is freedom for no one. So how do you get a *rule* — a law, a state — out of a principle that seems to forbid all rules?

Here is Kant's move, and it is one of the most elegant things in political thought. He doesn't limit your freedom for the sake of happiness, or order, or the greater good. He limits it *only for the sake of freedom itself* — everyone's, together. Watch.

An action is right, he says — genuinely *right,* rightful — if it can coexist with everyone's freedom under a universal law. {pause: 0.8} That's it. That is the Universal Principle of Right, and it is the hinge the whole building turns on. Not: does this action make people happy? Not: does it maximize welfare? Just: *can my doing this stand alongside your equal freedom to live your own life, under one law that applies to us both?*

Let me put it on a single example and keep it there. I swing my fist. Am I free to? Kant's test isn't "will it hurt someone" or "will it make the world worse." The test is: can my swinging fist coexist with your freedom, under a law we could both live under? And the moment my fist reaches your face, the answer is no — because now I'm not exercising my freedom, I'm *canceling yours,* steering your body with my will. So the law that stops my fist at your nose is not a limit *on* freedom. It is freedom's own logic, applied to both of us at once. The only rightful use of force in the whole world, Kant says, is force that stops a *stealing* of freedom — force used to hold the boundary where my liberty ends and yours begins.

## So what he's really saying   {tone: contemplative, note: "CHECKPOINT; restate the hard step in one plain sentence; let it settle"}

Let me stop and say that again plainly, because everything downstream rests on it.

The whole justification of the state — the police, the courts, the law, all of it — is not that it makes us happy, and not that it makes us prosperous, and not even that it keeps us safe. It's narrower and stranger than that. The state is justified *only* as the machinery that secures equal freedom — that draws and defends the lines between one person's will and another's, so that we can each be our own master without colliding. {pause: 0.8}

A government could make you rich and it would not, on this view, have earned the right to rule you. A government could make you safe and it would not have earned it. The only thing that earns it — the only thing — is securing everyone's freedom, equally, under a law that treats no one as anyone else's instrument.

Everything else, Kant thinks, is a bonus the state has no business demanding *from* you or forcing *on* you. It is not there to perfect your soul or fatten your purse. It is there to keep you free.

## The tyranny that works   {tone: tense, note: "STAGE THE CLASH vs Hobbes/Locke/Hume; steelman benefit hard, then let Kant refuse it; use a concrete stress test"}

And this is where Kant walks straight into a fight with almost everyone we've met so far — and it's worth staging the quarrel honestly, because the other side is not stupid.

Think back. Hobbes told us to submit to the sovereign because the alternative — the state of nature, the war of all against all — is so much worse that any peace is a bargain. Locke softened it: we form a government because it *protects* our lives, our liberties, our property better than we could alone. Different men, but listen to the shape of both arguments. They both ground the state in a *payoff.* You should accept authority because you come out ahead. It works. It benefits you. And across the channel, David Hume — whom we met last time — pushed it further still: justice itself is just the set of habits that turn out to be *useful,* the arrangements that happen to work, worn smooth by time. Right is whatever pays.

Now, that is an enormously appealing way to think, and I don't want to wave it away. It's probably how *you* think, most days. Judge the system by its fruits. Does it deliver? Are people better off under it? That's not cynicism — that's common sense.

So let me press Kant with the hardest version of it. Picture a tyranny that *works.* {pause: 0.8} A dictator — but a brilliant, benevolent one. Crime near zero. Everyone housed, everyone fed, the trains immaculate, the people, by every measurable standard, content. He asks nothing of you but obedience, and in return your life is safer and softer than any free society could make it. By the ledger of benefit — by Hobbes's logic, by Locke's, by Hume's — you should be grateful. It delivers. It works.

And Kant looks at that gleaming, comfortable machine and says: it is still *wrong.* {pause: 1.0} Not wrong because it might turn cruel later — grant that it never does. Wrong *now,* wrong at its root, even functioning perfectly. Because every one of those content, well-fed people is living at the disposal of another man's will. They are being *steered.* And you cannot buy that back with any amount of comfort, because the thing that's been taken isn't comfort — it's the one thing you were owed. A smoothly beneficial tyranny is a golden cage, and Kant's whole philosophy is the refusal to call a cage a home just because the food is good.

That's the payoff of the murderer at the door. A man who will not bend the rule of truth even to save a friend is, yes, terrifying in his rigor. But he is the same man who will not call a comfortable tyranny legitimate just because it *works* — and that second refusal might be the most important sentence in the defense of human freedom ever written.

## Rousseau's ghost, purified   {tone: measured, note: "the debt to Rousseau; general will purified from feeling into reason; set up the idea of reason"}

So where does he get it? Where does a principle come from that stands *above* what works, above what people happen to want, above what makes them happy?

Here's where the weeping Genevan comes back — the one whose book broke the famous walk. Because Rousseau had already handed Kant the raw material, and last episode we watched him forge it: the *general will.* The idea that a free people isn't just a heap of individuals each chasing their private advantage, but can form a single collective will aimed at the common good — and that real freedom is obeying a law you gave yourself, as part of that whole. Rousseau's great insight was that you can be *both* subject and author of the law — that if the law is truly yours, obeying it isn't submission, it's autonomy.

Kant took that and fell in love with it. But he thought Rousseau had left it dangerously soft. For Rousseau, the general will still ran on *feeling* — on a kind of civic passion, a love of the fatherland, a sentiment in the breast of the citizen. And feeling, Kant knew, is exactly the thing that curdles. Feelings can be whipped up. Crowds can be inflamed. A "general will" grounded in what the people passionately feel in the moment is one demagogue away from the mob.

So Kant did to the general will what he did to everything he touched: he pulled it up out of feeling and into *reason.* The united will of the people, he decided, must not be a matter of what they happen to emote, or even what they happen to vote for on a given afternoon. It must be a matter of what *reason itself* demands — what any free, rational being would have to will, if they were legislating for a community of equals. He took Rousseau's warm, dangerous flame and turned it into something cold, clear, and — he hoped — incorruptible.

## The contract that never happened   {tone: lucid, note: "the central dwell: contract as an idea of reason; define carefully; this is the heart"}

And this is the idea I most want you to leave with, because it's the one the plan asks me to dwell on, and it's the deepest thing in the episode. So let me build it carefully.

For three episodes we have circled the great myth of modern politics: the social contract. The story that, somewhere back at the dawn of society, free people came together and agreed — signed, consented, promised — to be governed. And you may have felt, as we went, the obvious objection tugging at your sleeve. *It never happened.* Nobody signed anything. No one alive ever consented to the state they were born into. You didn't. I didn't. The whole thing looks like a fairy tale we tell to make obedience feel voluntary.

Kant agrees. {pause: 0.8} That's the astonishing part. He looks the objection dead in the eye and says: you're right, it never happened. There was no original signing. The contract is not a historical event, and anyone who goes looking for it in the past is chasing a phantom.

And then he says: *it doesn't matter.* Because the contract was never supposed to be a fact about the past. It is — and here is his phrase, one of the great phrases in political philosophy — an *"idea of reason."* {pause: 1.0}

Let me define that, because it sounds like a dodge and it is the opposite of a dodge. An idea of reason is not a description of something that happened. It is a *standard* — a test you hold up against the world. The social contract, Kant says, has undoubted practical reality, but its reality is this: it binds every lawmaker to legislate in such a way that the laws *could have* arisen from the united will of the whole people. Not *did.* Could have.

So the question a ruler must ask of any law is no longer *did the people agree to this* — no one ever agrees to anything, the past is silent. The question becomes: *could* a whole people of free and equal citizens have agreed to this, as a demand of their own reason? Could this law have come from all of us, legislating together as free beings? {pause: 0.6} If yes, it is legitimate — even if no one ever actually voted for it. And if no — if it is a law that no free people could ever rationally impose on themselves — then it is illegitimate, even if every subject cheered when it passed.

## Why that changes everything   {tone: measured, note: "the hinge to Rawls; make the significance land without jargon"}

Stop and feel how radical that is, because it quietly detonates the whole problem we've been stuck on.

Every contract thinker before Kant was haunted by the same ghost: *actual consent.* They needed you to have really agreed, somehow, or the whole edifice wobbled — and since you plainly never did, they had to invent fictions to patch the hole. Tacit consent. You consented by using the roads. You consented by not emigrating. Flimsy stuff, and they knew it.

Kant just cuts the cord. He replaces the consent that *did* happen with the consent that *could* happen — real agreement with rational agreement. And that one move is one of the most consequential in the history of political thought, because it hands his greatest successor the master key. Nearly two centuries later, an American philosopher named John Rawls will ask a question that is pure Kant: not *what did people agree to,* but *what principles would free and equal people choose, reasoning together, if they were designing a society fairly?* And he'll build the most influential theory of justice of the twentieth century on it. That whole tradition — the idea that we test our institutions by what a rational, fair-minded people *would* accept — walks out of this one sentence in an old man's book from 1797. The consent stops being an event and becomes a question you can ask of any law, in any age, forever.

## The philosopher and the king   {tone: grave, note: "STAGE THE CLASH with a real carrier; the Frederick William II censorship; the collision of autonomy and autocracy — well documented, tell it straight"}

But I have been giving you Kant at his most soaring, and I owe you the ground he actually stood on. Because there is a bitter irony sitting at the center of this man's life, and it's too good — too human — to skip.

The philosopher of freedom, the man who wrote that no one may be steered by another's will, that autonomy is the whole dignity of a person — that man lived his entire life as a subject of one of the most absolute monarchies in Europe. Prussia. He came up under Frederick the Great, a hard, brilliant, enlightened despot who at least prized philosophy and let it breathe. But Frederick died in 1786, and the throne passed to his nephew, Frederick William II — a duller, more pious, more nervous king, surrounded by ministers who wanted the dangerous new thinking about religion shut down.

And in the 1790s, they came for Kant. He had published a book applying his cold, clear reason to religion itself — reasoning about God the way he reasoned about everything, without deference. And the king's government sent him an order. A reprimand, and a command: stop. Stop writing and lecturing on religion, or face the consequences. {pause: 0.8}

Now here is the collision. What does the philosopher of autonomy do when a real autocrat, a living human being with the power of the state, tells him to be silent? Does the man who wouldn't lie to a murderer defy the king and burn?

He obeyed. {pause: 1.0}

He wrote back, and he agreed to keep quiet on the subject — but listen to how he did it, because it's exact, and it's pure Kant. He phrased his promise as a pledge made by *Your Majesty's most loyal subject* — and he later made plain that the wording was deliberate. He had promised silence *to this king.* To Frederick William II. As long as *this* monarch lived. And when Frederick William II died, a few years later, Kant considered the promise discharged — the man he'd made it to was gone — and he took the subject up again.

Look at what that is. It is not heroism, exactly. He didn't martyr himself; he bent. But he bent *within the law of his own reason.* He kept the letter of a promise with a precision that verges on the absurd — the same precision that wouldn't lie to the murderer — and he used that very precision to reclaim his freedom the moment the promise's terms ran out. The philosopher of autonomy, cornered by a real king, didn't escape the cage. But he found, inside it, the one move his own principles allowed — and he made it. There's something almost unbearably him about that.

## The universalist's blind spot   {tone: somber, note: "the contested underbelly; race and women; flag honestly, do not smooth it, do not caricature either"}

And now I have to tell you the harder thing — the part that a tidy hero's tale would leave out, and shouldn't.

Because here is a man who built the most sweeping, thrilling claim about human equality in the whole tradition. *Every* rational being. *Every* human, by virtue of their humanity, owed the innate right to freedom. No exceptions, no rankings — that is the entire force of it. A moral law valid for any rational creature, anywhere in the universe. It is breathtaking in its reach.

And the same man wrote about race in ways that ranked human beings — that treated Europeans as the fullest specimens of humanity and others as lesser. And he held that women, and servants, and the propertyless, while they might be citizens in some passive sense, lacked the independence to be *active* citizens — to vote, to have a voice in the laws. The philosopher who said no one may be at the disposal of another's will drew, around the edges of "everyone," a smaller circle than his own words demanded.

I'm not going to smooth that over, and I'm not going to use it to cancel the ideas either — both of those are the easy way out. Scholars argue about it, hard and in good faith: was this the incidental prejudice of an eighteenth-century Prussian who simply hadn't followed his own logic to its end — or something deeper, a rot closer to the core? That argument is live, and I won't pretend to settle it for you. {pause: 0.8}

But I'll say this. There is a real sense in which Kant's *principle* is more radical than Kant was — in which the universal freedom he wrote down is a weapon that later generations would pick up and turn against exactly the exclusions he himself accepted. The people he left outside the circle would one day quote *him* to force their way in. That's the strange fate of a truly universal idea. It outgrows the man who thought it. It refuses to stay as small as its author.

## Foundation or ghost   {tone: contemplative, note: "the central tension, steelmanned both ways; then step from referee to advocate; earned judgment"}

So let me put the whole thing on the scale, honestly, because this is where the episode has to land, and Kant deserves the hardest version of the case against him.

On one side: this is the most *principled* answer anyone has given to the question that has haunted us all series. What makes power legitimate? Kant's answer owes nothing to your comfort, nothing to the accidents of your history, nothing to what a crowd happens to feel on a given night. It floats above all of it, incorruptible: authority is legitimate when, and only when, it secures the equal freedom of all under laws that free and rational people could give themselves. No tyrant can buy his way past that test with bread and safety. No mob can shout it down. It is a fixed star — the same for a Prussian in 1797 and for you tonight — and in a world where might so easily dresses up as right, a fixed star is not nothing. It might be everything.

But now the objection — and it's the one his greatest successor will build an entire philosophy to press. All of that soaring rightness lives *where,* exactly? In reason. In an idea. In the head. Kant's freedom is not something you can point to in the world — it's a law you legislate in the silence of your own rational will, a contract that never happened, a united will no one ever spoke. And a critic can look at that magnificent structure and ask a devastating question: is a freedom that exists only inside your mind, in the form of a law — is that freedom *real?* Or is it a beautiful ghost — a consolation you tell yourself while the actual, messy, feeling humans out in the actual world go on being hungry, and unequal, and unfree in every way you can touch?

Here's where I come down, having tried to give both sides their due. I think Kant is *more right than his critics will allow,* and I think he is not right enough. He is right that you cannot ground human dignity in benefit — that the moment you say "obey because it pays," you have already sold the thing that mattered, and a good enough tyrant can always out-bid you. That refusal is permanent, and we need it, and I would not give it back. {pause: 0.8}

But the critic has a point too, and it's not a small one. A freedom that never has to answer to what actual people actually want, feel, and suffer — a right that lives so far above history that history can't touch it — starts to float free of the very humans it was built to honor. You can be perfectly free in Kant's sense and perfectly miserable in every other. And at some point a person is entitled to ask what all that immaculate freedom is *for.*

## The next man is already unhappy   {tone: measured, note: "ENDING; bookend to the door; recast as a beginning; hand the ghost to Hegel; address the listener for the close"}

Which is exactly where the next man in our story is standing — already impatient, already unconvinced.

He would read Kant, and he would grant him nearly everything — the genius, the reach, the fixed star. And then he would say: *it's a ghost.* Freedom that lives only inside your head, as a law you give yourself in the quiet of your own reason — that is not freedom, Hegel would say. That is freedom's *shadow.* Real freedom can't stay locked in the mind. It has to come *out* — it has to become *actual,* in the things you can touch: in families, in the rough bargaining of markets, in institutions, in the long unfolding of history itself. A freedom that never becomes real in the world is just a lovely idea a lonely man had, on a walk, in a town on the edge of the map. And the whole of Hegel's monstrous, magnificent philosophy will be an attempt to bring the ghost down to earth.

That's next time.

But before you go, walk back with me to the door. {pause: 0.8} The knock. The murderer. The friend in the back room. And the old man who wouldn't lie to save a life. When we started, that answer looked like coldness, or foolishness — a rule worshipped past all reason. You may still think so; I half do. But I hope it looks *bigger* now. Because the same stubbornness that wouldn't bend the truth for a friend is the stubbornness that won't call a golden cage a home, won't sell your freedom for your comfort, won't let any king or crowd or caring, smothering state decide for you how you ought to live. He drew a line and he would not cross it — not for anyone, not for any prize.

The question he leaves you with is the one you'll carry into the rest of your life, every time some kind and powerful thing offers to take the hard choices off your hands. *Would you take the deal?* {pause: 1.0} The comfort, the safety, the warm certainty — in exchange for being, quietly, steered.

Kant already answered. Now it's your turn — and that, he'd tell you, is the whole point. The answer was never supposed to come from him. It was always supposed to come from you.
