## The round room   {tone: grave, rate: slow, note: "cold open on a physical place and one anonymous man; unhurried, concrete, no doctrine; withhold the name until the final line of the beat"}

London. A wet morning, somewhere in the middle of the eighteen-fifties.

A man comes out of a doorway in Soho and walks east, through Bloomsbury, past the railings and the plane trees, into the forecourt of the British Museum. He shows a reader's ticket. He has held one since the summer of 1850, and for the first seven years he worked in the older rooms — but in 1857 they finished the great circular chamber under the dome, and that is where you should picture him for the rest of his life. Rows of desks radiating out from the centre like the spokes of a wheel. A gallery of books running all the way round. Above it that enormous pale dome, and beneath the dome an absolute, upholstered, coughing silence.

The room is free. That is the miracle of it. Any respectable person with a ticket may sit at one of those desks, fill out a slip, and have very nearly any printed thing they can name carried to their desk, free, from the greatest collection on earth.

He arrives when it opens. He stays. He keeps the hours of that room the way other men keep the hours of an office, and he does it for years, and then for decades. Slip after slip. Notebook after notebook. Price series, wage tables, output figures, reports of committees, the whole grey printed output of the most powerful commercial empire in the history of the world, requested one volume at a time by a foreigner who cannot vote in it.

Now the other room. {pause: 0.8}

Back west, in Soho, there are two of them — two rooms — on Dean Street, up above a shop, and in them live the man, his wife, their children, and a housekeeper who stays with the family for the rest of her life and is never really paid. A Prussian police agent who talked his way inside wrote up what he found: broken furniture, everything under a layer of dust, manuscripts and children's toys and the remains of meals on the same table, nowhere clean for a visitor to sit. The rent goes unpaid. Things go regularly to the pawnshop, including, more than once, his coat — which means that on certain days the man cannot go to the Reading Room at all, because he has nothing respectable to wear into it.

And three of his children died in those rooms. Two infants. And then, in the spring of 1855, a boy of eight, the one the family called Musch, who was his father's delight, and whose death broke something in him that the letters show never quite mended. Poor children died in that London by the thousand; being a philosopher bought no exemption from the arithmetic he happened to be studying.

So hold the two rooms side by side, because the gap between them is almost comic, and it is the reason this episode exists. In the small one: a stateless refugee whom three countries have expelled, who cannot reliably keep his coat out of pawn. In the large one: that same man, at a desk, filling notebook after notebook with what he believes is nothing less than the law of motion of modern society — the hidden mechanism of all human history, the thing that every philosopher and economist and statesman in Europe has been looking straight at and failing to see.

One of those two estimates of him was badly wrong. It turned out not to be his.

His name was Karl Marx.

## How he got to that desk   {tone: measured, note: "the biography, compressed and unsentimental; Engels named as collaborator and financier only — a passing presence, his own story explicitly deferred to Ep 16"}

He did not set out to be a revolutionary. He set out to be a professor.

Born in Trier, in the Rhineland, in 1818, into a comfortable professional family — his father a lawyer who had converted from Judaism to Lutheranism in order to keep practising, which tells you something about the Prussia the son grew up in. University at Bonn and Berlin. A doctorate. And then the discovery that a young man with his politics and his associations was never going to be handed a lecture hall in that country. So he went into journalism, which is the traditional refuge of the unemployable clever, and within a year he was editing a liberal paper in Cologne, and a few months after that the Prussian censors shut it.

From there his career becomes a map of expulsions.

Prussia first. Then Paris, where he falls in among the radical émigrés and reads political economy seriously for the first time — until France expels him, at Prussia's request. Then Brussels, until Belgium expels him too. Back into Germany for the great year, 1848, editing again, and expelled again when that collapses. Somewhere in the middle of it he formally renounces his Prussian citizenship and never acquires another one. Much later he applies for British naturalisation and is refused; the Home Office file survives, with a police report describing him as a notorious agitator. So for the last thirty-four years of his life, the most consequential political thinker of the century is a man with no state at all, living on the sufferance of the one country in Europe relaxed enough not to bother deporting him.

And he lives, in large part, on another man's money.

That man is Friedrich Engels — son of a German mill-owning family, sent to England to learn the cotton trade, who spent years inside a Manchester firm drawing an income from precisely the industry his friend was dissecting, and posting a steady stream of it to Dean Street and later to Kentish Town. He matters enormously to this story and he gets his own hearing next time, so I am going to hold nearly all of him back. For now keep just one fact in view, and not as a joke at his expense: the most famous indictment of industrial capitalism ever written was underwritten by the profits of a cotton mill, and neither of the two men involved found that especially awkward. By the end of this episode you will understand why. The explanation is already lying there in the irony.

## What was on the desk   {tone: curious, note: "bring the industrial world in through the documents Marx actually read; establish the woman at the frame as the episode's single handrail — dated to the early 1840s so her twelve-hour day is lawful; she must not be swapped for another figure later; factual, no invented quotation"}

Now to the slips he was filling out, because his choice of reading is the whole of his method.

He was not, in that room, mainly reading philosophy. He was reading the British government's own paperwork. The parliamentary blue books. Commission evidence. Reports on public health, on mines, on bakeries, on the employment of children. And above all the half-yearly returns of the factory inspectors — a small corps of officials with the legal power to walk into a private mill, examine the workforce and prosecute the owner — who wrote down, every six months, in flat administrative prose, exactly what they had found.

Open one of those on the desk beside him and step into a Lancashire cotton mill, in the early eighteen-forties — before the ten-hour law, when a grown woman's working day still had no legal ceiling on it whatsoever. Fix that date, because we will be coming back to this mill for the next half hour, and the year is going to matter.

A shed full of machinery driven from a single steam engine, belts running to every frame from a shaft overhead. Raw cotton off a ship, from a plantation. Several hundred people on the floor. Now put one of them at the centre of the picture and keep her there, because she is the person this whole episode is about: a woman at a spinning frame. She is there twelve hours, six days a week, performing one operation, in air kept deliberately hot and damp so the thread will not snap, in a noise that means nobody speaks. Among the smaller figures moving between the machines are children — because when the frames run, waste gathers underneath them, and a child is the right size to get under a running machine and clear it. That is not incidental. That is why children were valuable in that building, and it is why some of them went home from it with fewer fingers than they arrived with.

And the law on the matter, when those reports began in the 1830s, said this: a child of nine could lawfully be worked nine hours a day, and up to forty-eight hours in the week. {pause: 0.6}

Hear that number properly. That was not the abuse. That was the *reform.* That was Parliament intervening, against the industry's furious resistance, to bring the working day of a nine-year-old *down* to nine hours.

One more thing about that mill, and everything else depends on it. It was not backward. It was the most advanced arrangement of human effort that had ever existed anywhere on earth. Visitors came from all over Europe to look at the cotton districts the way you would go and look at a launch pad. Whatever is wrong in that shed is not a hangover from the old world. It is the new one, working.

## Nobody broke the law   {tone: measured, note: "the through-line question; run the entire liberal-rights catalogue on HER; make the intactness of every right the scandal; the law's gendered vocabulary is turned into part of the argument, not left as a slip; open the fork; no Marxian doctrine named yet"}

The strangest feature of that shed takes a moment to see, because it is an absence.

Nothing illegal is happening in it.

Run the place against everything this series has spent fourteen episodes constructing. Hobbes wanted us out of the war of all against all, safe from violent death — and nobody on that floor is being murdered by a warlord; the Queen's peace holds in Lancashire. Locke wanted life, liberty and property secured by law under a government resting on consent — and the owner's property is secure, the worker's tenancy is a contract, the courts are open, Parliament sits, the franchise is widening. Montesquieu wanted power divided so it could not crush the individual — and it is divided; that is the only reason an inspector can take an owner to court at all. Kant wanted a condition in which each person's freedom can coexist with everyone else's under a universal law — and there is a universal law here, binding the mill owner and the spinner in identical terms.

And above all: nobody was forced. The woman at the frame was not dragged into that building at first light. She came. She entered a free contract, offering her labour for a wage, and either party may end it. She is not a serf; no lord holds her person or her soil. She is not a slave; nobody bought her. She is, in the full and precise sense that four centuries of European political thought have laboured to define, a free *man.*

And that is not my slip. That is the vocabulary the law itself used for the person it was formally liberating. The rights of man. The free man. The man who may dispose of himself as he sees fit. She cannot vote, and will not be able to in her lifetime. And through most of the years we are talking about, in England, if she married, the wage she earned at that frame was legally her husband's to collect. So the great catalogue of liberties was drawn up for a kind of person she was not quite permitted to be — and even so, she holds enough of it to make the point I am about to put to you. That is what the shed shows. Almost the whole catalogue can be in place, perfectly undamaged, and her life can still be exactly what it is.

Because her children are under the machines. And she will be dead a great many years before the gentleman who owns the frames. And if you ask her what her liberty has bought her, she will struggle to find the answer. {pause: 1.2}

So: do the rights reach into that room? I would rather you took a swing at that now, before anybody hands you a system to answer it with.

Not *should* they. Do they. She holds, on paper, very nearly the whole catalogue: legal freedom, formal equality before the law, property in her own person, the right to contract, courts she may enter, a government that in principle rests on the consent of people like her. Is she free? {pause: 1.0}

And if your honest answer is *not really,* then a fork opens, and everything after it depends on which way you go. Either the rights are a good thing that simply has not arrived in that room yet, and the work is to push them further in. {pause: 0.6} Or the rights are something else entirely — a beautifully painted surface stretched over a much cruder machine, and the machine, not the paint, is where her life is actually being decided.

Serious people took both roads. I will bring you the best advocates for the first one later — one of them standing in a street full of gunsmoke, the other holding a notebook on a factory floor, and it happens to be the second man's notebook lying open on Marx's desk. But the man at that desk took the other road, and took it further than anyone before or since. He got there by picking up the largest philosophical machine of the age and turning it upside down.

## Keep the engine, flip it over   {tone: lucid, note: "the Hegel inversion; then NAME THE DIFFICULTY honestly and give a reason to stay"}

Last episode ended on a promise, and it is time to keep it.

We left Hegel having built the most ambitious contraption in the history of philosophy: history as a process that genuinely *moves,* driven by its own contradictions, climbing towards freedom — freedom becoming real, at last, in the institutions of an ethical community. And I told you a young admirer would keep the whole engine and turn it over.

The turn has a famous shape. Marx says that in Hegel the method is standing on its head, and that it has to be set the right way up if you want the rational kernel out of the mystical shell.

Right side up. That is where this episode gets its title, and the image is doing real work, so we should handle it carefully.

What does he *keep?* Very nearly everything. History has a direction. It moves by conflict rather than accumulation. Every social order manufactures, out of its own ordinary functioning, the forces that will destroy it and the order that will replace it. Freedom is what the whole process is heading towards. He keeps all of that — and he keeps the intoxicating confidence that comes with it, the conviction that the past is not a heap of accidents but something *legible,* and that a person who has read it properly can see where it must go next.

What does he throw out? The driver. For Hegel, the thing moving through history, the thing whose biography history *is,* is spirit. Geist. Mind, consciousness, ideas making themselves real in the world. And Marx says: no. That is the world seen as a photographic negative. Ideas do not drive history. Ideas are the *steam* — the visible white plume above an engine that is actually made of iron and coal and human muscle and somebody's ownership of all three.

He put it, in an early manuscript, about as bluntly as it can be put. It is not consciousness that determines life. It is life that determines consciousness.

Now — {pause: 0.6} this is the hardest turn in the episode, and possibly in the whole series, so let me be straight with you about that and give you a reason to stay. It is not hard because the sentence is hard. The sentence is easy. It is hard because the shallow version of it is absolutely everywhere, and the shallow version is wrong, and if you settle for it you will spend the rest of your life either dismissing Marx cheaply or agreeing with a Marx who never existed. Give me the next ten minutes and you will have the real thing, which is a great deal harder to answer.

## Where did you get that idea?   {tone: curious, note: "ON-RAMP FROM THE FAMILIAR, then estrange; build materialism out of the listener's own convictions; the doctrine stays unnamed"}

Start with something you already have.

You hold political convictions. Views about what is fair, about what people are owed, about how much of your money the state may take and what it owes you in return. And you did not receive those as commandments. You arrived at them — you have read, argued, changed your mind, dropped positions you found you could not defend. They feel like conclusions, because that is what they are.

Now a rude question, and then a much more interesting one.

The rude question: how much of what you believe about fairness would survive being born into a different position? You know the honest answer roughly, and it is *some but not all.* Nobody's convictions float entirely free of their circumstances, and most of us know somebody whose principles fit their bank balance a little too snugly for coincidence.

But the rude question is not Marx's question, and this is exactly where he gets flattened. His question is not *are you a hypocrite.* It is stranger, and much more unsettling, than that.

His question is: where did the whole *menu* come from? Not your answer — the range of available answers. The shared floor of assumptions that makes some positions thinkable, some arguable, and some simply peculiar.

Consider that a Roman senator, a medieval abbot and a Lancashire mill owner would each have given you a sincere, considered, deeply held account of what justice requires, and that those three accounts barely overlap at any point. Not because two of them were lying. Each of them found his own answer *obvious.* The abbot did not experience the proposition that some men are born to till the ground and others to pray for their souls as an ideology he had adopted. He experienced it as the shape of the world, the way you experience gravity.

So what actually changed between them? Marx's answer: look at how each of those societies fed and clothed and housed itself, and at who owned the means of doing it. The abbot's world runs on land, oxen, hand tools and bound labour. The mill owner's world runs on coal, iron, steam, wage labour and shipping. Change what a society produces with, and who owns the producing, and what is *obvious* changes with it.

He had a line for this, from a book of 1847, half a joke and entirely serious. The hand-mill, he says, gives you a society with feudal lords. The steam-mill gives you industrial capitalists.

And now go back into the shed holding that. The doctrine that makes that shed lawful — that a free person may contract their labour on any terms they please, and it is nobody's business but theirs and their employer's — was, at that moment, the most advanced moral thinking in Europe. Genuinely progressive. Genuinely liberating. It had broken the guilds, the serf's bond, the whole apparatus of inherited rank. And it did something else that nobody set out to do. It made the sentence *her children work nine hours a day and it is no one's business but hers* sound not monstrous, but reasonable.

## The base and the painted house   {tone: lucid, note: "NAME AS PAYOFF — historical materialism; build it piece by piece on the mill; forces sit OUTSIDE the base (Cohen) because the fettering argument needs that gap; then the primacy thesis and the accusation against the whole series"}

The country you have just been walking around in has a name. It is called *historical materialism,* and it is Marx's single largest contribution to political thought — the part that survives even for people who reject every other thing he said. So we build it properly, one piece at a time, using nothing but that mill as the handrail.

Piece one: the *forces of production.* Concretely, everything a society produces with. The tools, the machines, the raw materials, the land — and, crucially, the skills and knowledge inside people's heads. In our mill: the frames, the steam engine, the shafting, the coal, the cotton, and the acquired human competence to run all of it without dying. That is the productive power of that society, described physically.

Piece two: the *relations of production.* That means who stands in what position with respect to all of the above. Who owns the machinery. Who owns nothing but their own capacity to work, and therefore must sell it to somebody who does own machinery. Who gives orders on the floor and who takes them. Who keeps the cloth at the end. In our mill: one man owns the building, the engine and the frames; several hundred people own their hands and their hours; and the cloth belongs to the first man.

Those relations — that arrangement of ownership and command, and nothing else — are what Marx calls the *base,* or the economic structure. Hold that word, and notice very carefully what is *not* inside it. The machines are not in the base. The skills are not in the base. The forces of production sit *outside* the arrangement; they are what the arrangement exists to house. Keep that gap in view, because in about five minutes it does an enormous amount of work: it is the reason a growing productive power can turn the very arrangement that once sheltered it into a shackle.

Piece three: everything else. The law of property and contract. The courts. Parliament. The police. The schools. The churches. The newspapers. The philosophy, the morality, the settled common sense of the age. The whole world of ideas and institutions a society erects above its economic base. That is the *superstructure.*

And now the claim itself, which he set out most compactly in a preface written in 1859. The base *explains* the superstructure, and not the other way round. The mode of production of material life conditions the whole process of social, political and intellectual life. The law of free contract is not a discovery human reason made and then generously applied to industry; it is the legal form an economy of wage labour requires in order to run, and it turned up when that economy did. The philosophy according to which a person is self-owning and may dispose of themselves as they see fit in a market — that philosophy is what a society organised around exactly such transactions produces when it sits down to think about itself.

That is the *primacy thesis.* The base is primary; the superstructure derivative. And I hope you can hear how large a claim it is. {pause: 1.0}

Because look what it does to this series. Fourteen episodes, one question: what makes authority legitimate? We have had it answered by the polis, by natural law, by covenant, by consent, by the general will, by pure reason, by spirit. Marx's response is not to add a fifteenth answer to that list.

His response is that the question is itself a piece of superstructure. That *legitimacy* is the story a particular ownership arrangement tells about why it deserves to go on existing — and that every one of those magnificent answers is best read not as a discovery about justice but as a *symptom.* The noise a specific economy makes when it explains itself to itself. Aquinas's serene hierarchy of law, arriving in a world of manors and bound peasants. Locke's sacred property, arriving alongside the improving landlord and the colonial plantation. Kant's autonomous rational will, arriving with the free market in human labour.

If he is right, then a great deal of what you and I have been doing together has been reading the paint and calling it the house. {pause: 1.0}

That accusation is going to hang over every remaining episode of this series, and I will not be able to dispose of it. Neither will anyone else.

## The fetter   {tone: measured, note: "development thesis + fettering; run it on feudalism→capitalism concretely, then turn the same logic on capitalism; include the Manifesto's genuine awe"}

But historical materialism is not only a claim about where ideas come from. It is an engine — he kept Hegel's engine — and an engine needs something that makes it turn. Two moving parts do that.

The first is what the philosophers who reconstructed him call the *development thesis.* Across history, the productive forces tend to grow. Human beings accumulate technique. Knowledge does not easily un-happen; tools get better; the capacity to produce ratchets upward — unevenly, with long stalls and real catastrophic reversals, but over the long run in one direction.

The second is the pressure that follows from it. A given set of relations of production — a given arrangement of who owns what — survives for as long as it can *host* that growth. When the growth outruns it, when the arrangement starts obstructing the productive power that has developed underneath it, the arrangement is finished. Marx's word for what it has become is a *fetter.* A shackle. Relations that were once the very framework within which production expanded turn into chains upon it. And then, he says, comes an era of social revolution.

Watch the machine work once, on the ground it was built to explain.

Feudal Europe: land, hand tools, custom, and a peasant tied to one lord's particular soil, owing labour and dues, forbidden to simply pack up and leave. For several centuries that arrangement worked perfectly adequately — it organised production about as well as a world of oxen and water-wheels required. Now introduce the steam-mill. A factory needs things feudalism structurally cannot supply: thousands of people who can be hired and fired at will; people who can *move,* to where the coal and the water and the port are; people who own nothing anchoring them to a village and who therefore need a wage to eat. It needs land that can be bought and sold rather than held by inherited right. It needs law that treats a contract as sovereign and a person's ancestral station as irrelevant.

So the new productive forces demanded new relations — and the class whose position in the emerging economy made it their urgent business to get them went out and *broke* the old ones. That, on this reading, is what the great revolutions and reform acts and legal upheavals of the early modern world actually were, underneath the language of liberty and right in which they were fought and are still remembered. Not in the first place an advance in human moral understanding. A change of hosts, because the old host had become a fetter.

And Marx *admired* it, which people forget. Read the pamphlet he and Engels wrote in the first weeks of 1848 and you will find some of the most exhilarated prose about capitalism ever composed. The bourgeoisie, they say, has played a most revolutionary part. It tore up a thousand years of frozen hierarchy, dissolved every fixed and ancient relation, hauled the entire planet into a single market, and multiplied humanity's productive power beyond anything the ancient world could have dreamed. All that is solid melts into air. In their mouths, that is not a lament. It is closer to awe.

Now feel where the argument is obliged to go next. {pause: 0.8}

If an ownership arrangement dies when it begins to fetter production, then the interesting question is not whether capitalism is cruel. Marx thinks it is spectacularly cruel — but cruelty does not kill a social order. Feudalism was cruel for a millennium and it took a steam engine to finish it. The real question is whether *this* arrangement will, in its turn, begin obstructing the productive power it brought into being. And he thought he could see it starting: an economy of unprecedented abundance that convulses every few years into crisis; warehouses full and people hungry in the same month; machines standing idle and hands turned away at the gate, not because there is too little but because there is too much to sell at a profit. A system running into the limits of its own logic. Which is exactly why he expected it to go the way of the manor.

## So what he is actually saying   {tone: contemplative, note: "CHECKPOINT — short plain sentences; restate; let it settle"}

Stop for a moment and take the whole of it in flat words, because we have climbed a fair distance and I need it to hold.

Every society has to eat. To eat, it has to produce, using tools and land and skills and machines. Those things get more powerful over time. To produce with them, human beings have to stand in some definite relation to one another: who owns, who works, who commands, who keeps the product. That arrangement is the foundation of the society. Everything else — its laws, its politics, its religion, its moral common sense, its philosophy — grows on top of that foundation and, in the main, justifies it. When the arrangement can no longer carry the productive power that has grown up underneath it, it cracks; and the class whose position in the new productive world gives it both the interest and the strength to break the old order proceeds to break it. And afterwards, the new order's laws and morality and philosophy will look, to the people living inside them, self-evident. {pause: 0.8}

That is historical materialism. Not "money makes people selfish." A theory of how whole societies change, in which the deepest cause is not what people believe but how they produce, and who owns the producing.

## Not a puppet show   {tone: measured, note: "PRE-EMPT THE MISREADING; then hold the genuinely contested determinism question open — Cohen vs looser readings, Engels's late letter"}

Now the misreading, because it is the one nearly everybody makes.

It is tempting to hear all that as: economics causes everything, ideas are irrelevant, individuals do not matter, people are puppets and history is a machine. If that were the claim it would be easy to knock down — and it would also make nonsense of the man's own life. Somebody who genuinely believed ideas were inert does not spend thirty years in a reading room manufacturing one. And he certainly does not write, as Marx did in a private note in 1845, that the philosophers have only interpreted the world in various ways, and that the point is to *change* it.

The most careful sentence he ever wrote on this comes from a short book about French politics. Men make their own history — but they do not make it just as they please. They do not make it under circumstances of their own choosing, but under circumstances directly encountered, given and inherited from the past.

Both halves matter. You act. You genuinely act, and it genuinely tells. But you act inside a situation you did not select, using materials handed to you — including the ideas in your head and the very words you think them in — by a world that was already there when you arrived. That is a real position, and a great deal harder to wave away than the puppet show.

There is something else here that has never been settled, and you should hear it from me rather than trip over it later.

How *strong* is the primacy of the base supposed to be? On one reading — argued most rigorously by the philosopher G. A. Cohen, whose reconstruction gives us the crisp version of the development and primacy theses I have been leaning on — it is close to a genuine causal machine, with the growth of productive technology as the deep motor and everything else, in the final analysis, accommodating itself to that. On other readings it is a far looser thing: a tendency, an emphasis, an instruction about where to look first, with law and politics and belief carrying real causal weight of their own. And there is textual support on both sides, because Marx wrote thousands of pages across forty years and did not write them as a system.

Engels, near the end of his life, was pressed on exactly this by a correspondent, and his answer was strikingly modest: the production and reproduction of real life is the *ultimately* decisive element, and neither he nor Marx had ever claimed more than that. Whether that is the authentic doctrine or a late softening of it is — genuinely — still argued about. It is one of the reasons Engels needs an episode of his own.

I am not going to pretend to settle it for you. But notice that even the loose version is a serious accusation. In fact the loose version is the one that is hardest to escape.

## The thing you made walks away   {tone: measured, note: "DWELL — alienation; intuition first, from the woman at the frame — the SAME figure as before; four dimensions defined plainly; contingency is the punchline; implicate the listener at the end"}

Set the machinery down for a few minutes, because there is an earlier, angrier, more human piece of Marx that we need. It comes from a set of notebooks he filled in Paris in 1844, at twenty-five. He never published them. They sat unread for close to ninety years, surfaced in the 1930s, and landed in the middle of the twentieth century like a letter from a dead man.

Back to the frame. Back to her.

Twelve hours a day, six days a week, one operation. The thread that comes off it is not hers. She will never see the cloth it becomes, never hold the finished shirt, never meet the person who wears it. Everything she makes leaves the building the moment it exists, and goes out into a world where it will meet her again, if at all, as somebody else's property in a shop window, priced past her reach. The more she produces, the larger and richer the thing outside her becomes — and the more of her own life has gone into it, and the poorer she remains. Her own output stands up, walks out, and joins the other side.

That is the first face of what Marx calls *alienation:* estrangement from your own product. The word means, plainly, that something which came out of you has become foreign to you — a thing over there, no longer recognisably yours.

The second face cuts deeper, because it is not about the product at all. It is about the *doing.* Ask her whether she is present in those twelve hours; whether the work expresses anything she is. She would look at you oddly. Those hours are not her life. They are what she sells in order to have a life during the few hours that are left. She is at home when she is not working and not at home when she is. The activity that fills most of her waking existence — the exercise of her own strength and attention and skill — belongs to another person while it is happening. It is not the fulfilment of her. It is the price of her survival.

The third face is other people. Under this arrangement, nearly everyone she deals with is structurally set against her. The owner's gain is her loss at the wage bargain, and hers is his. The other hands are her competitors; every hungry family at the gate is a reason her wage can be cut. The buyer wants the cloth cheap, which means her hours cheap. Solidarity, in that shed, has to be *built* — deliberately, against the grain of the thing — because the arrangement itself sorts people into opposed positions and then invites them to call the result human nature.

And the fourth is the hardest term, so take it slowly. Marx says she is alienated from her *species-being.* A strange phrase, inherited from the German philosophy he grew up inside, and its plain content is this. He thinks the distinctive thing about human beings — the capacity that makes us the kind of creature we are — is that we shape the world *on purpose.* We can form an intention, work up a plan, and make matter conform to it. Other animals build; a spider spins and a swift nests. But the spider does not consider alternative webs, and does not spin one for the pleasure of spinning it well. We do. We can produce for reasons beyond immediate need, produce beautifully, produce for other people, stand back and see ourselves in the thing we made. Free conscious production, for Marx, is the human essence.

Which means — and this is what gets missed most often — work is not the villain of his story. Making, building, shaping: that is the very thing he thinks we are *for.* And that is precisely why a form of work which reduces a human being to a hand on a lever, repeating one motion for twelve hours in order not to starve, is not merely unpleasant. It is a kind of amputation. It takes the single capacity that constitutes us and converts it into a means of staying alive — and it does that to the majority of the human race.

Now the move that separates Marx from ordinary melancholy about modern life. {pause: 0.6}

All four of those estrangements, he insists, are *contingent.* They are not the human condition. Not the price of civilisation, not original sin, not the tragic cost of complexity, not simply how labour must always feel. They are the effects of one particular arrangement of ownership, in one particular period, and if the arrangement changed they would go.

That is a real philosophical claim and a real wager, and it is what makes him a revolutionary rather than a pessimist.

And if you want to test whether any of it still bites, do not reach for a factory. Reach for whatever you do all day. {pause: 0.6} Is what you produce recognisably yours? Are you present in the doing of it, or renting those hours out? Do the people beside you gain when you gain, or is the arrangement quietly zero-sum? {pause: 1.2}

Because if some of that landed, then the question you are now holding is his question. And it is not answered by reminding you that you are legally free to resign.

## One careful pass at the arithmetic   {tone: lucid, note: "COMPRESS — labour theory of value and surplus value in a single disciplined pass, staying with HER; punchline is that no cheating is required; then one honest sentence conceding what later economics did to the theory"}

Which brings us, once and only once, into the economics. There is one piece of *Capital* you genuinely need. I will take it slowly and then leave it alone.

Marx wants to answer a question that sounds childish until you actually try it. Where does profit come from?

Not the easy cases. Not fraud, not monopoly, not squeezing a supplier or fleecing a customer — those explain particular fortunes, not the general fact. He wants to know where profit comes from *in general,* in an honest market, where everything exchanges at its proper value and nobody is cheating anybody at all.

His starting point he takes over openly from the classical economists: the *labour theory of value.* The value of a commodity is determined by the amount of labour it takes to produce — and Marx sharpens that into a phrase worth getting exactly right. Not labour. *Socially necessary labour time.* Not how long *you* personally dawdled over it; how long it takes on average, with the normal skill and the normal technology of that society. Which is why, once machines exist, a beautifully hand-woven length of cloth is worth no more than the machine-woven equivalent: the socially necessary time has collapsed, and your extra hours simply do not count. The market is brutally indifferent to your craftsmanship.

Now the puzzle. If everything trades at its value, then buying and selling can only move value around. Nobody gets richer as a *class* by swapping equivalents. So profit has to be coming from somewhere inside production.

Marx's answer is a genuinely elegant piece of reasoning. What she sells at that frame is not cloth, and it is not exactly a day's labour. It is her *capacity to work* — her labour-power — rented out for a fixed period. And like any other commodity, that capacity has a value: what it costs to produce and maintain it. Food, rent, clothing, fuel, enough to raise the next generation of workers. Suppose all that comes to the equivalent of four hours of work a day.

But she has been hired for twelve. {pause: 0.8}

In the first four hours she reproduces the value of her own wage. In the remaining eight she goes on working, and everything she creates in those eight hours belongs, by contract, to the man who owns the frame. Marx calls it *surplus value,* and it is his answer: profit is unpaid labour time, extracted lawfully, in daylight, in the open, with the account books straight.

And now the sentence that makes the whole apparatus dangerous. On this account, *nobody cheated.* The wage was fair — she received the full value of her labour-power. The contract was free; both parties consented; any court in England would enforce it. There was no fraud anywhere in the transaction. And exploitation happened regardless, structurally, as a matter of arithmetic, because the thing purchased can produce more value than it costs.

If that is right, look what it does to the shed. You cannot fix it by enforcing contracts better, because no contract was broken. You cannot fix it with equality before the law, because there *was* equality before the law. The wrong is not a violation of the rules. The wrong *is* the rules, working properly.

One honest footnote is owed. Later economics largely abandoned the labour theory of value; the profession came to explain prices by scarcity and demand at the margin rather than by embodied labour time, and that remains the mainstream view. So the technical machinery in this beat is seriously contested on its own ground, and I will not pretend otherwise. What has proved much harder to dislodge is the structural insight the machinery was built to carry: that a distribution can be produced entirely by voluntary, lawful, consensual transactions and still stand in need of a justification nobody inside it ever gave.

## The debt he admitted   {tone: warm, note: "the genealogy beat — the spine of the series; Marx as heir; the vulgar-economy distinction shows what he thought thinking was"}

And notice where he got the tools.

The labour theory of value was not his invention. It was the common property of the classical economists — Adam Smith, whom we met in Episode 12 wearing his other hat as a moral philosopher, and David Ricardo after him. Marx read them for years at that desk. He was frequently scathing about them. And he never once stopped insisting that they had been doing real science.

His praise is specific, and it tells you what he thought thinking *was.* Smith and Ricardo, he said, tried to get behind the appearances of the market to its inner structure — to find, underneath the froth of prices and haggling, the essential relations that generate all of it. That, for Marx, is what a science does. His contempt was reserved for what he called *vulgar* political economy: the writers who never got past the surface, who wrote down what the market looks like from inside a counting house and mistook that description for an explanation — and who, entirely sincerely, produced apologetics under the impression they were producing analysis.

So the great antagonist of capitalism assembled his critique out of capitalism's own best economists, sitting in the library of the world's leading capitalist power, working from statistics gathered by that power's own inspectors. He is not an outsider throwing rocks over a wall. He is an heir. That is this series' oldest lesson and it holds even here, at the point of sharpest rupture: nobody thinks from nowhere. Hegel handed him the engine. Smith and Ricardo handed him the machinery of value. The cotton districts handed him the subject. He built the most influential critique in the history of political thought out of parts inherited from the tradition he was attacking.

## Four days in June   {tone: tense, note: "1848 and the June Days as the class split made visible; real named figures; hedge the casualty figures honestly; then Tocqueville as diagnostician with his own complicity conceded"}

Which brings us to the one moment when he believed he had watched the theory happen in the street.

In February 1848 a small London printer produced a pamphlet of barely more than twenty pages for a tiny émigré society. It announced that the history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggle, and it closed by calling on the workers of the world to unite. Within weeks — and by pure coincidence; the timing was luck and nothing else — Europe went up.

Paris first: barricades, the king gone, a republic proclaimed. Then Vienna, Berlin, Milan, Budapest, Prague. Governments that had looked eternal in January were negotiating with crowds by March. Marx and Engels went straight into Germany, into the middle of it, and started a newspaper in Cologne.

But it is what happened in Paris that summer that he never stopped thinking about.

The February revolution had been made by a coalition. Liberal reformers, professionals, shopkeepers — and the workers of the eastern faubourgs, arm in arm with them, under the word *fraternity.* The new republic opened national workshops to give the unemployed something to do. Then, through the spring, the propertied half of that coalition looked at the cost and at the crowds and grew frightened. In June the government closed the workshops and told the men to enlist in the army or leave the city.

Paris rose. And the republic that had been born four months earlier in the name of brotherhood handed emergency powers to General Louis-Eugène Cavaignac, who took the working-class districts street by street with artillery. Four days. Thousands killed in the fighting, thousands more arrested and transported to the colonies — the precise counts were disputed then and are disputed now.

Marx wrote it up in his paper, and his reading was merciless and, on its own terms, perfectly simple. February's fraternity was a fiction that lasted exactly as long as it took the two classes inside it to notice they wanted opposite things. When the crunch came, the liberals did not stand with the workers against reaction. They stood with the army against the workers. And that, he says, is not a betrayal by wicked individuals. That is what a class does when its property is in question, and everything else is scenery.

Now, there was another man in that city that summer, and I am not going to let him stand mute, because he saw exactly what Marx saw and drew the opposite conclusion from it.

Alexis de Tocqueville was sitting in the National Assembly through all of it. He is the man who, years before, had walked through the English cotton towns and written that from this foul drain flows the greatest stream of human industry, out to fertilise the whole world. Weeks before the February rising he had stood up in the Chamber and told his complacent colleagues that they were sleeping on a volcano. Then he watched the volcano go off — and what appalled him was not only the killing but what the killing revealed: that his society had come apart into two peoples who no longer recognised any common interest whatsoever.

Same diagnosis as Marx. And before I let him reply, I have to concede the thing that makes him an awkward witness — because I have just told you the liberals sided with the army, and he was one of them. He was no bystander to the June repression. He was a deputy who supported the state of siege that handed Cavaignac his powers; he went out into the streets among the government's troops while the fighting was going on; and in the memoir he wrote afterwards he defended the crushing of the insurrection as a grim necessity. Marx's charge about where men of property stand when property is at stake is not a caricature of Tocqueville. It is a description of what he did, and he never pretended otherwise.

So why? Not simple cowardice, and not merely his own acres. He said what he feared, in that same Assembly, in that same year, when the socialists proposed to write a right to work into the constitution. Make the state the owner of the workshops and the employer of every hand in the country, he argued, and you have not distributed power — you have gathered all of it into one place, and left no independent ground anywhere in the society from which any single individual could ever resist it. You would get equality. You would get it under one master. A man who had spent his life studying how free societies keep their footing thought property — widely dispersed, legally secure — was one of the few things standing between a citizen and an omnipotent government.

That fear is worth taking seriously, and the twentieth century did not exactly refute it. But it is still only half an answer to Marx, because it says what *not* to do. It does not say what to do about the shed. And for that, this episode needs a different man entirely — one who has been lying on the desk the whole time.

## The man who wrote the reports   {tone: measured, note: "THE REFORMIST ROAD GETS ITS REAL CARRIER — Leonard Horner, whose half-yearly returns Marx quarried and whom Marx praises by name in Capital; steelman the reform case at its strongest, INCLUDING the hours statutes that cut her own day and the relay-system prosecutions; mark the speech clearly as reconstruction AND name Marx as the person being addressed, so the 'you' inside it does not land on the listener"}

Because the other road out of that mill was not walked by a French deputy or a philosopher. It was walked, of all unlikely things, by civil servants.

Go back to the blue books on Marx's desk and look at them as a reformer would. That nine-hour limit on a child's day was not a law of nature and it was not a gift from the mill owners. Parliament imposed it, in 1833, against the industry's furious resistance — and it did something more radical than the limit itself. It appointed inspectors: paid officials with legal authority to enter a private factory, examine the children, and prosecute the owner.

One of the first four men appointed to that job was a Scot named Leonard Horner, and he drew the cotton districts. Lancashire. The very mills we have been standing in. He held the post for the better part of a quarter of a century, and he spent it in a grinding, unglamorous war on two fronts: against manufacturers who lied to him, ran the machines through the meal breaks, kept two sets of clocks and dragged him through the courts — and against his own government, which was perpetually inclined to soften the law he was employed to enforce. He pushed for clocks an inspector could check, for age certificates he could verify, for guards on the machinery. He lost a great deal of the time. And every six months he wrote down what had happened, and Parliament printed it.

Which is how he ends up inside *Capital.* Marx read Horner's half-yearly returns in that round room and quarried them for the most devastating pages he ever wrote about the factory — and he named the man and praised him without reservation, saying Horner had rendered undying service to the English working class, and that he had waged a lifelong contest with the manufacturers and with the Cabinet both.

Stay with the shape of that for a moment. The greatest indictment of capitalism ever written is built, in substantial part, out of the paperwork of the reform apparatus that the same book files under superstructure.

So take the reform case at its strongest, in the voice of the road Horner actually walked — and understand who is being answered. Not you. What follows is put to the man at the desk, to Marx himself, and every "you" in it means him. These are my words rather than Horner's, a reconstruction and not a quotation. But it is his case, and he lived every year of it.

*You have shown me a mill and told me it indicts our rights. It does no such thing. It measures how far into that mill we have so far got.* Twenty years ago there was no limit at all on a child's day in a cotton shed, and no official on this earth could cross that threshold without the owner's leave. Now there is a limit, and I can.

*And look again at the year you picked for your shed, because you picked it carefully.* On the day you sat that woman down at her frame, no statute in England said one word about how long a grown woman might be kept there. Since then Parliament has brought women inside the law, and then cut the textile working day to ten hours, and then settled it at ten and a half — *her* day, not merely her children's. The owners answered by inventing the relay system: shuffling their hands in and out in staggered shifts so that no single person could ever be *proved* to have been worked too long. I spent years hauling those men into court over it. I lost cases I should have won, and I brought the next one anyway — and the shorter day is still standing.

*Every instrument in that list is made of the material you keep calling decoration.* The mortality figures you have been copying out were compiled by sanitary reformers who wanted drains and clean water — and who, over the following decades, city by city, by statute and rate and inquiry, *got* them. The franchise was widened in 1832 and will be widened again, and again after that. *The tool that will empty this shed of children is the tool already emptying it — this statute, this inspectorate, this widening vote, applied harder and pushed further in. And against that, you propose to abolish the only machinery that has ever actually reached these people, on a promise that something better will be improvised afterwards.*

That is a serious position, held by serious people, and it was held by the ones with mud on their boots.

Which means Marx's reply has to answer a law that *bit,* not only a law that was evaded.

He starts with the evasion, because it is sitting in his sources: Horner's own returns are, page after page, a chronicle of owners undercutting the Acts faster than a handful of inspectors could prosecute them, and of a government that softened the law whenever the pressure behind it slackened. But then he goes further, and this is the sharper move. Cut her day from twelve hours to ten, he says, and the owner does not simply hand over the difference. He raises the speed of the machinery and the number of frames she must tend, and takes out of ten hours what he used to take out of twelve. Marx followed exactly that in the inspectors' own later reports — the working day shortened by statute, and every pore of it packed tighter in compensation. The hours were a real victory. The ratio came back.

And underneath it sits the thing no inspector can reach, because there is nothing there for an inspector to find. Four hours paid, the rest not — and no clause of any Factory Act, in 1833 or 1847 or 1850, says a single word about who owns the frames.

Keep both of those in your hands. We need them at the end.

By the spring of 1849 the risings had failed nearly everywhere. Marx's paper was suppressed, he was expelled for the last time, and he came to London, and stayed. He had watched his theory confirmed, as he saw it, and defeated, in the same season. And the defeat is what sent him to that desk — not to organise the next revolution, but to answer the harder question of why the thing has the shape it has.

## The rights you would fight for   {tone: measured, note: "DWELL — COMMIT: steelman political emancipation generously and let the listener own it before anything complicates it"}

Now we reach the heart of it: the sharpest thing Marx ever said about everything this series holds dear.

Commit to something first. I will make it easy, because there is no trick in it.

Bring to mind the rights you would personally go to a barricade for. Not abstractions — the specific ones. That the police cannot come through your door without a warrant. That you cannot be held without charge. That what you say about your government is your own business. That your religion, or your lack of one, is nobody's affair. That the law is the same for you as for the wealthiest person in your country. That what you own is yours and cannot be taken on somebody's whim. That you may leave — a job, a marriage, a town, a country.

Those are not decorations. People died in enormous numbers for every item on that list, and there are places on this earth tonight where people are dying for them still. Anyone who sneers at that list has not thought carefully about what its absence is like. {pause: 1.0}

And Marx knew it. This matters, because he is routinely quoted as though he held liberal rights to be worthless, and he did not. He called what those rights achieved a great step forward — a genuine emancipation; the dissolution of a whole world of estates and guilds and hereditary rank and religious disability, in which what you could do and be was fixed at birth by your station. He gave the credit, fully and explicitly.

He simply gave it a name that limits it. He called it *political emancipation* — and set beside it a second thing, which he called *human emancipation,* and said the first is not the second, and that mistaking one for the other is the characteristic illusion of the modern age.

The argument is in an essay from 1843. And I should tell you plainly that it is a text with a real ugliness in it: it works its way towards the political argument through a stretch of writing about Jews and money that trades in the crudest stereotypes of his day, and it has troubled readers ever since, as it should. We are taking the argument about rights. We are not laundering the language it arrived in.

## Rights of separation   {tone: lucid, note: "COMPLICATE — the same rights re-read from below; 'rights of separation'; then human emancipation, the Hegel echo, and the hole in Marx's own answer; the callback at the end must be HER, closing the handrail"}

So take the list you just committed to, and read each item again with one question in mind. What *shape* is this right? What relation between you and other human beings does it assume?

They are all fences. {pause: 0.6}

The French Declaration of Rights, in its 1793 version, defines liberty as the power to do anything that does not harm another. Marx pounces on that formula, and once he has pointed at it you cannot unsee it. Look what it makes of your fellow human beings. They are the *limit* of your freedom. Not its content, not its medium — its boundary. Freedom is your zone; other people are where your zone stops. And every right on your list has that architecture. A wall against the state. A wall against the neighbour. A wall around the property. Rights, he says, that you hold not *with* other people but *against* them.

He calls them *rights of separation* — and the person they are cut to fit is someone withdrawn into himself, into his private interest and his private whim, treated as a self-sufficient atom knocking against other atoms. The right of property, on this reading, is the right to dispose of your things without reference to anyone else. Religious liberty is the right to be left alone inside your own conscience. Even the right to security comes down to society undertaking to guarantee each of its members the preservation of his separateness.

So: political emancipation frees you *from.* From the state's arbitrary hand, from the lord, from the guild, from the priest, from the accident of your birth. Marx's charge is that it does not, and structurally cannot, give you freedom *with.* And he thinks that absence is not an oversight awaiting a patch but the entire design — because the economy these rights serve requires exactly this kind of person: an individual detached from any binding community, free to move, free to sell their hours, meeting everybody else through contract and exchange and in no other way at all.

Which is why the woman at the frame holds every right on your list and is not free. Nobody trespassed on her. Her fences are all perfectly intact. She is legally, formally, immaculately unobstructed — and her fences enclose nothing.

*Human emancipation,* by contrast, would be freedom *in* community. An arrangement in which other people are not the boundary of your freedom but the medium of it; in which what you can become is enlarged rather than fenced by everybody else's existence; in which the productive life of a society is something its members conduct together and recognise themselves in, rather than something confronting them as an alien power that happens, on good days, to employ them.

And you can hear Hegel breathing all the way through that, can't you. Freedom realised in a community rather than possessed against one — that is his teacher's move, kept entire. Marx has simply decided Hegel put freedom's home in the wrong building. Not the state, which Marx regards as part of the paintwork. If freedom is going to become real anywhere, it has to become real where people actually make their living.

Now the honest problem, and it is enormous. {pause: 0.8}

What would that look like? Which institutions? What decides what gets produced? Who decides who does the unpleasant work? What happens to the person who dissents from the collective, and what do they have to say for themselves when it comes for them? On this — the constructive question, the question every other thinker in this series at least attempted — Marx is famously, maddeningly thin. Thousands of pages diagnosing what exists; a scattered handful about what should replace it, most of them vague and some barely more than a phrase. He had a reason: he thought blueprint-drawing was unserious, the vice of utopian dreamers, and that the shape of a new society would be worked out in practice by the people making it.

But a critique that dissolves rights into ideology while declining to say what protects a human being in whatever comes next has left a hole in itself. And history put something in that hole.

## The states with his name on them   {tone: grave, note: "the honest boundary — flag the Marx-vs-Marxism gap and name the one real thread, without litigating the 20th century here"}

Which brings me to the thing you have been waiting for me to say.

You cannot hear the word "Marx" in the twenty-first century without also hearing everything done in his name in the twentieth. The one-party states. The secret police. The famines. The camps. The walls. Tens of millions of dead under governments that quoted these books.

So let me be exact about what this episode is and is not doing. We have been reading Marx: a stateless journalist who died in 1883, whose great work was unfinished, whose notebooks on alienation were not published until the 1930s, and who never in his life held power over another human being. The regimes came afterwards, built by other men in other countries out of selected parts of this material, in circumstances he did not foresee and would very likely not have recognised. Those regimes, and the arguments they forced, deserve a proper hearing of their own rather than a paragraph bolted onto somebody else's episode, and this series will come back to them.

What I will not do is either of the two cheap moves. I will not treat *Capital* as a murder weapon, as though a man who spent thirty years in a library is answerable for everything a later general secretary did with his sentences. And I will not do the opposite thing — the shrug that says none of it was really Marx, that the record is entirely a distortion, so the theory floats serenely above all of it. Because there is at least one thread running from the texts to the outcomes, and I named it a moment ago. If rights are ideology; if the demand for legal protection against power is a symptom of an economic order that is passing; then when a new power arrives claiming to act on behalf of history and of the workers, you have argued yourself out of the only vocabulary you would need in order to resist it. That gap is really in Marx. It is not a slander invented by his enemies. What people built in it is on them. That it was there is on him.

## Back to the desk   {tone: contemplative, note: "ENDING — bookend on the round room and the reports; step from referee to advocate with a verdict that answers the reform case; recast as a beginning; then hand over to Engels for Ep 16 — hint at his Manchester material without spending its reveals"}

Back to the round room, then, because this is where it has to finish.

The dome. The spokes of desks. The silence. A stateless man with a reader's ticket, and in front of him an inspector's report describing a shed he never entered, in a county he seldom visited, full of people he would never meet — and out of that paper he is building the most consequential accusation in the history of political thought.

I have refereed this fight for the better part of an hour. Here is where I come down.

I think he is right about the shed, and being right about the shed is not a small thing — it is the permanent gain. He proved that a person can hold the entire catalogue of formal liberties and still live a life that is not their own; that "nobody violated your rights" and "your life is yours" are two different statements; and that any political theory unable to see the difference is describing a world with the poor quietly edited out. And the habit of mind he leaves behind — the reflex of asking, whenever somebody explains why an arrangement is legitimate, *who owns what here, and whose comfort does this beautiful argument leave undisturbed* — that reflex is now a permanent part of how educated people think, including people who loathe him. You cannot un-learn it. You should not want to.

And I think the strong version of his central claim is too strong — and that the road Leonard Horner walked turned out to be the one that reached the shed. Look at what actually emptied it. Not a revolution: inspectors, statutes, sewers, unions that were legalised rather than victorious, and a franchise that kept widening until the people on that floor could vote out the men who owned the frames. Every one of those instruments is superstructure, and every one of them bit the base. That is the empirical trouble with treating ideas as steam. Locke's argument about consent did work no landowner's interest ever predicted. Kant's argument about the dignity of persons has been turned, over and over again, against the precise economic order Marx says produced it. Arguments get away from the conditions that made them. They have a life of their own, and they bite their own parents. A theory that renders a society's moral vocabulary an echo of its balance sheet cannot explain why that vocabulary keeps being picked up and used to indict the balance sheet — and cannot explain why, in the end, it worked.

And his deepest failure, to me, is the one on that desk. Having taught us to be suspicious of every claim of legitimacy, he left us nothing to defend a human being *with.* The fences he dismissed as rights of separation are indeed thin, cold, individualist things. They are also, on the whole historical record, the only structures anyone has ever built that a person can get behind when the powerful come for them — including powerful people arriving in the name of the workers. Tocqueville's fear turned out to be as well founded as Marx's disgust. Marx saw, correctly, that a fence around an empty yard is not freedom. He never quite conceded that a yard with no fence around it is not freedom either.

But do not let my verdict close this. {pause: 0.8}

Because the accusation does not evaporate because I have registered my objections to it, and it is yours now to carry. Every episode after this one you are going to hear differently. When the next thinker tells you what makes a law legitimate, some part of you is going to want to know what that thinker's society happened to be producing at the time, and who owned the machines. That question is a gift and a curse and you cannot hand it back. The argument is going on right now, in your own country, every time somebody says that a bargain was freely entered into and is therefore just.

He died in March 1883, in London, stateless to the end, with the second and third volumes of his life's work still in the condition you would expect of a man who wrote for thirty years in a reading room: a mountain of notebooks in an all but unreadable hand. About a dozen people came to the grave at Highgate. One of them stood up and said that the name would endure through the ages, and so would the work.

That man was Friedrich Engels — and he then went home and spent the rest of his life making it true. He took the mountain and turned it into books, editing and assembling volume two, and then volume three, out of the manuscripts: adding, deciding, arranging, choosing what the theory would say. And around those volumes he built something Marx never built. A doctrine with a name, a border, and a marching order, handed on to a movement that would carry it into the next century and out across the world.

He also called himself the second fiddle. He was the mill-owner's son who ran cotton by day and financed the critique of capital by night — and years before he ever met Marx, in his early twenties, he had gone down into the courts and cellars of industrial Manchester with a notebook, and come back up with a charge against the respectable people of England that I am not going to spoil here.

So before this series crosses back to Britain, we are going to spend an episode with the man who paid the rent. Because there is a real question about him, and it is not a small one — whether he made Marx legible, or made him rigid. Whether "Marxism" is Marx's. Or his.

The second fiddle. That is next time.
