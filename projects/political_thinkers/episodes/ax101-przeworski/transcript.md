## Alongside, not after   {tone: measured, note: "state the companion framing plainly and get out of the way; then two flat biographical facts, no portent — the flatness is the effect"}

This one sits beside the series rather than inside it. It is a companion piece: a longer look at a single question the socialist episodes kept circling and never quite closed. It is not the next chapter, nothing later depends on it, and if you have not heard the others you will still be able to follow every step of what happens here.

Two facts, then, to start with.

A boy is born in Warsaw on the fifth of May, 1940. The city is under German occupation. It has been for seven months.

His father is not present at the birth, and never will be. His father was a doctor, conscripted as an army doctor, and taken prisoner — not by the Germans, by the Soviets, coming the other way. He was murdered at Katyn, in the mass killing of Polish officers and professionals that the Soviet state then denied for half a century. The son, writing about it much later, puts it with the flatness of a man who has had decades to find a gentler formula and has decided there isn't one. He allows himself one clause about the timing: at about the time I was born.

{pause: 1.0}

So here is a life that begins on the exact seam where the two great tyrannies of the twentieth century met and ground against each other. One of them holding the city he was born in. The other holding the ground his father was buried under.

His name is Adam Przeworski. He grew up to be a political scientist, and the question that organized the most important decade of his work is about as cold as a question can be, and he asked it in exactly these words:

Why was there no revolution in the West?

## The insult inside the explanation   {tone: curious, note: "brisk, faintly needling — this is the received wisdom being turned over and found unpleasant on the underside"}

Notice what an odd question that is, and how much it assumes.

It assumes there should have been one. And by the theory almost everyone in the socialist tradition was working from, there should have been. Marx's expectation pointed straight at the *most* developed capitalist economies — the ones with the biggest factories, the densest cities, the most concentrated and organized and experienced working class. Britain. Germany. The industrial heartlands. Those were the places where the contradictions were supposed to sharpen to breaking point.

And those are precisely the places where nothing of the kind happened. The revolutions, when they came, came in Russia and China and Cuba — agrarian countries, peasant countries, places Marx's own model said were not ready. Meanwhile the German and British and Swedish and French working classes, the ones with every reason and every resource, joined unions, sent delegates to parliaments, bargained over hours and pensions and holiday pay, and by the middle of the century had settled into the arrangement we now call the welfare state and mostly stopped talking about expropriating anybody.

For a century there has been a standard explanation for that, and you have heard versions of it your whole life. The workers were bought off — cheap consumer goods and a mortgage and a fortnight at the seaside, and the revolutionary energy drained away. Or they were deceived: the newspapers, the schools, the church, the whole apparatus manufacturing a consciousness that served somebody else's interests. Or, hardest and most personal, they were sold — their own leaders, the men who ran the parties and the unions, got comfortable inside the institutions they were supposed to be dismantling and quietly changed sides. Lenin called Kautsky a renegade in a title, in print, and the word stuck to him for the rest of the century.

Those explanations are satisfying to make, and they have something in common that is easy to miss while you are enjoying them. Every one of them says the workers did not understand their own situation. Bought off, deceived, betrayed — in each case somebody who is not a worker is telling the workers what their real interests were, and informing them that they failed to act on them. It is an account that flatters the theorist and diminishes the millions of people it is about. Which does not make it false. Plenty of true things are unflattering.

But Przeworski went at the same question with a completely different instrument, and got a completely different answer. He asked: suppose they understood perfectly. Suppose nobody was fooled. Suppose every worker in Germany in 1955 had a clear-eyed grasp of exactly what capitalism was and exactly what it was doing to them. What would it then be rational for them to do?

## The number on the table   {tone: lucid, note: "the handrail is established here — keep it concrete and physical; slow slightly on the two options"}

Before any of the theory, put yourself in a room.

You are a worker in a plant that makes turbines. Big ones — the kind that go into power stations. It is a good plant, it is profitable, and you are in a union that has, for once, real strength: high membership, a credible strike threat, a management that would very much rather not find out how long you can hold out.

You are at the table. The firm's books are open enough that everyone in the room has a rough idea of what the year produced. And you have two ways to play it.

The first: take everything. Push the wage claim right up to the edge of what the enterprise can pay and still keep the doors open. You have the leverage this year. Use it. Every pound left on the table is a pound that ends up as somebody else's profit, and you are the one who stood at the lathe.

The second: ask for less than that. Real gains, a proper rise, but not the maximum — and leave a margin behind.

{pause: 1.2}

Now, the case for taking everything is not stupid and it is not greedy. It rests on something true. The margin you leave is not yours. Once it is profit, the decision about what happens to it belongs to somebody else entirely, and there is nothing in the arrangement that obliges them to do anything with it that helps you. They can build a new production line. They can also buy a bigger house, or a company in another country, or nothing at all. You will not be consulted. You are being asked to give up money you could have today, in exchange for a promise nobody has made you and nobody is required to keep.

That is the honest form of the militant's case, and the twentieth-century labour movement contained an enormous number of extremely intelligent people who made it.

And yet: the plant needs a new line. You know it does; you work in it. The machines are old, the tolerances are drifting, and if nothing is spent on that building in the next five years then in ten years the work goes somewhere with better machines and the wage you fought so hard for is a wage at a plant that has closed.

So: hold out for the maximum, or leave the margin?

{pause: 1.2}

## What you just did has a name   {tone: lucid, note: "the hard turn — name the difficulty, on-ramp, then the unannounced restatement; this beat is the spine and should feel like climbing, not like being told"}

If you found yourself hesitating — if some part of you thought *leave the margin, but only if it actually gets spent on the line* — then you have just reasoned your way, in about ninety seconds, to the model that made Przeworski's name.

And this is the hard turn of the whole episode, so stay with me through it, because everything else here is downstream of it. It is hard because it asks you to accept something that sounds on its face like an apology for exploitation: that a bargain between the owner and the worker can leave *both* of them genuinely better off than open conflict would, and that this is not a conjuring trick.

Start from how we normally picture a wage negotiation, which is as a pie-splitting problem. There is a fixed amount that the year produced. Wages and profits come out of the same amount. Every pound to one side is a pound off the other. Pure conflict, and nothing to discuss except who is stronger. That picture is the one Marx's framework encourages, and inside a single year it is basically correct.

Now change one thing. Extend the frame beyond the single year.

Because profit is not only the owner's consumption. Some part of it becomes next year's productive capacity — the new line, the better machines, the extra shift, the plant that gets built rather than not built. Which means the size of next year's pie is not fixed at all. It depends on what was done with the margin left over from this one. And that is the word for what has just happened to the problem: it has stopped being zero-sum, where my gain is your loss, and become **positive-sum** — an arrangement in which there is a way for both parties to end up ahead of where conflict would have left them.

That single change turns the whole thing from a fight over a fixed quantity into something stranger: a bargain across time, where restraint now can buy more later. Przeworski's own summary of it is two sentences, and he means every word of both literally: *by exercising wage restraint, workers induce capitalists to invest, which causes the economy to grow. Hence, workers end up ahead.*

Two terms in that, both worth pinning down as they go past. **Wage restraint** means what you just considered doing in the turbine plant — not pressing the claim to the maximum the enterprise could bear. Its opposite is straightforward maximization: take what your leverage can take. And the arrangement as a whole has a name: **class compromise** — a settlement that both classes prefer to open conflict with each other, which is precisely what makes it stable, and precisely what makes it so hard to denounce.

The plain version, before we complicate it: workers accept less than they could seize, on the understanding that what they leave behind gets ploughed back rather than pocketed, and that they get their share of what it grows into. Growth, rising wages later, and — crucially — the tax base that pays for schools and pensions and hospitals.

{pause: 1.0}

Now, it is very tempting to hear that as trickle-down: be nice to the rich, wait for crumbs. That is not the argument, and the difference is the entire point.

Trickle-down is a claim about generosity, or about spontaneous benevolence in the mechanism. Przeworski's compromise is a claim about an *enforceable exchange* — and it is strictly conditional. It only holds if the investment actually happens. If the workers exercise restraint and the owners take the margin and buy the bigger house, then the workers have not made a bargain, they have simply been robbed, and militancy becomes the rational strategy again. The model does not say restraint is always correct. It says restraint is correct *when it is reciprocated*, and it is worthless when it isn't.

Which raises the obvious problem, and the obvious problem is trust. You are giving up something certain and immediate for something uncertain and deferred, offered by a party whose interests are not yours. In the turbine plant, one round of that might work on a handshake. Across an entire economy, for decades, it cannot.

So something has to stand behind the terms. And that is where the state enters this model, doing a job it does not do in the orthodox Marxist account at all. Not the executive committee of the ruling class. Not a neutral referee either. The state is the **enforcer of the bargain** — the thing that institutionalizes it, gives it durability, and makes each side's compliance credible enough that the other side keeps showing up.

And now look again at the welfare state. In the model it is one of the things the compromise *produces* — an output, alongside growth and rising wages. But the way to see it, I think, is that once it exists it does a second job the model badly needs done. Unemployment insurance, pensions, public health care make the bet survivable. If the compromise fails for you personally — the plant closes anyway, the line was never built, you are fifty-four and unqualified for anything else — you do not fall all the way to the bottom. Which means you can afford to keep taking the bet.

Read that way, the safety net is not charity attached to the bargain. It is the collateral that makes the bargain possible.

## The trough   {tone: measured, note: "this is the complication that traps the listener in their own earlier answer; grave in the middle, and let the second-person choice sit"}

Fine, you might say. It is a bargain rather than a swindle. But it is still a bargain in which one side owns the turbine plant and the other side is rented by it. Suppose you want to end that arrangement rather than optimize your position inside it. What then?

This is where the argument turns cold, and where it becomes something much more formidable than a defence of moderation.

Ask what getting from here to there actually looks like. Not the destination — the road. Whatever else a transition to collective ownership is, it is not a switch you throw between Tuesday and Wednesday. The mechanism that decided where investment went stops deciding. Whatever is going to decide instead has to be built, staffed, learned, and it will make mistakes while it learns. Capital that can leave, leaves. Supply chains that ran on one set of expectations are running on none. Output falls. And output falling means consumption falling — for a while, and nobody can tell you in advance how long a while.

Przeworski's name for that dip is the **transition trough**. The concept is unmistakably his; I will be honest that I cannot swear the exact phrase is, and it does not matter much, because the shape of it is what does the work.

And the shape of it is this. Anyone proposing to leave the compromise is proposing that the people with the smallest margin in the entire society go through a period of getting *poorer*, for an indefinite length of time, in exchange for a payoff that is large but genuinely uncertain — and against a real possibility of outright defeat, in which the trough is all you ever get.

Put it back into your own hands one more time.

You are that worker. You believe the analysis. You think the arrangement is unjust and you would rather it did not exist. And what is on offer is: your children eat worse for ten years, possibly fifteen; the plant may not survive it; the attempt may be crushed; and if it all works, the world on the other side is better than this one in ways you will probably be too old to enjoy.

Or you can vote for the wage rise, the shorter week, and the pension.

{pause: 1.4}

For most people that is not a close call, and the point is not that they were brave or that they were cowards. The point is that there is a sum here, and it comes out the same way for almost everyone who has ever had to do it.

Now hold the two halves of the model together, because the combination is what makes it bite. Reform delivers gains that are modest, certain, and soon. Revolution demands a loss that is certain and soon in exchange for a gain that is large, late, and unsure. Which means that the *better reform works, the more irrational revolution becomes*. Every improvement in the workers' position raises what they stand to lose by gambling it.

Reform does not defeat revolution by winning the argument. It defeats it by arithmetic — and it gets more effective at defeating it precisely in proportion to how much good it does.

That is not a comfortable thing to have proved. It is not a conservative thing to have proved either. It is just, if he is right, true.

## Not traitors   {tone: measured, note: "the verdict, then the biography compressed; the September Group is named and left, per plan"}

Which brings us back to the accusation.

For a century the reformists stood charged with betrayal. They had the diagnosis, they had the movement, they had the votes, and they used all three to negotiate better terms inside the system rather than end it. Renegades. And Przeworski, who is nobody's apologist for capitalism, looked at that charge with the tools of rational choice and returned a verdict on it that is worth quoting exactly as he gave it: *social democrats were not traitors. Rather, they did as well as they could under the circumstances.*

And the wider conclusion he drew from years of the same work, again in his own words: *the central thing I learned was that reformism was a rational strategy for workers. It was in the interest of workers to support capitalist democracy.*

Hear how strange a defence that is. It is an acquittal that does not restore anyone's honour. He is not saying the social democrats were brave, or that they were secretly right all along about where history was going. He is saying they were *correct* — in the narrow, chilly sense in which a person who declines a bad bet is correct. They did the sums available to them and the sums came out that way.

And it is worth asking who is delivering that verdict, because it changes how it sounds.

Not a Cold War polemicist. A man educated at the University of Warsaw under Marxist teachers, among them Leszek Kołakowski — who would himself end up as one of the most devastating critics Marxism ever produced. A man who left Poland for the United States more or less by accident and stayed. And a man who spent a formative year in Chile at the start of the Allende government, watching a democratically elected socialist administration try, in real time and under real constraint, to do the thing his model says is so hard. That is not an abstraction he read about.

His intellectual home in this period has a name, and I am going to give it and move on: **analytical Marxism**, the circle known as the September Group, and known to itself, with some affection, as the No-Bullshit Marxism Group — G. A. Cohen, Jon Elster, John Roemer, Erik Olin Wright, and Przeworski. What they were doing was simple to describe and enormously consequential in practice. They held Marxist claims to ordinary standards of evidence and argument, with no special dialectical machinery permitted to do the heavy lifting. If you claimed a class acted a certain way, you had to be able to say what individual people had reason to do, and why. No historical necessity. No laws of motion. Reasons, incentives, and evidence.

Which is exactly why his answer looks the way it does. Once you insist that classes only do what their members have reason to do, "why was there no revolution" stops being a question about false consciousness and becomes a question about payoffs. And the payoffs, laid out honestly, favour the ballot.

## Nobody has to threaten you   {tone: tense, note: "the ceiling; this is the beat where the good news curdles — controlled, not melodramatic"}

So far this is a vindication of the reformist road. Half the model is a vindication of the reformist road.

The other half is the ceiling, and it is the reason nobody should read Przeworski as a happy ending.

Go back to the turbine plant, but move up. Your side won. There is a union, there is a national settlement, there are pensions and a health service, and now there is a government you voted for that wants to go further than the settlement — that wants to move some real decisions about the economy out of private hands.

What stops it?

Notice, first, what does *not* have to happen. There is no coup. No conspiracy. Nobody needs to bribe a minister, and no delegation of industrialists needs to arrive at the finance ministry to explain the facts of life. Nothing illegal or even impolite occurs.

What happens instead is this. One structural fact governs everything: the decision to invest is privately held. Whether the new turbine line gets built, whether the plant expands or contracts, whether anything at all is committed for five or ten years out — those decisions belong to people whose criterion is expected profit. And every single thing the government needs runs through those decisions. Growth runs through them. Employment runs through them. Tax revenue — the money that pays for the pensions and the hospital — runs through them.

So a government that adopts measures which look, to those decision-makers, like a durable threat to profitability, will find that investment slows. Money goes elsewhere or goes nowhere. Unemployment rises, revenue falls, and within a year or two the government is presiding over a deterioration in exactly the conditions of the people who elected it — at which point it does the arithmetic, and moderates.

And the sharp edge of it: *capital did not have to lift a political finger*. There is no lobbying in that story, no newspaper campaign, no threat of any kind. Thousands of separate people made ordinary decisions about their own money, and the aggregate of those decisions functioned as a veto that nobody cast.

The name for this is the **structural dependence of the state on capital**. And it is a very different kind of constraint from the one the tradition was used to arguing about. It is not that the ruling class controls the government. It is that the government's own success depends on conditions the ruling class controls without needing to intend anything.

Which yields the sober conclusion this whole episode has been walking toward, and I will give it as a paraphrase of his argument rather than as a quotation, because the punchy versions of this line that circulate are not his exact words: a social democratic party can win elections and still find itself governing according to the logic of the system it wanted to change. It can redistribute. It can regulate. It can civilize capitalism, substantially and durably. What it cannot easily do is transcend it.

Improve it, not abolish it. That was never a failure of nerve. It was the shape of the room.

## The man who deflated his own thesis   {tone: curious, note: "intellectual-honesty beat — warm toward him, and precise; do NOT let the correction sound like a footnote"}

Now — and this matters, and it is the sort of thing that usually gets flattened out when an idea becomes a slogan — Przeworski did not leave that claim standing at full strength. He went after it himself.

In 1988, with Michael Wallerstein, he published a paper called "Structural Dependence of the State on Capital," and if you know only the title you will assume it argues the hard thesis. It does something better. It builds a formal model of the thing and *tests* it — and finds that the strong version does not survive its own mathematics. A democratic state, on their model, turns out to have considerably more room to tax and to redistribute than the hard claim allows. The ceiling exists. It is not where the fiercest version of the argument put it, and it is not a lid clamped down flat.

I flag that carefully because the strong reading is the one that circulates, usually attached to his name, and it is not quite what the man concluded. The powerful intuition lives mostly in the 1985 book, *Capitalism and Social Democracy*. The 1988 paper is him interrogating it.

Which tells you something about the person that the theory alone doesn't. He constructed the most rigorous available version of his own most striking claim, discovered that it constrained the claim, and published that. It would have been very easy not to. The claim was more useful to him at full strength — it was the punchline, the thing people quoted. He formalized it into something smaller and truer instead.

That is what it looks like when someone means it about holding their own tradition to ordinary scientific standards. It is also, incidentally, why the ceiling in his work is worth taking seriously: it has survived its author's attempt to break it.

## Paper stones   {tone: contemplative, note: "the third leg; Engels's image is the pivot and should land quietly"}

There is a third piece, and it is the one that hurts the party rather than the class.

Set aside investment for a moment and count votes. Manual industrial workers — the class the whole strategy was built on — were never going to be an outright majority of any electorate. Not in Germany, not in Britain, not anywhere. Even at the high-water mark of industrial employment, a party appealing exclusively to that class was a party appealing to a large minority.

Which produces a trap with no clean way out. To win a majority, a workers' party has to reach beyond workers: clerks, farmers, teachers, the whole middle stratum. It has to become what the literature calls a **catch-all party** — a multi-class party, no longer the political arm of one class but a coalition of several. And that means softening the program that made you distinctive, because what is attractive to the people you already have is precisely what is alarming to the people you need. Every step of softening risks the loyalty of the base you started from, some of whom notice, and some of whom stop turning out.

Class purity and electoral majorities pull in opposite directions, permanently. That is the formal core of the book he wrote with John Sprague in 1986, and every social democratic party in the world has spent the last hundred years living inside it, usually without a vocabulary for what was happening to them. They did not drift rightward because their leaders grew soft. They drifted because the alternative was a permanent, principled, dignified minority.

The book has a title I have been saving, because it comes from inside this series. It is called *Paper Stones* — from a phrase of **Engels**, who observed that under universal suffrage ballots had become *paper stones*.

Feel what that image is doing. The stone is the paving stone: you lever it out of the street and you throw it, and in the nineteenth century that is what a barricade was made of. Engels's point was celebratory. The worker no longer has to tear up the road — he has something better, something that works at scale, something the state cannot baton-charge. The ballot is a stone that always hits.

Przeworski takes the phrase and lets the other half of it show. Because a paper stone has one more property. It doesn't break anything.

## Defend it, or get out of it   {tone: measured, note: "the clash staged on THIS episode's terrain — ledger vs finding; take the analytic verdict, then hand over the practical question"}

So what is the twentieth-century settlement, finally? Something to defend, or something to escape?

One half of that argument this series has already made, at length, and I am not going to make it again. Call it the ledger, and put **Eduard Bernstein**'s name on it, since he was reading it off the evidence before anyone had a model to explain why the evidence looked like that. What was delivered — to how many people, over how long, and how far it exceeded what a worker of the 1860s could have imagined asking for. Take that case as made. Nothing in Przeworski weakens it. If anything he supplies the reason it was possible at all.

What is new here is the other half, and the interesting thing about it is that it does not need a single line of **Rosa Luxemburg**'s polemic to be made. It can be built entirely out of Przeworski's own machinery, turned back on itself.

Look at what he says the settlement rests on. Not justice. Not anybody's permanent conversion to decency. It rests on a balance of position: on organized workers being strong enough to make conflict expensive, and on owners needing *those particular workers, in that particular country*, badly enough to pay for peace. That is a bargain between parties, and a bargain is worth exactly what the parties' positions are worth on the day. Nothing in the model makes those positions hold still. Let capital become more mobile. Let the industries that concentrated ten thousand people under one roof disperse into a thousand smaller sites and a supply chain running through four countries. Nobody has broken the compromise. The terms are simply renegotiated, from chairs that are no longer the same height — and the side that has to ask is the side that used to be able to stop the turbines.

And then the second move, which is worse, because it is the strategy's own success doing the damage. The compromise works by making militancy irrational and restraint profitable. Act on that for sixty years and you get a movement that is genuinely superb at what it has been practising: negotiating, drafting, administering, winning elections. You also get a movement that has quietly let the other capacity go — not betrayed it, not renounced it, simply had no reason to maintain it, decade after decade, because maintaining it cost money and members and respectability and bought nothing. So on the day the terms are worsened, the repertoire available is to ask politely, from inside institutions whose own survival depends on the confidence of the people doing the worsening. The winning strategy unbuilds the capacity to do anything but win that way.

Przeworski's model does not refute the escape reading. It sharpens it — and I think it adjudicates for it, so let me be precise about which question.

On the structural question — is this arrangement a durable achievement or a contingent equilibrium? — the escape side is the stronger reading, and it is stronger by Przeworski's own account, not against it. He does not describe a right that has been secured. He describes an equilibrium: something that holds while the payoffs hold, and that nothing in the world is committed to preserving.

What the defending side has against that is not a counter-argument. It is a fact. It is a ledger — real, enormous, paid for, and reaching more human beings than any revolutionary project ever delivered anything to. Anyone who waves that away is not being radical, they are being careless with other people's lives. But a ledger of gains does not answer a finding about the world. That the settlement gave you a great deal tells you nothing whatever about what holds it in place, or for how long.

Which leaves you with the practical question, and it is genuinely open, and it is the one I would rather you carried out of here than any verdict of mine. If what you have is a good arrangement resting on a balance of power that is already shifting: do you spend what strength you have defending it, holding the line as long as the line can be held? Or do you spend it building something that would not depend on that balance — knowing the road there runs through the trough, and knowing what the trough has done to the people who have walked into it before?

{pause: 1.2}

## The number on the table, again   {tone: contemplative, note: "bookend the turbine plant with what the listener now knows — no re-issued command — then the two-part verdict"}

Back to the plant, and the number on the table.

You know more now than you did. You know that leaving the margin is not weakness, that it can be the correct move, and that it only works if somebody makes the other side keep their end. You know that the safety net under you is the collateral that lets you go on betting. You know that the better this arrangement works for you, the less rational it becomes to ever leave it. And you know that the ceiling above it is not a conspiracy — it is a thousand ordinary decisions about money, adding up into a veto no one had to cast.

None of which tells you what to ask for this year. It tells you what you are actually standing in when you ask.

{pause: 1.0}

Where I come down is this, and I think the two halves have to be held together or neither is honest.

Reform was not a betrayal. That accusation should be retired, and Przeworski is the reason it should be retired — it fails not on sentiment but on the arithmetic, and the people who made the choice understood their position at least as well as the people who denounced them for it.

Reform was also not a triumph, and it is not finished business. It bought an enormous amount, and it bought it on terms that are conditional, renegotiable, and dependent on a balance of strength nothing guarantees. Some version of that bargain is being struck this year, over your head, by people you will never meet — and it reaches you as the ordinary weather of the economy: what is affordable, what is covered, what you are in a position to refuse.

## A system in which parties lose elections   {tone: warm, note: "HANDOVER — addendum-style: back to the series, then forward to the minimalist creed"}

This has been a companion, so it does not hand you on to a next chapter. It hands the question back to the series, and then forward, out of it.

Back: because three arguments this series has already staged look different from here. **Bakunin** insisted the revolutionary state would become a new tyranny, and lost the fight. **Bernstein** insisted there was no collapse coming and bet everything on the ballot, and was condemned for it by a party that then quietly took the road he had mapped. **Kautsky** insisted socialism without democracy was unthinkable, and was called a renegade for it. What Przeworski adds is not another opinion in that quarrel. It is the account of *why the ballot won* — and, in the same breath, the account of what the ballot could never do.

Forward: because the man who spent one career explaining the limits of democratic politics spent the next one studying democracy itself, in the countries that were building it and losing it and building it again, and he arrived somewhere unexpectedly bare. Not a definition full of ideals — deliberately minimal, almost disappointing to read for the first time.

Democracy, he wrote, is a system in which parties lose elections.

That is it. Not the general will, not the common good, not the rule of the people in any exalted sense. Just this: those who hold power can be removed by counting, and when they are counted out, they go.

## What it is measured from   {tone: somber, note: "hold this whole section low and level; the Katyn passage lands here, once, and does new work"}

It sounds thin until you set it against what it is measured from.

Because the alternative to a system where the losers pack up their offices is a system where the losers are the ones taken away. That is not a rhetorical flourish and it is not a metaphor. In the spring of 1940, several thousand Polish officers, doctors, professors, lawyers — the people who would have staffed a country's public life — were taken to a forest and shot in the back of the head, and the state that ordered it spent the next fifty years insisting somebody else had done it. That government had no mechanism, anywhere in its design, for being removed from power. There was no way to count it out. There was no way to vote it out of office.

The man who later proved, with mathematics, that the Western working classes were rational to decline the revolutionary road was born that spring, in an occupied city, to one of the men in that forest.

## The one thing not negotiable   {tone: warm, note: "close — lift back up, address the listener, and stop"}

And after a lifetime of showing how much the ballot cannot do, he still put it at the centre — not because the paper stone breaks anything, but because of what the alternative to it is.

That is the argument he leaves you holding. Not whether the settlement is good enough. It isn't. Whether you can improve on it without giving up the one thing inside it that is not negotiable.
