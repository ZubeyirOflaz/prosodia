## Alongside, not after   {tone: measured, note: "state the companion framing plainly and get out of the way; then two flat biographical facts, no portent — the flatness is the effect"}

This one sits beside the series rather than inside it. It is a companion piece: a longer look at a single question the socialist episodes kept circling and never quite closed. Nothing later depends on it, and if you have not heard the others you will still be able to follow every step of what happens here.

It also does something no other episode in this series does. It does not stop where the century stopped. It runs all the way forward, into the year you are living in.

Start with how it began.

A boy is born in Warsaw on the fifth of May, 1940. The city is under German occupation. It has been for seven months.

His father is not present at the birth, and never will be. His father was a doctor, conscripted as an army doctor, and taken prisoner — not by the Germans, by the Soviets, coming the other way. He was murdered at Katyn, in the mass killing of Polish officers and professionals that the Soviet state then denied for half a century. The son, writing about it much later, puts it with the flatness of a man who has had decades to find a gentler formula and has decided there isn't one. He allows himself one clause about the timing: at about the time I was born.

{pause: 1.0}

So here is a life that begins on the exact seam where the two great tyrannies of the twentieth century met and ground against each other. One of them holding the city he was born in. The other holding the ground his father was buried under.

His name is Adam Przeworski. He grew up to be a political scientist, and the question that organized the most important decade of his work is about as cold as a question can be, and he asked it in exactly these words:

Why was there no revolution in the West?

His answer arrived in a book published in 1985, and it is the most rigorous account anyone has given of why the workers Marx expected to revolt reached instead for a ballot paper. That is the first half of this episode.

The second half is the part that makes this worth doing now. Because 1985 turned out to be a spectacularly strange year in which to publish a theory of how democratic capitalism holds together — and we have had forty years since to find out whether the theory was right. So we are going to build the model carefully, and then take it out and break it against the record, and see what is still standing. I am not going to tell you the verdict in advance. I did not decide it in advance.

## The insult inside the explanation   {tone: curious, note: "brisk, faintly needling — received wisdom turned over and found unpleasant on the underside"}

Start with how odd his question is, and how much it assumes.

It assumes there should have been one. And by the theory almost everyone in the socialist tradition was working from, there should have been. Marx's expectation pointed straight at the *most* developed capitalist economies — the biggest factories, the densest cities, the most concentrated and organized and experienced working class. Britain. Germany. The industrial heartlands. Those were the places where the contradictions were supposed to sharpen to breaking point.

And those are precisely the places where nothing of the kind happened. The revolutions, when they came, came in Russia and China and Cuba — agrarian countries, peasant countries, places Marx's own model said were not ready. Meanwhile the German and British and Swedish and French working classes, the ones with every reason and every resource, joined unions, sent delegates to parliaments, bargained over hours and pensions and holiday pay, and by the middle of the century had settled into the arrangement we now call the welfare state and mostly stopped talking about expropriating anybody.

There has been a standard explanation for that for a century, and you have heard versions of it your whole life. The workers were bought off — cheap goods and a mortgage and a fortnight at the seaside, and the revolutionary charge drained away. Or they were deceived: the newspapers, the schools, the church, a whole apparatus manufacturing a consciousness that served somebody else. Or, hardest and most personal, they were sold — their own leaders got comfortable inside the institutions they were supposed to be dismantling and quietly changed sides. Lenin called Kautsky a renegade in a title, in print, and the word stuck to him for the rest of the century.

Those explanations are satisfying to make, and they have something in common that is easy to miss while you are enjoying them. Every one of them says the workers did not understand their own situation. Bought off, deceived, betrayed — in each case somebody who is not a worker is telling the workers what their real interests were, and informing them that they failed to act on them. It flatters the theorist and diminishes the millions of people it is about. Which does not make it false. Plenty of true things are unflattering.

Przeworski went at the same question with a different instrument. He asked: suppose they understood perfectly. Suppose nobody was fooled. Suppose every worker in Germany in 1955 had a clear-eyed grasp of exactly what capitalism was and exactly what it was doing to them. What would it then be rational for them to do?

## The number on the table   {tone: lucid, note: "the handrail is established here — keep it concrete and physical; slow slightly on the two options"}

Before any of the theory, put yourself in a room.

You are a worker in a plant that makes turbines. Big ones, the kind that go into power stations. It is a good plant, it is profitable, and you are in a union that has, for once, real strength: high membership, a credible strike threat, a management that would very much rather not find out how long you can hold out.

You are at the table. The firm's books are open enough that everyone in the room has a rough idea of what the year produced. And you have two ways to play it.

The first: take everything. Push the wage claim right up to the edge of what the enterprise can pay and still keep the doors open. You have the leverage this year. Use it. Every pound left on the table is a pound that ends up as somebody else's profit, and you are the one who stood at the lathe.

The second: ask for less than that. Real gains, a proper rise, but not the maximum — and leave a margin behind.

{pause: 1.2}

The case for taking everything is not stupid and it is not greedy. It rests on something true. The margin you leave is not yours. Once it is profit, the decision about what happens to it belongs to somebody else entirely, and nothing in the arrangement obliges them to do anything with it that helps you. They can build a new production line. They can also buy a bigger house, or a company in another country, or nothing at all. You will not be consulted. You are giving up money you could have today in exchange for a promise nobody has made you and nobody is required to keep.

That is the honest form of the militant's case, and the twentieth-century labour movement contained an enormous number of extremely intelligent people who made it — Rosa Luxemburg, whom this series has met already, put it as forcefully as anyone ever has: reforms were a means toward the goal and never a substitute for it, so that to give up the final aim was simply to give up socialism, whatever you chose to call what remained.

And yet: the plant needs a new line. You know it does; you work in it. The machines are old, the tolerances are drifting, and if nothing is spent on that building in the next five years then in ten years the work goes somewhere with better machines, and the wage you fought so hard for is a wage at a plant that has closed.

So: hold out for the maximum, or leave the margin?

{pause: 1.2}

## What you just did has a name   {tone: lucid, note: "the hard turn — name the difficulty, on-ramp, then the unannounced restatement; this beat is the spine and should feel like climbing"}

If you found yourself hesitating — if some part of you thought leave the margin, but only if it actually gets spent on the line — then you have just reasoned your way, in about ninety seconds, to the model that made Przeworski's name.

This is the hard turn of the whole episode, so stay with me through it, because everything else is downstream of it. It is hard because it asks you to accept something that sounds on its face like an apology for exploitation: that a bargain between the owner and the worker can leave *both* of them genuinely better off than open conflict would, and that this is not a conjuring trick.

Start from how we normally picture a wage negotiation, which is as a pie-splitting problem. There is a fixed amount that the year produced. Wages and profits come out of the same amount. Every pound to one side is a pound off the other. Pure conflict, nothing to discuss except who is stronger. That picture is the one Marx's framework encourages, and inside a single year it is basically correct.

Now change one thing. Extend the frame beyond the single year.

Because profit is not only the owner's consumption. Some part of it becomes next year's productive capacity — the new line, the better machines, the extra shift, the plant that gets built rather than not built. Which means the size of next year's pie is not fixed at all. It depends on what was done with the margin left over from this one. And that is the word for what has just happened to the problem: it has stopped being zero-sum, where my gain is your loss, and become positive-sum — an arrangement in which there is a way for both parties to end up ahead of where conflict would have left them.

That single change turns the whole thing from a fight over a fixed quantity into a bargain across time, where restraint now can buy more later. Przeworski's own summary is two sentences, and he means every word of both literally: by exercising wage restraint, workers induce capitalists to invest, which causes the economy to grow. Hence, workers end up ahead.

Two terms there, both worth pinning down as they go past. Wage restraint means what you just considered doing in the turbine plant — not pressing the claim to the maximum the enterprise could bear. Its opposite is straightforward maximization: take what your leverage can take. And the arrangement as a whole has a name: class compromise — a settlement that both classes prefer to open conflict with each other, which is precisely what makes it stable, and precisely what makes it so hard to denounce.

The plain version, before we complicate it: workers accept less than they could seize, on the understanding that what they leave behind gets ploughed back rather than pocketed, and that they get their share of what it grows into. Growth, rising wages later, and — crucially — the tax base that pays for schools and pensions and hospitals.

{pause: 1.0}

It is tempting to hear that as trickle-down: be nice to the rich, wait for crumbs. That is not the argument, and the difference is the entire point.

Trickle-down is a claim about generosity, about spontaneous benevolence in the mechanism. Przeworski's compromise is a claim about an enforceable exchange, and it is strictly conditional. It only holds if the investment actually happens. If the workers exercise restraint and the owners take the margin and buy the bigger house, then the workers have not made a bargain, they have simply been robbed, and militancy becomes the rational strategy again. The model does not say restraint is always correct. It says restraint is correct when it is *reciprocated*, and worthless when it isn't.

Remember that condition. In about twenty minutes it is going to matter more than anything else in this episode.

Which raises the obvious problem, and the obvious problem is trust. You are giving up something certain and immediate for something uncertain and deferred, offered by a party whose interests are not yours. In the turbine plant, one round of that might work on a handshake. Across an entire economy, for decades, it cannot.

So something has to stand behind the terms. That is where the state enters this model, doing a job it does not do in the orthodox Marxist account at all. Not the executive committee of the ruling class. Not a neutral referee either. The state is the enforcer of the bargain — the thing that institutionalizes it, gives it durability, and makes each side's compliance credible enough that the other side keeps showing up.

And now look again at the welfare state. In the model it is one of the things the compromise produces, an output alongside growth and rising wages. But once it exists it does a second job the model badly needs done. Unemployment insurance, pensions, public health care make the bet survivable. If the compromise fails for you personally — the plant closes anyway, the line was never built, you are fifty-four and unqualified for anything else — you do not fall all the way to the bottom. Which means you can afford to keep taking the bet.

Read that way, the safety net is not charity attached to the bargain. It is the collateral that makes the bargain possible.

## The trough   {tone: measured, note: "the complication that traps the listener in their own earlier answer; state the gamble, let the arithmetic land, and end on the acquittal — no biography here"}

Fine, you might say. It is a bargain rather than a swindle. But it is still a bargain in which one side owns the turbine plant and the other side is rented by it. Suppose you want to end that arrangement rather than optimize your position inside it. What then?

This is where the argument turns cold, and where it becomes something much more formidable than a defence of moderation.

Ask what getting from here to there actually looks like. Not the destination — the road. Whatever else a transition to collective ownership is, it is not a switch you throw between Tuesday and Wednesday. The mechanism that decided where investment went stops deciding, and whatever is going to decide instead has to be built and staffed and learned, and it will make mistakes while it learns. Capital that can leave, leaves. Supply chains that ran on one set of expectations are running on none. Output falls. And output falling means consumption falling — for a while, and nobody can tell you in advance how long a while.

Przeworski's name for that dip is the transition trough. The concept is his. The exact phrase may not be, and it doesn't matter — the shape is what does the work.

And the shape is a wager, with terms. On one side: the people with the smallest margin in the entire society get poorer for an indefinite number of years, in exchange for a payoff that is large but genuinely uncertain, and against a real chance of outright defeat in which the trough is all anybody ever gets. On the other side: a wage rise, a shorter week, a pension, this year, near-certain. That sum has been put to a great many people over a great many decades, and it comes out the same way for almost all of them — not because they were cowards and not because they were brave, but because it is a sum and it has an answer.

Now hold the two halves together, because the combination is what makes it bite. Reform delivers gains that are modest, certain, and soon. Revolution demands a loss that is certain and soon in exchange for a gain that is large, late, and unsure. Which means that the *better* reform works, the more irrational revolution becomes. Every improvement in the workers' position raises what they stand to lose by gambling it.

Reform does not defeat revolution by winning the argument. It defeats it by arithmetic — and it gets more effective at defeating it precisely in proportion to how much good it does.

Which is how the oldest accusation in this tradition gets answered. For a century the reformists stood charged with betrayal: they had the diagnosis, the movement and the votes, and they used all three to negotiate better terms inside the system rather than end it. Przeworski, who is nobody's apologist for capitalism, looked at that charge with the tools of rational choice and returned a verdict worth quoting exactly as he gave it: social democrats were not traitors. Rather, they did as well as they could under the circumstances. And the wider conclusion he drew from years of the same work, again in his own words: the central thing I learned was that reformism was a rational strategy for workers. It was in the interest of workers to support capitalist democracy.

{pause: 1.0}

Hear how strange a defence that is. It is an acquittal that does not restore anyone's honour. He is not saying the social democrats were brave, or secretly right all along about where history was going. He is saying they were *correct*, in the narrow, chilly sense in which a person who declines a bad bet is correct.

## No bullshit   {tone: curious, note: "short biographical beat — brisk, dry, and out again; the rule is the point, not the CV"}

And it matters who is delivering that verdict. Not a Cold War polemicist.

A man educated at the University of Warsaw under Marxist teachers, among them Leszek Kołakowski, who would himself end up as one of the most devastating critics Marxism ever produced. A man who left Poland for the United States more or less by accident and stayed. A man who spent a formative year in Chile at the start of the Allende government, watching a democratically elected socialist administration try, in real time and under real constraint, to do the thing his model says is so hard.

His intellectual home in this period was analytical Marxism — the circle known as the September Group, and known to itself with some affection as the No-Bullshit Marxism Group: G. A. Cohen, Jon Elster, John Roemer, Erik Olin Wright, and Przeworski. Their rule was that Marxist claims had to meet ordinary standards of evidence and argument, with no dialectical machinery permitted to do the heavy lifting. If you claimed a class acted a certain way, you had to say what individual people had reason to do, and why.

Which is exactly why his answer looks the way it does. Once you insist that classes only do what their members have reason to do, why was there no revolution stops being a question about false consciousness and becomes a question about payoffs. And the payoffs, laid out honestly, favour the ballot.

## Paper stones   {tone: contemplative, note: "the party's problem rather than the class's; Engels's image is the pivot and should land quietly"}

There is a second piece, and it hurts the party rather than the class.

Set aside investment for a moment and count votes. Manual industrial workers — the class the whole strategy was built on — were never going to be an outright majority of any electorate. Not in Germany, not in Britain, not anywhere. Even at the high-water mark of industrial employment, a party appealing exclusively to that class was a party appealing to a large minority.

Which produces a trap with no clean exit. To win a majority, a workers' party has to reach beyond workers: clerks, farmers, teachers, the whole middle stratum. It has to become what the literature calls a catch-all party — no longer the political arm of one class but a coalition of several. And that means softening the program that made you distinctive, because what is attractive to the people you already have is precisely what is alarming to the people you need. Every step of softening risks the base you started from.

The cost, in his account, comes twice — the withdrawal of working-class support on the way in, and the abandonment of socialist policies once in power.

The trap itself, though, is unmistakably the book's. Class purity and electoral majorities pull in opposite directions, permanently. That is the formal core of the work he wrote with John Sprague in 1986, and every social democratic party in the world has spent a century living inside it, usually without a vocabulary for what was happening to them. They did not drift toward the centre because their leaders grew soft. They drifted because the alternative was a permanent, principled, dignified minority.

That book has a title worth saving for last, because it comes from inside this series. It is called Paper Stones — from a phrase of Engels, who observed that under universal suffrage ballots had become paper stones.

Feel what that image is doing. The stone is the paving stone: you lever it out of the street and you throw it, and in the nineteenth century that is what a barricade was made of. Engels's point was celebratory. The worker no longer has to tear up the road — he has something better, something that works at scale, something the state cannot baton-charge. The ballot is a stone that always hits.

Przeworski takes the phrase and lets the other half of it show. Because a paper stone has one more property. It doesn't break anything.

## Nobody has to threaten you   {tone: tense, note: "the ceiling; controlled, not melodramatic — and plant the flag that he will come back to this"}

Which brings us to the third piece, and the reason nobody should read Przeworski as a happy ending.

Go back to the turbine plant, but move up. Your side won. There is a union, a national settlement, pensions and a health service, and now there is a government you voted for that wants to go further than the settlement — that wants to move some real decisions about the economy out of private hands.

What stops it?

Notice, first, what does not have to happen. There is no coup. No conspiracy. Nobody bribes a minister, and no delegation of industrialists arrives at the finance ministry to explain the facts of life. Nothing illegal or even impolite occurs.

What happens instead is this. One structural fact governs everything: the decision to invest is privately held. Whether the new turbine line gets built, whether the plant expands or contracts, whether anything at all is committed for five or ten years out — those decisions belong to people whose criterion is expected profit. And every single thing the government needs runs through those decisions. Growth runs through them. Employment runs through them. Tax revenue, the money that pays for the pensions and the hospital, runs through them.

So a government that adopts measures which look, to those decision-makers, like a durable threat to profitability, will find that investment slows. Money goes elsewhere or goes nowhere. Unemployment rises, revenue falls, and within a year or two the government is presiding over a deterioration in exactly the conditions of the people who elected it — at which point it does the arithmetic, and moderates.

And the sharp edge of it: capital did not have to lift a political *finger*. No lobbying in that story, no newspaper campaign, no threat of any kind. Thousands of separate people made ordinary decisions about their own money, and the aggregate of those decisions functioned as a veto that nobody cast.

The name for this is the structural dependence of the state on capital. It is a very different kind of constraint from the one the tradition was used to arguing about. It is not that the ruling class controls the government. It is that the government's own success depends on conditions the ruling class controls without needing to intend anything.

Which yields the conclusion the 1985 book is remembered for, and I will give it as a paraphrase of his argument rather than as a quotation, because the punchy versions that circulate are not his exact words: a social democratic party can win elections and still find itself governing according to the logic of the system it wanted to change. It can redistribute. It can regulate. It can civilize capitalism, substantially and durably. What it cannot easily do is transcend it.

Improve it, not abolish it. On this account that was never a failure of nerve. It was the shape of the room.

Hold that claim in mind exactly as I have stated it, because three years later somebody takes a hammer to it, and the somebody is him.

## The date on the title page   {tone: measured, note: "THE TURN — this is the hinge of the whole episode; land the irony flatly, then let it sit"}

So that is the model. Three moving parts. A positive-sum bargain that makes restraint rational. A trough that makes revolution irrational. A ceiling that makes transformation nearly impossible.

It is elegant, it is cold, it explains a century of European political history, and it is the best answer anyone has given to the question he started with.

Now look at the spine of the book.

Capitalism and Social Democracy, Cambridge University Press, 1985.

{pause: 1.2}

Think about what 1985 is. Ronald Reagan has just begun his second term. Margaret Thatcher has broken the miners' strike in March of that year. The postwar settlement in the English-speaking world is not being adjusted; it is being taken apart, deliberately, by governments elected to do exactly that. Capital controls are coming off. The industrial base of the countries the model was built from is beginning to move.

The most rigorous theoretical statement ever produced of the postwar class compromise was published at the moment that compromise was dying.

And the irony sharpens further. Przeworski himself, decades later, dated the death — and he dated it earlier than his own book. In a working paper from 2019 he writes that the compromise held, in his words, until about 1978, and that it was shattered in the United Kingdom and the United States by the respective victories of Thatcher and Reagan, whose first targets were the unions.

Until about 1978. The book came out in 1985.

So we have an unusually clean experiment, and the rest of this episode is going to run it. A model of how democratic capitalism holds together, published at the precise moment it stopped holding together that way. Four decades of evidence since. Let's find out what the model got right, what it got half-right, and what it simply did not see.

Three tests. The neoliberal turn. Globalization and finance. And the collapse of the parties the model was written about.

## The self-coup   {tone: tense, note: "Movement 2, domain (a). Let his own vocabulary do the work — this is him describing a deliberate act, not a drift"}

Test one: Reagan and Thatcher.

If you had only the 1985 model in your hand, you would expect something like this. The compromise is an equilibrium. Both sides prefer it to conflict. Equilibria are stable because departing from them makes you worse off. So what could break it?

What actually happened was not a drift, and it was not an accident, and the most striking thing about Przeworski's later account is how little he tries to make it sound like economics.

In that same 2019 paper he reaches for a different vocabulary altogether. He calls the neoliberal turn an autogolpe — a self-coup — of the bourgeoisie, and he dates the breakdown of the class compromise to roughly 1978 to 1980.

And then, in his own words, unbroken: premeditated, planned, vigorously promoted by all kinds of think tanks.

He goes on to say it was spread coercively, by American influence inside the international financial institutions, and codified as the Washington Consensus — which is the name for the standard package that the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank pressed on country after country: privatize, deregulate, open the capital account. In an interview the same year he puts it even more starkly: I think of the neoliberal shift as an autogolpe — a self-coup. It was deliberate, it was planned — it was the second bourgeois revolution.

An autogolpe is what you call it when a government seizes power from itself — suspends the constitution it already governs under, and keeps ruling. It is a word from Latin American politics, and he is a man who lived in Chile in 1970. He chose it deliberately.

Now put that word against the model. In the model, the compromise is a bargain that both sides prefer. In this description, one side looks at a bargain it is currently winning and decides to tear it up anyway — not because the arithmetic changed, but because it could.

Why could it? The model actually tells you, if you read it backwards. Remember what made the bargain a bargain and not a surrender: organized workers strong enough to make conflict expensive. Take that away and there is nothing to compromise with. Przeworski gives the number himself: average union density dropped by more than ten percentage points between the peak in 1980 and 2010. The peak in 1980. That is not a coincidence of dating; the unions were the first target, by his account and by the plain historical record — the miners in Britain, the air traffic controllers in the United States.

And there is one more move he singles out, which is quieter and which turns out to matter more than any strike. He points to what he calls the stealth opening of the capital account by the Thatcher government — the removal of the controls that kept money inside the country — and says it changed the trade-off between growth and redistribution. Nobody marched for that. Almost nobody noticed. We will come back to it, because it is the bridge to the second test.

Then the last piece, and it is the one that stings, because it is about his own side. Asked why the parties of labour did not stop any of this, he answers in seven words: the Social Democrats began speaking like neoliberals.

So how does the model score on test one?

Give it this much: the vocabulary of the model narrates the break cleanly. Wage restraint, the enforcement of the bargain, private control of investment — you can tell the whole story of 1979 to 1985 in those terms without adding anything. Break the enforcement mechanism and the bargain becomes a surrender. That is the model working.

But a model whose entire purpose was to explain a stable positive-sum equilibrium did not, in its 1985 form, contain the reason that equilibrium could be *deliberately* demolished from above by the side that was supposedly benefiting from it. Game theory is very good at telling you why people stay where they are. It is much weaker at telling you the day one of the players decides to overturn the table, because the balance of force has shifted and it is tired of sharing.

Przeworski supplied that reason. He supplied it thirty-four years later.

## Where the margin went   {tone: lucid, note: "domain (b), the load-bearing evidence beat — quiet, factual, and devastating; reuse the turbine handrail once"}

Test two, and this is the one the model cannot afford to lose. Because everything rests on a single conditional, and I asked you to remember it: restraint is correct when it is reciprocated, and worthless when it isn't.

So. Was it reciprocated?

There is a clean way to measure that. If workers hold back on wages and the margin goes into investment, and the investment makes the economy more productive, then productivity and wages should rise roughly together. The workers get their share of what the restraint grew. That is the bargain, expressed as two lines on a chart.

Before the late 1970s, in the United States, those two lines move together. Output per hour goes up; typical workers' compensation goes up with it, close enough that you can hardly separate them. That is the class compromise, visible.

Then they come apart.

The best cross-country measurement is an OECD working paper from 2017, by Schwellnus, Kappeler and Pionnier. Across twenty-four OECD countries between 1995 and 2014: productivity rose to about 127, on an index where 1995 equals 100. Real median compensation rose to about 118. And the share of national income going to labour at all fell in roughly two-thirds of OECD countries.

Now — I have to be honest with you about the state of this evidence, because it is genuinely fought over. The direction is robust: nobody serious disputes that a gap opened. The size of it depends on choices that sound technical and aren't — whether you measure productivity gross or net of depreciation, which price index you use to deflate wages. Some of the very large numbers that circulate on this do not survive careful checking. And the economists Stansbury and Summers have argued that the causal link is weaker than the chart suggests — that productivity growth still raises pay, and something else is driving the divergence. Take the direction as established, and treat any precise multiple you are quoted with suspicion.

But one thing sets this evidence apart from everything else in the episode.

The most important witness against the class-compromise model is Adam Przeworski.

In that 2019 working paper, this is what he writes. Until about 1978, increases in wages almost exactly followed increases in productivity, so that the functional distribution of income was stable. That last phrase is just the split between wages and profits — it held steady. Then he sets down the figures for what came after, and these are figures for the United States, 1973 to 2014: productivity up 72.2 percent, hourly compensation up 9.2 percent. And he observes that the average labor share took a precipitous dip from about 1980.

{pause: 1.2}

Read that again, slowly, and notice what it is.

The man who proved that wage restraint was rational because it bought workers a share of future growth is, in his own late work, the one documenting that after 1978 it stopped buying them that share. He is not being attacked with this evidence. He went and got it.

Take it back to the turbine plant, and let it be your children rather than you.

They left the margin. Year after year, the responsible thing, the thing the model says is correct — hold the claim below the maximum, keep the plant competitive, let the profit be ploughed back. And on the evidence, from somewhere around 1979 onward, a widening part of what the restraint produced did not come back to them as wages. The bargain kept being kept on one side.

The model does not fail here in the way you might expect. It does not fail by being wrong about how the bargain works. It fails by having presented as a settled institution what turned out to be a temporary balance of forces — and by containing no account of what happens to a workforce that goes on behaving as if a bargain exists after the other party has quietly stopped paying.

Which is, if you want the cruellest formulation, a model that taught a movement to be rational at roughly the moment rationality stopped paying.

## Who the government has to please now   {tone: curious, note: "the contested beat — present the debate honestly, attribute both sides, do not resolve it"}

But *why* did it break like that? What actually changed?

Here I have to slow down, because this is where the honest answer is "we are not sure," and the confident answers on offer are ideological on both sides.

The intuitive story goes: capital got mobile. Once the controls came off — the stealth opening Przeworski flagged — money could leave. And a threat that can be carried out cheaply is worth far more at a bargaining table than one that can't. The turbine plant that could not be moved in 1965 can be moved in 1995, or its next production line can be built somewhere else, which comes to the same thing. Nobody has broken the compromise. The terms are simply renegotiated from chairs that are no longer the same height, and the side that has to ask is the side that used to be able to stop the turbines.

That story is coherent, it is consistent with the model's own logic, and I think it is probably a large part of the truth. But watch what happens when you try to nail down the harder version of it — the claim that firms stopped investing and started hoarding.

There is a real, measured phenomenon here: after 2008, non-financial corporations across the OECD became net lenders. They were saving more and investing less, putting money out rather than taking it in — which for a corporation is close to a category error, and which a large literature reads as proof that finance has captured the firm. But Gruber and Kamin, in a 2016 paper in the IMF Economic Review, looked at the same data and cautioned that it probably does not reflect a shift in corporate behavior relative to past norms — investment, they argue, fell about as much as you would expect given what was happening to GDP, to interest rates and to profits. That dispute is not settled. I am telling you both readings because the financialization story is the one you will hear told as fact, and it isn't one yet.

Where the ground is firmer is a change in who the state has to keep happy — and for this it is worth bringing in someone who is not a friend of this model.

The German sociologist Wolfgang Streeck argues that the modern state has undergone a transformation Przeworski's mechanism did not anticipate. Przeworski's constraint was indirect: the government depends on private investment, so it must not frighten investors. Streeck's point is that the state now also depends on private credit. It runs deficits; it must sell bonds; and the people who buy them can decline. He gives that constituency a name — the Marktvolk, the market people, set against the Staatsvolk, the people of the state, which is to say the electorate. One of those constituencies votes. The other one can walk. And his conclusion is that in a crisis, restoring investor confidence takes precedence over regaining public trust. He describes the whole shift as a move from a tax state to a debt state.

Now, some care. Streeck is not a disciple correcting the master; he does not cite Przeworski, and the connection I have just drawn between them is my reading, not a documented lineage. And his larger thesis — that capitalism is ending, that capitalism and democracy are going to part ways and may end in something like a social dictatorship — is separately and vigorously disputed; the historian Adam Tooze is among those who think it is overdrawn. Take Streeck as a foil that sharpens the question, not as a verdict.

But the question he sharpens is real, and it is the model's own question, moved forward forty years. Przeworski asked what a democratic government can do when the people who decide about investment can decline to invest. The version now is: what can a democratic government do when the people who finance the government itself can decline to finance it?

The direction of the constraint is the same. The channel is more direct, and quicker.

## The dilemma was real. The answer was wrong.   {tone: measured, note: "domain (c) part one — vindication and inversion in the same breath; keep the multi-causality honest, do not let one villain emerge"}

Test three. The parties.

Of the model's three pieces, the catch-all dilemma is the one that reads today like prophecy. A workers' party cannot win with workers alone. To win it must broaden. Broadening dilutes. Dilution costs it the base.

Look at what happened. The Third Way, in the 1990s: Labour under Blair, the SPD under Schröder, the American Democrats under Clinton. Broaden, moderate, reassure the markets, keep the working-class vote because where else is it going to go. And then, from around 2010, one of the most comprehensive collapses any party family has suffered in a democratic century. The SPD's worst result since the Weimar Republic in 2017. The French Socialists, who had held the presidency five years earlier, effectively annihilated. The Dutch Labour Party reduced from a party of government to a fragment.

So: the dilemma was real, exactly as described. But now look closely at what the model implied about how to resolve it, because this is where something inverts.

The model's logic says broadening is the rational move. It is how you get to a majority. The cost is real but the alternative is losing forever.

Does the record bear that out? There is a study by Polacko, published in West European Politics in 2021, covering twenty-two democracies and three hundred and thirty-six elections between 1965 and 2019. Its finding: social democratic parties' shifts to the right on economic policy — and this is the paper's own language — significantly reduce their vote share under higher levels of income inequality. Not "fail to help." Reduce. There is related work, by Karreth, Polk and Allen, finding that moving to the middle produced gains that were real but short-lived, at the expense of electoral success in the decade that followed.

If that holds, the model has an odd shape now: it correctly identified the trap, and the escape route it pointed to turns out, under conditions of high inequality, to lead further in.

And I have to flag that "if." The causal direction here is disputed — the political scientist Keman argues the arrow runs the other way — and the older view that parties win by moving toward the centre is not a discredited relic. Conditional on the electoral system, conditional on the level of inequality, the answer changes.

But now the larger and more important caution, and I'll be firm about it, because there is a story here that is very satisfying to tell and the evidence does not support it.

The satisfying story is: social democracy died because Blair and Schröder and Clinton sold it out. Self-inflicted, one villain, one wound.

That story does not survive contact with the literature. Bandau published a systematic review in Government and Opposition in 2022, covering fifty-one studies of exactly this question, and the finding is almost aggressively unhelpful to anyone who wants a culprit: there is, in his words, not one explanation that stands out. What there is instead is four families of explanation that interact. Sociological — the industrial working class shrank; the electorate the parties were built on physically stopped existing at that scale. Materialist — globalization and fiscal constraint narrowed what any government could actually deliver. Ideational — the parties adopted their opponents' economic ideas and had nothing distinctive left to say. Institutional — unions weakened, parties professionalized and drifted toward the state and away from their own members.

Those crystallize into two rival hypotheses in the field, and neither one has won. One is postindustrial dilemmas: this was structural, and close to unavoidable — the ground moved under a party family built for a world of large factories. The other is neoliberal contamination: this was self-inflicted, a rightward drift that hollowed the parties out from inside.

Notice where the model sits in that argument, because it sits on both sides. Neoliberal contamination is essentially the catch-all dilemma turned self-destructive — that is Przeworski's mechanism, running exactly as he described it and ending worse than he expected. But postindustrial dilemmas points at something the 1985 model largely does not contain: not the tension between class purity and electoral breadth, but the fragmentation of the class itself, into service workers and graduates and the precariously self-employed, with genuinely different material interests and no shared institution to reconcile them.

The model gave a superb account of a two-player game. What arrived was not a two-player game.

## The other party at the plant gate   {tone: tense, note: "the blind spot — the sharpest failure; keep the causal question genuinely open"}

And now the part the model did not see coming at all.

Go back to the first principle of the whole framework. Politics runs on the class cleavage. There is a workers' side and an owners' side, and the interesting question is what the workers' side can achieve.

So when the parties of the left lost the workers, where did the workers go?

Not to the radical left, mostly. To the populist right.

Which, on the 1985 framework, is close to incomprehensible. That is the other side's party. Workers voting for it are voting against their material interest in the most direct way imaginable, and this is a model whose entire foundation is that workers understand and act on their material interests.

Berman and Snegovaya, writing in the Journal of Democracy in 2019, describe what actually happened, and it is neither what the model would predict nor simply false consciousness. Those parties moved. The French National Front spent the 1980s as a fairly conventional free-market operation under Jean-Marie Le Pen — and then, under his daughter Marine, shifted toward protectionism, an interventionist state, and a strong social safety net. Parallel moves in Austria, in Denmark, in Germany, in Sweden. On the economics, the populist right went left.

With a condition attached, and the condition is everything: those benefits are for us. Scholars call it welfare chauvinism — a generous state, for a bounded membership. Economically left, culturally exclusive.

The honest difficulty is this. It is very tempting to say the populist right captured the working-class vote by moving economically left, because that reading rescues the model — it would mean workers were following their material interests after all, and simply found a different vehicle. Careful people in this field do not concede that. A substantial body of work holds that the cultural and immigration appeals were at least as decisive as the economic shift, and possibly more so. Both readings are live. The evidence does not pick one, and neither will I.

But whichever reading is right, the model has a problem, and it is the same problem either way.

The 1985 framework has one axis. More or less redistribution; more or less of the product to labour. Every position it can represent is a point on that line. What has organized Western politics over the last decade or so is a *second* axis, cutting across the first — national, cultural, about belonging — and along that second axis the old coalitions do not hold together at all. A party can be left on the first line and right on the second, and millions of people will vote for it, and the model has no coordinates in which to write that down.

That is not a wrong prediction. It is a missing dimension. And it is the most serious failure in the whole stress test — because the model does not merely fail to predict the outcome, it lacks the vocabulary to describe it.

## The author takes his own model apart   {tone: curious, note: "Movement 3 pivot. Build it in layers — plant on-ramp, plain definitions, finding before quotation, then the checkpoint. Warm toward him, precise about the correction"}

So we have run three tests, and before I total them up there is one more thing on the record, and it is the strangest item in this entire story.

The single most damaging attack on Przeworski's most famous claim was published in 1988. By Przeworski.

Remember the ceiling — structural dependence, the veto nobody has to cast. That is the idea that made his reputation outside the academy. It is the intellectual backbone of a certain kind of left pessimism you can hear any day of the week: capital always wins, the market has a veto, elections cannot touch it.

In 1988, with Michael Wallerstein, he published a paper in the American Political Science Review called "Structural Dependence of the State on Capital." From the title you would expect a defence. What it does is build a formal model of the thesis and test it.

Before the finding, get clear on what the strong version of the thesis is actually claiming — because it is an intuition most of us carry around without ever examining it.

Stand in the turbine plant one last time, and this time stand on the other side of the table. You own it. There is a new production line you could build. You will build it if what you expect to get back from that line beats what the same money could do elsewhere — another industry, another country, a government bond, a house. Now let a government tax the return on that line hard enough. The comparison flips. The line does not get built. Multiply that decision by every owner in the country and there is your ceiling: push redistribution past a certain point and investment stops, and no election can vote its way around it. That is the strong claim, and it has the feel of physics.

Przeworski and Wallerstein put it into equations, and found that it isn't physics.

Their answer turns on a distinction between a static question and a dynamic one, and the words are less forbidding than they look. The static question compares two settled states of the world — one with a small welfare state, one with a much larger one — and asks whether investment goes on happening in both. The dynamic question is about the road between them: what happens while a society is travelling from the first to the second.

Statically, they found, the ceiling is not there. And the reason is a distinction the intuition slides straight past. What governs the owner's decision is the expected return on the *next* pound he commits — on the line, not on the plant he already has. It is not the total share of the existing product that wage earners get to consume. Those are two different quantities, and a system of taxes and transfers can be designed to move one of them a very long way without doing much to the other. Which is to say: you can shift a great deal toward workers, and the new line still gets built. Here is how they put it, and I will give it verbatim, because it is their own thesis they are talking about:

In a static sense the theory is false: virtually any distribution of consumption between wage earners and owners of capital is compatible with continual private investment once an appropriate set of taxes and transfers is in place.

False. Their word, about their own claim. And listen to the last clause, because that is where the whole finding lives — once an appropriate set of taxes and transfers is in place. The ceiling is not a law of nature. It is a question of how the tax code is written. Which makes it a question of policy design, and therefore of who has the political power to do the designing.

{pause: 1.0}

Now a checkpoint, because this takes back less than it might sound like.

Everything in the mechanism I described earlier stands, untouched. The investment decision really is privately held. Everything a government needs really does run through it. Nobody has to threaten anybody, and no minister has to be bribed. What falls is only the strongest claim built on top of that mechanism: the claim that it fixes a permanent maximum on how far a democracy can redistribute.

And what survives is not small. The constraint bites dynamically — in the window in which policies are anticipated but not yet implemented. The announcement. The campaign. The transition. That is where capital's reaction has real force, and it can be brutal there.

So: not a lid. A toll gate on the road to any change.

There has since been an independent test of the strong version, and it lands in the same place. The strong thesis predicts a particular cycle, and it is worth having in your head before the numbers: wages rise, the profit share gets squeezed, investment falls, unemployment rises, and wages fall back again — the market undoing labour's gains without anyone deciding to. Fiorio, Mohun and Veneziani, in Political Studies in 2021, went looking for that profit-squeeze cycle in British data running from 1892 to 2018. They find it in eighty-seven of a hundred and twenty-four years — which is a real pattern, and which also means roughly thirty percent of the record does not conform to it at all. Income shares, they conclude, are much too variable to support a strong structural-dependence reading. And the result I would underline: higher union density correlates with a long-run rise in the wage share. Their conclusion — power resources matter, and therefore institutions and politics matter; the social democratic model is more undetermined than the strong thesis suggests. It is one country over one long run — suggestive, not settled cross-national fact.

And yet the softer version is not dead, and he never abandoned it. Asked about it in 2019, Przeworski still says: any government has to anticipate the effect of its decisions on the reaction of firms, as indicated by the stock market. Anticipate. Not obey.

Which tells you something about the man that the theory alone doesn't. He constructed the most rigorous available version of his own most striking claim, discovered that it cut the claim down, and published that. It would have been very easy not to. The claim was more useful to him at full strength — it was the punchline, the thing people quoted. He formalized it into something smaller and truer instead.

And it means the slogan is his least defensible legacy. Capital does not hold a permanent veto. The author of the veto proved it doesn't. What it holds is a toll gate on the road to change, and a seat at every table — quite bad enough without exaggeration, but leaving more room than the pessimists claim, and the room is exactly as large as organized political power can make it.

## The ledger   {tone: measured, note: "the verdict — this is where the referee may become an advocate about the MODEL; keep the mixed picture honest"}

Total it up, then. Forty years on, where does the 1985 model stand?

Take the failures first, because they are real and they do not belong at the bottom of the page.

The class compromise, as a bargain, broke. Restraint went on being exercised and the return on it thinned. Przeworski documented that himself and dated its beginning to before his own book was printed. Any reading of him that treats the postwar settlement as the natural resting state of democratic capitalism is refuted by the record and by its author.

The model had no account of why a winning side would demolish an equilibrium it was profiting from. It described a stable bargain; what happened was, in his later word, a coup.

And the model has one axis where politics turned out to have two. It could not represent the realignment that swallowed the very parties it was written about.

Now the vindications, which are also real.

The catch-all dilemma was not merely correct, it was correct in a way that took thirty years to become obvious. A party built on a class that is shrinking must broaden or die, and broadening costs it the thing that made it. Every social democratic party in Europe has now lived out that sentence.

The insight that the state's freedom of action runs through decisions it does not make — that stands. Softened by his own hand, extended by his critics into the world of sovereign debt, but standing. Every finance minister in the democratic world is doing the anticipation he described, this week.

And the deepest one, which I think survives everything: that a political settlement is not a moral achievement but an equilibrium. It holds while the payoffs hold. It is not a right that has been secured; it is a bargain worth what the parties' positions are worth on the day. That is the least comfortable thing in the model and it is the thing the last forty years have proved most thoroughly.

So the verdict I would give — and this is a judgment, so weigh it against the evidence I have laid out rather than taking it from me — is that the model is a superb anatomy and a poor forecast. It explained the structure of the trap with a precision nobody had reached before. It mispredicted the resolution, because it took the balance of forces that produced the postwar settlement and treated it as furniture.

Which is, when you think about it, the failure mode of every good model of a stable system. It tells you why things stay as they are. It cannot tell you the day they stop.

There is one more entry on that ledger, and it is not really about the argument. In an intellectual world where people defend their famous claim to the grave, the sharpest witness against this model at every point where it fails — the 1978 dating, the productivity gap, the false static thesis — is the man who built it. That is not nothing. That is what the No-Bullshit rule looks like when it costs you something.

## What he did next   {tone: contemplative, note: "his own arc — quieter here; the Katyn callback is ONE line, do not re-do the opening"}

He did not stop thinking in 1985, and where he went next is the last piece.

He turned from the question of socialism to the question of democracy itself — the transitions out of dictatorship in Latin America and Eastern Europe, including his own country. And out of that work came the line he is most quoted for, from Democracy and the Market in 1991, which I will give you the way it is universally repeated, and flag that I am relying on how it is repeated rather than on my own reading of the page:

Democracy is a system in which parties lose elections.

That is it. Not the general will. Not the common good. Not the rule of the people in any exalted sense. Just this: those who hold power can be removed by counting, and when they are counted out, they go.

It sounds thin until you set it against what it is measured from. The alternative to a system where the losers pack up their offices is a system where the losers are the ones taken away — and the reason that is not a rhetorical flourish, in his case, is a forest in the spring of 1940. The state that ordered what happened there had no mechanism, anywhere in its design, for being removed from power.

Later, with collaborators, he took that minimal definition to the data and found something else that cut against the arguments of his day: that democracies perform about as well economically as authoritarian regimes do — that the dictator's usual excuse, that he alone can deliver growth, does not hold up. And a striking regularity, that no democracy above a certain level of income had ever collapsed. I flag both of those as reaching me through summaries rather than from the primary text, so hold them a little loosely.

But it is the late Przeworski, still writing in his late seventies, who closes this loop — because that is where the two halves of his life come back together.

In an interview in 2019 he said this, and hear it against everything the first half of this episode established: I don't think that elections are an instrument of change. I think they are impotent when it comes to reducing inequality.

{pause: 1.2}

That is the man who proved the ballot was the rational choice, saying the ballot cannot do the thing the choice was made for.

And in Crises of Democracy, published the same year, he sets out what makes democracies break. Not tanks; tanks are the old way. What he names are: high levels of economic and social inequality. Intense political polarization, in which the stakes of losing seem especially high. And the emergence of large-scale street violence and social unrest. The mechanism he stresses is not a coup at all but erosion — formally legal and constitutional measures that imperceptibly change the nature of the regime over time. Subversion by stealth. Nothing illegal. Nothing that looks, on any given Tuesday, like the end of anything.

Look at the first item on that list.

Inequality. The thing his 1985 compromise was supposed to hold in check. The entire point of the positive-sum bargain was that workers got their share of the growth their restraint had bought, and that the share was large enough and secure enough to make the arrangement worth defending. When that stopped being true — on his own evidence, from about 1978 — the model does not say what happens next. But the man does. What happens next is a threat to democracy itself.

The circle closes badly. The bargain that made the ballot rational is the same bargain whose failure now, on his account, endangers the system the ballot belongs to.

## The one thing he would not give up   {tone: warm, note: "close — refuse the easy declinism, hand the question over, and stop"}

You would forgive him, at that point, for declaring the whole thing finished. A great many people his age did.

He refused. I do not believe there is a worldwide crisis of democracy, he said — flatly, in an interview, at a moment when saying the opposite would have been easier and considerably better received.

And he refused the other comfort too, the one where the trouble is somebody else's fault. He treats populism not as an invasion from outside the system but as something inherent in democracy itself — a demand for a stronger and more direct voice, which is a thing democracies are supposed to be for. On the American case he is blunt about where to look. Donald Trump's support, he says, is not really about Trump. It reflects something deeper, something that lurks in the everyday life of society. Democracies stopped delivering material progress to a great many of the people inside them. Everything since is the bill.

So think about what he ends up holding.

He proved that reform was rational, and lived to document that the arrangement which made it rational stopped paying somewhere around 1978. He proved that capital constrains the state, and then proved the strong version of that false with his own mathematics. He proved that the ballot beat the barricade, and said in his late seventies that the ballot is impotent against inequality. Almost every load-bearing claim of his most famous book, he came back and put a crack in.

And he still would not give up the paper stone.

Not because it breaks anything. It doesn't. He is the man who told us it doesn't. But because of what the alternative to it is — which he has known since before he could know anything at all.

That is what he leaves you holding, and I am not going to resolve it, because he didn't, and I have no better standing than he does.

You are back at the table with the number on it. You know more than you did. You know that leaving the margin can be the correct move, and that it only works when somebody makes the other side keep their end. You know that the safety net under you is the collateral that lets you go on betting. You know that the better the arrangement works, the less rational it becomes to ever leave it — and you now also know, from four decades of evidence, that the reciprocation the whole thing depends on can quietly stop, without any announcement, and that the people still exercising restraint may be the last to be told.

So the question is not the one the twentieth century argued about. It is not ballot or barricade. That argument was settled, and it was settled by arithmetic, and he showed the working.

The question you are left with is what the ballot is for, if the thing it was most needed for is the thing he says it cannot do. Whether the answer is to rebuild the power that made the bargain a bargain, or to want something the bargain was never going to give you — that is genuinely open, and it is being decided in your lifetime, largely by people who have never heard of any of this.

He would tell you to do the sums. He would also tell you that the sums depend on the strength of your position, and that your position is not a fact of nature.

It is a thing that people built. Which means it is a thing that people can build again.
