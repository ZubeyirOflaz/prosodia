## A piece of good news   {tone: curious, note: "cold open on evidence with the wrong shape; dry, faintly puzzled, not portentous — the catastrophe must arrive as a surprise, so do not telegraph it"}

Here is a piece of good news.

Somewhere in the eighteen nineties, in London, a German exile in his forties goes looking at the figures. Wages. Incomes. The registers of who owns what. He is not a professor and he has no chair to protect. He is a journalist, largely self-taught, with a journalist's instinct that if you want to know what is happening in the world you go and look at what is happening in the world.

And what he finds is this.

Working people are getting better off. Not comfortable — better off. Real wages are rising. The layer of society between the very rich and the very poor, the clerks and shopkeepers and foremen and technicians, is not being crushed out of existence; it is getting thicker. And ownership, which is the sharpest of the three, is not funnelling into fewer and fewer hands. It is spreading out. The joint-stock company has arrived, and a share in an enterprise can now be held by hundreds, by thousands of people who have never once set foot in it.

Consider how that ought to feel.

If you have any decent instincts at all, that is good news. It is not enough. It is nowhere near enough — there are still children in those mills and there are still families living in one damp room. But it is the direction you would want the world to be moving in, and the man reading those figures has spent every year of his adult life trying to move it that way.

It ruined him.

Not financially, and not all at once. But the good news he had just found was, for the movement he belonged to, something very close to the end of the world. Because that movement's entire theory of how the world would be saved required the news to be bad. It required wages to fall. It required the middle to be ground away into nothing. It required ownership to concentrate into fewer and fewer hands until the whole apparatus stood so top-heavy and so brittle and so obviously intolerable that it came down under its own weight. In that theory the misery is not simply a scandal to be denounced. The misery is the *engine*. The misery is the thing that delivers the future.

And here are the figures, saying: the engine has stalled.

So stand where he is standing, because this is not a nineteenth-century problem and you have almost certainly been in a smaller version of it. You believe something. You have organised your life around it — your friendships, your work, what you are willing to be unpopular for. And evidence comes into your hands that the central factual claim underneath that belief is wrong. It is not enemy propaganda. You gathered it yourself. You have checked it. And you cannot make it go away.

What do you do with it? {pause: 1.4}

There are three honest answers and you know all of them. You can decide you have misread it — that the trend is temporary, local, a wobble on a longer curve, and the theory is still sound at the scale that matters. You can keep it to yourself: believe it privately, say nothing, let the movement carry on with its comforting arithmetic, because a cause under attack does not need help from inside. Or you can publish, and hand your enemies a loaded weapon with your own name engraved on the stock.

The exile in London had one more reason for silence than you do, and it was a heavy one. He was not a critic sniping from outside. He was the inheritance. He had grown up intellectually under Engels's eye; he was one of the men Engels named to look after his papers when he died — a literary executor, most sources say jointly with August Bebel, and either way about as complete a vote of confidence as that movement had to give. He was the keeper of the flame. He was, in the most literal sense available, the trustee of the doctrine.

I told you his name at the end of last episode, so there is no reveal to spring here. Eduard Bernstein. He waited until Engels was dead, and then he published.

## The bill   {tone: lucid, note: "the live question set as a concrete VOTE; establish the handrail here and keep it for the whole episode; the second complication is the hinge — let it land"}

Before the theory, the choice — because this argument is not really about German professors, and it is very easy to make it sound as though it is.

Here is the choice in its simplest form.

You are a socialist, elected to a parliament. Not a philosopher: an elected member, sent there by people who work in mills and mines and expect something from you. There is a bill in front of the chamber. It would cut the legal working day. It would put inspectors into factories with the power to shut a dangerous one down. It is a modest bill; it will not abolish anything; it leaves every owner in place and every title of property exactly where it was. But it will pass only with your votes, and if it passes, several hundred thousand people will go home earlier and fewer of them will be killed at work.

Do you vote for it?

Obviously you vote for it. That took you no time at all, and I would think less of you if it had.

Now change one thing.

Suppose the theory you hold — the one you have organised your life around, the one you would go to prison for — says that this system ends when it becomes unbearable. That the collapse comes out of the crisis, and the crisis comes out of the misery. On that theory, every hour you shave off the working day is an hour of pressure released from a boiler you were counting on to burst. You are not merely making life better. You are making the intolerable tolerable. You may be buying four hundred thousand people a shorter Tuesday at the price of postponing their grandchildren's liberation by a generation.

Now do you vote for it? {pause: 1.4}

That is not a trick and it is not a caricature. That, in various disguises, is the argument that split the European left down the middle and kept it split for a century, and every person in this episode is answering it. If the diagnosis is right — if capitalism really is the machine Marx described, and I spent two episodes on that machine and am not going to rebuild it today — then what follows? Do you wait for the revolution, and keep the movement pure and ready for it? Or do you work for reform, take what you can get, pass the bill, win the seat, run the town, and see how far that road goes?

And underneath that, a second question, which is the one still biting at the end of the hour. Suppose you take the reform road, and it works. You win elections. You form governments. You get inside the machinery of the state and start pulling levers.

Does the system change, or do you?

## The heir   {tone: measured, note: "biography compressed hard — one run, no dawdling; the Fabian debt hedged as reinforcing not originating; the Erfurt detail planted here to pay off later"}

Bernstein was born in Berlin in 1850, into a family with no money and no library. He did not go to university. He worked as a bank clerk, and he read, and he wrote, and he came into the socialist movement as a young man at the moment it was becoming the thing nobody had planned for: a mass party.

Then Bismarck made him an exile. The Anti-Socialist Laws of 1878 outlawed the party's organisations and its press while leaving its candidates, absurdly, still able to stand for election — so the German socialists spent twelve years as a banned movement that kept winning votes. Bernstein went to Zurich and edited *Der Sozialdemokrat*, the party paper, which was printed in Switzerland and smuggled into Germany by the sackload. That is his apprenticeship: contraband journalism, for an illegal party, in somebody else's country.

He met Engels in December 1880, and the relationship took. For the next fifteen years he is close to the centre — one of the two men, with Karl Kautsky, most trusted to say what the doctrine was. When the German party wrote its foundational statement at Erfurt in 1891, the work was divided between them: Kautsky wrote the theoretical half, the Marxist analysis of where history was going, and Bernstein wrote the practical half, the list of demands. Remember that division of labour. It is going to look very pointed in about ten minutes.

In 1888 he was expelled from Switzerland and went to London, where he stayed for roughly thirteen years, until 1901. And London is where it happens, though not for the reason usually given. The usual story is that he fell in with the Fabians — the English gradualists we will get to — and caught reformism off them like a cold. There is something in it: he knew them, he liked some of them, the atmosphere was empirical and unhurried and unrevolutionary and it clearly worked on him. But he later denied flatly that they were his source, and he was rude about them in print — he thought their pragmatism had no vision in it whatsoever. So take the Fabians as reinforcing what he was already finding, not as the origin of it. What actually changed his mind was Britain itself: a country with an old, entrenched capitalism, a large organised labour movement, a working class that was visibly not collapsing into destitution, and figures in the public record that anyone could go and check.

Engels died in August 1895. Bernstein's revision began appearing the following year — a series of articles under the title "Problems of Socialism," running through 1896 to 1898, and running, of all places, in *Die Neue Zeit*: the movement's flagship theoretical journal, edited by Kautsky. He published his heresy in his best friend's magazine. Kautsky printed it.

In 1899 it became a book: *The Preconditions of Socialism and the Tasks of Social Democracy*. In English it was known for most of a century by the looser title *Evolutionary Socialism*, which is a decent summary and a bad translation.

And here is why it detonated. Plenty of people in that movement had gone soft in practice — voted for bills, sat on committees, quietly stopped believing in the barricade. Bernstein did something none of them had done. He said it out loud, in theory, with evidence, as a claim about the world. He did not say *let us be a little more moderate*. He said: **the analysis is wrong.**

## The crash that never came   {tone: lucid, note: "the two empirical fronts — collapse theory, then immiseration; commit the listener to the shareholding argument, THEN complicate it; steelman Kautsky's tendency-not-prophecy reply before moving on"}

Four things, and the first two are the ones you can argue with a table of numbers.

The first is the collapse. In German the word is *Zusammenbruchstheorie* — collapse theory, breakdown theory — and it is the expectation that capitalism is heading not for bad years but for one great terminal crisis. Crises get worse. Each one shakes the structure harder than the last. Eventually one of them brings the whole thing down and the future begins.

Bernstein said: look at what has actually been built since Marx wrote.

Credit. An elastic, sophisticated financial system that can absorb a shock and spread it out over time instead of transmitting it instantly to everybody. Cartels and trusts — combinations of firms that damp down the competition that used to drive them all into the wall together. Telegraphs and railways and steamships, so that a glut here can be sold there, and a merchant in Hamburg knows on Tuesday what happened in Chicago on Monday. World markets, which are enormous and absorbent.

None of that abolishes crisis. Bernstein never said capitalism had stopped hurting people. His claim is narrower and harder to dodge: capitalism has grown *shock absorbers*. It adapts. It bends where the theory said it would snap. And a system that bends is not going to hand you a catastrophe to seize power in — which is a problem, because the entire strategy of waiting was built on the assumption that the catastrophe was coming and the only question was whether you would be organised enough to use it.

The second front is the one about people, and it has a name too: *Verelendung* — immiseration. The prediction that under capitalism the mass of workers get progressively poorer, and that wealth concentrates in fewer and fewer hands as the small are eaten by the large.

Against that, Bernstein put the good news from the top of this episode. Real wages rising. The middle strata multiplying rather than vanishing. And the argument he thought was decisive: the joint-stock company.

Follow that one, because it is genuinely clever. The theory says ownership concentrates — a hundred small workshops become one great factory owned by one man, and repeat until a handful of magnates own everything. But look at how a great factory is actually owned now. It is a company. It has shares. The shares are held by a widening body of people. Not only magnates: managers, professionals, small investors, insurance funds holding other people's savings. The *enterprises* are getting bigger, yes. But the *ownership* of them is being sliced thinner and spread wider. The number of propertied people is going up, not down.

That is a real observation and it has never entirely gone away. But press on it, because it is also the weakest thing he says, and you can feel exactly where it gives.

Ten thousand people each owning one share, and one man owning a block big enough to appoint the board — is that ownership dispersed, or is that ownership concentrated with a very large crowd of spectators attached? Owning a share is not the same as controlling an enterprise, and Bernstein's argument slides between the two. Counting shareholders tells you how many people receive a dividend. It does not tell you who decides whether the factory opens on Monday. And in 1899 the person who decided that was still very much a person, and not a crowd.

So Bernstein has a real result and an overreach in the same paragraph, which is what tends to happen when you are the first person to notice something.

Now — his own side's best answer, because he got one, and it is better than a shrug.

Kautsky's reply was: you have mistaken a *tendency* for a *prophecy*. Marx was not running a weather forecast with a date on it. He was describing the direction a system is pushed by its own logic — the pressure it exerts, other things being equal. Other things are never equal. Trade unions push back; laws push back; good decades happen. That a tendency is being resisted is not evidence that it does not exist. Show me a decade of rising wages and I will show you the organised workers who fought for them and the state that was frightened into conceding, and neither of those refutes anything.

That is a serious answer. It is also — and Bernstein saw this immediately — an answer with a very convenient property. A tendency that can be indefinitely offset by everything that actually happens is a claim about the world that the world cannot contradict. At what point would you admit it was wrong? If the collapse has not come in fifty years, is that fifty years of evidence, or fifty years of delay? {pause: 1.0}

Nobody in that argument ever answered that question, and I do not think it has been answered since.

## The ghost in the machinery   {tone: contemplative, rate: slow, note: "the hardest turn — name the difficulty, on-ramp from the Hegel and Marx episodes, one plain checkpoint after the hard step, pre-empt the obvious misreading; this beat is unhurried"}

The third front is the abstract one, and it is the one people skip. Stay with it for four minutes, because this is where Bernstein stops being a man with a table of wage statistics and becomes a philosopher — and it is the move that makes everything after him possible.

His target is the dialectic.

Start from where we have been. In the Hegel episode I spent an hour on it, and the core of it is that history moves by contradiction: a thing develops, and in developing generates its own opposite, and the collision of the two produces something new that contains them both. Marx took that engine, said it had been running upside down in Hegel's hands, and set it on material ground — on the way people produce, and on who owns the means of doing it. The contradiction becomes a real, physical, social one, between the way things are made and the way they are owned. And it sharpens. And at the point of maximum development, it breaks.

Notice what that structure does to your expectations before you have looked at a single fact.

It tells you that the better the system works, the closer it is to its end. It tells you that concentration and crisis and misery are not merely bad things but *ripening* things. It tells you that the breakdown is not an accident that might or might not happen, but the internal logic of the thing arriving at its conclusion. Bernstein's charge is that this is not a discovery. It is a *habit of mind* — a piece of German metaphysics that came into the theory from Hegel and stayed there, wearing a laboratory coat, quietly guaranteeing in advance the very catastrophe the evidence would not deliver. There is a chapter of his book on exactly this, and its title translates as "the snares of the Hegelian dialectical method." Snares. Traps you walk into.

Restate that plainly, because it is the hard step and it deserves a checkpoint: Bernstein is saying that the reason Marxists kept expecting a collapse that never arrived is not that they had bad data. It is that they had inherited a philosophy which told them, before they looked, what the data was going to say.

Now pre-empt the obvious misreading, because it is very tempting. This is not "Bernstein disliked abstract philosophy and preferred common sense." He is not an anti-intellectual, and he is not saying Hegel was stupid. His claim is specific and it is about *method*: that a system of thought designed to reveal the necessary movement of history will always find necessary movement, because that is the only shape it can see. Cut it out, he says, and go and count things.

Which sounds like housekeeping, until you notice what you have just cut.

The dialectic was not decoration. The dialectic was the *guarantee*. It was the reason a man could sit in a cold room in 1885, organising a union that would be smashed within a year, and still know with something like religious certainty that he was on the winning side of history. It was the thing that made socialism a *science* rather than a hope — the assurance that the future was not something you had to argue people into, it was something coming whether they liked it or not, and your only job was to be ready when it arrived.

Take the guarantee away and there is a hole where the whole floor used to be.

And now Bernstein owes you an answer to a question that had not needed asking for fifty years. If socialism is not going to happen by itself — if history does not owe it to you — then why should anybody want it? What, exactly, is the argument?

## Kant against cant   {tone: warm, note: "the neo-Kantian strand, short and load-bearing; flag the pun as invisible aloud and the translation dependence; the Rawls line flagged clearly as parallel, NOT descent"}

His answer was to go back a hundred years and fetch Kant.

The fourth front of the revision, and the deepest: if socialism is not a prediction, it has to be a *justification*. Not an *is* that is coming. An *ought* that we choose. And an ought needs grounds — moral grounds, the kind you argue for, the kind somebody can reject and you then have to persuade them out of.

He was not doing this alone. There was a whole revival running through German philosophy in those decades, a movement back toward Kant that had grown up in reaction against the great speculative systems — the slogan everybody quotes, "back to Kant," is a later crystallisation of it rather than anyone's actual battle cry. And one wing of that revival was doing something very specific: building socialism directly out of Kant's ethics rather than out of history at all. The Marburg School — Hermann Cohen above all, with Paul Natorp beside him — plus scholars like Karl Vorländer, who wrote a book called *Kant and Marx*, and Rudolf Stammler, and Franz Staudinger. Even a working politician came out of it: Kurt Eisner studied under Cohen at Marburg long before he led the Bavarian revolution of 1918.

Their move is clean, and if you remember Episode 13 you already have it. Kant's categorical imperative, in its humanity formulation: act so that you treat humanity, in yourself and in every other person, always at the same time as an end, and never merely as a means. And the picture that goes with it — the kingdom of ends, a community of rational beings each of whom counts as an end in themselves, and none of whom is merely useful to the others.

Cohen's argument is that this is not just a rule for private conduct. It is a specification for institutions. If persons must never be treated merely as means, then an economic arrangement in which one person's labour is simply an input into another person's return is not a regrettable feature of an otherwise decent order — it is a standing violation of the deepest thing morality says. The kingdom of ends is not a private state of grace. It has to be *built*, in how work and ownership and provision are actually organised. Which, for Cohen, meant cooperative socialism: socialism as the practical realisation of Kant's ethics.

That is the missing floor, and it is why Bernstein reached for it. Where the orthodoxy said *this is coming*, the neo-Kantians said *this is right* — and being right requires no help at all from history.

He put it, in the conclusion of his book, as a pun, and I have to tell you the pun is there because you cannot possibly hear it. He summoned the spirit of the great philosopher of Königsberg against the *cant* of the movement — cant meaning pious, hollow, self-congratulating talk, the noise an organisation makes when it has stopped checking whether its own words are true. Kant against cant. In print it is a very good joke. Read aloud it is one word twice, which is either a disaster or extremely fitting for a line about empty noise. And the exact English wording shifts between translations, so take the sense rather than the syllables.

One further thing about that strand, and I am flagging it as my own reading rather than a documented line of descent, because the difference matters. If you know where this series ends, you have just heard the shape of it. A theory of justice that grounds itself in persons as free and equal ends, worked out by argument rather than derived from history — that is what John Rawls will be doing in 1971, and the family resemblance to the ethical socialists is real and worth naming. But there is no established chain of influence from Cohen and Natorp and Vorländer to Rawls. It is a parallel, not an inheritance. Two sets of people reached for the same philosopher because they had the same hole to fill.

## The sentence they cut in half   {tone: wry, note: "the famous line: give it in full, flag the clip, then the walk-back; light and faintly amused — this is a beat about how movements read"}

Which brings us to the eight words everybody knows about Eduard Bernstein, most of which he did not write.

The line, as it travels: *the final goal is nothing, the movement is everything.*

The actual sentence, from an article in 1897, before the book, is longer and far more careful. He wrote: I openly confess that I have extraordinarily little sense or interest in what is *generally understood* as the "final goal of socialism." This goal, whatever it may be, is nothing to me; the movement is everything.

Hear the difference. The clipped version says: I do not care where we are going. The full version says: I do not care about the thing you people mean when you say "final goal" — the capital-letter destination, the day of transformation, the pictures in the pamphlets — because I do not think anybody knows what it will look like, and I think pretending we do is a way of avoiding the work. Those are not the same statement. One is an abdication. The other is a complaint about a particular kind of religiosity, made by a man who was in fact organising, arguing and standing for election in pursuit of a goal he could describe perfectly well.

It has been clipped for a hundred and twenty-five years, mostly by people quoting it to prove he had given up.

And he knew it had gone wrong almost at once. In an October 1899 piece he half took it back: he conceded he had thrown the phrase out defiantly, against what he considered an absurd accusation, and admitted that a movement without a goal drifts about without a compass. Which is a sentence I have some affection for, because it is a man discovering that a good line is a liability. He was right that the destination cannot be drawn in advance — I made that argument in the other direction last episode, on behalf of the people who *did* draw it. He was also right, on reflection, that a movement which stops being able to say what it is for eventually stops being a movement and becomes an administration.

Keep that admission somewhere handy. In sixty years it comes back with a party congress attached.

## The heresy trial   {tone: tense, note: "the Revisionismusstreit; Luxemburg steelmanned at FULL strength — she is the best argument against the whole episode; the Hanover vote put to the listener; Dresden tally honestly unavailable"}

The movement's reaction was not embarrassment. It was fury, and the fury was proportionate, because he had not attacked the party's tactics. He had attacked its reason for existing.

Kautsky answered with a book — *Bernstein and the Social Democratic Program*, 1899 — defending the orthodoxy line by line. August Bebel, the party's great organiser and its moral centre, led the leadership against him.

And the fiercest reply came from a woman of twenty-eight who had been in the German party for about a year, and it is the best thing written on that side of the argument by anybody: Rosa Luxemburg's *Social Reform or Revolution?*, 1899. She is not in this series as an episode — she belongs to the revolutionary pole, with Lenin, and this hour is about the people who refused it — but her case has to be made properly here, because if it is right, everything that follows in this episode is a slow-motion defeat.

So take it at full strength.

Luxemburg does not oppose reforms. That is the caricature and it is false; she spent her life in the daily work of a mass party. Her claim is about what reforms are *for*. Fighting for the shorter day, the safer factory, the higher wage — that is the school. It is how a class learns what it is, how it discovers its own power, how it builds the organisation and the confidence without which nothing else is possible. Reforms are the *means*. What they are not, and can never become, is the *destination* — because they operate inside the wage relation and cannot abolish it. You can improve the terms on which one class works for another indefinitely, and never once touch the fact that it does.

And then the knife. If you drop the final goal — if you say the movement is everything and the destination nothing — you have not become more practical. You have changed what the organisation *is*. A party aimed at transformation that fights for reforms is a revolutionary party using reforms. A party that has quietly stopped believing in transformation, and fights for reforms, is a very good pressure group inside the existing order. It will still have the songs. It will still have the red flag on the platform. But it will be, in its actual operation, one of the load-bearing institutions of the system it was founded to end — and the more successful it becomes, the truer that gets.

That is a serious argument, and I do not think Bernstein ever fully met it. Keep it in view; it comes back at the end of the hour with sixty years of evidence attached, and the evidence cuts both ways.

Then the party voted.

Hanover, October 1899. You are a delegate. You are not a theorist. You have been arrested, or your brother has; you sell the paper, you run the local, you have done the door-knocking in a country where until nine years ago this whole enterprise was illegal. A resolution comes forward from Bebel affirming that the party sees no reason whatever to change its principles or its tactics. Against it stands a book by a man everybody in the hall respects, arguing that the principles are factually wrong.

How do you vote? {pause: 1.2}

They voted for the resolution. Reported two hundred and sixteen to twenty-one. Revisionism condemned.

At Dresden, in September 1903, they did it again — the congress condemned, in about as decided a language as a resolution permits, the revisionist attempt to abandon the tactics of class struggle. I would like to give you that tally as well, and I cannot; I have not been able to obtain a reliable count for Dresden, and I would rather leave the hole visible than fill it with a plausible number.

So that is the condemnation. Held twice, overwhelmingly, on the record.

Now watch what the same party actually did for the next sixty years.

## The Pope of Marxism   {tone: measured, note: "Kautsky's portrait, brisk; the epithet flagged as an anonymous label; establish him as the centrist so the 1918 break lands as principle, not drift"}

The most interesting man in this quarrel is the one who ends up hated by both sides, and he has been standing in the room the whole time.

Karl Kautsky. Born in Prague in 1854. Founded *Die Neue Zeit* in 1883 and edited it until 1917, which made him, for a generation, the man who decided what Marxism *was* — the journal was where doctrine got settled, and he held the pen. Principal author of the theoretical half of the Erfurt Program. Author of *The Class Struggle*, 1892, the popular commentary that taught orthodox Marxism to a mass movement that mostly had not read Marx and was never going to.

He was widely called the Pope of Marxism. That epithet gets attributed to various people; I could not trace it to any of them with confidence, so treat it as what it most likely was — something everybody said, that nobody in particular said first.

And his position is worth understanding, because it is not a fudge, though both wings later insisted that it was.

Against Bernstein, Kautsky defended the goal. He kept the analysis, kept the class struggle, kept the conquest of state power as the aim, and argued — in *The Road to Power*, 1909 — that capitalism had entered an era of revolution, and that the working class had to be aiming at power itself, not at a permanent programme of improvements. No frictionless growing-over into socialism. The confrontation is coming.

Against the people who would shortly be called Bolsheviks, Kautsky defended the method. His revolution was to be made by winning: by democratic majorities, mass organisation, unions, elections, a working class that becomes the overwhelming bulk of society and then takes power because it *is* the bulk of society. A revolution, in his phrase, that cannot be *made* — not conjured up by a determined minority at a moment of its own choosing, not staged as a coup.

Revolutionary in doctrine, democratic in method. That is the position. And in normal times it looks like the sensible centre, which is precisely why it survived so long unexamined.

Then came 1917, and the position stopped being theoretical.

## Two fundamentally distinct methods   {tone: grave, note: "the 1918 break; build the condition/form distinction in layers from a felt ambiguity; Lenin steelmanned properly including the survival argument; then verbatim Lenin and a plain verdict — this is where I stop being a referee"}

Start with a phrase, because the whole fight is inside it.

*The dictatorship of the proletariat.* Marx's phrase, and the movement had been repeating it for decades the way you repeat any inherited formula, which is to say without ever pressing on it.

So press on it. Two situations. Both can be described in exactly those words.

In the first: the workers are the great majority of the population. They vote. They win. They hold the parliament, they hold the government, and they use that power to reorganise ownership over the objections of a wealthy minority who lose, and go on complaining freely in newspapers they still own. The propertied class no longer rules. That is a class dictatorship in the sense of a *social fact* — one class is on top — arrived at and exercised through ordinary democratic institutions.

In the second: a party seizes the state in the name of the workers, dissolves the assembly, bans the opposition press, and rules by decree and by force, on the grounds that the workers' true interest is known to it and cannot wait for the workers themselves to catch up.

Both have been called the dictatorship of the proletariat. Which one did Marx mean? {pause: 1.2}

Kautsky's answer, in his 1918 pamphlet *The Dictatorship of the Proletariat*, is the load-bearing distinction of this entire episode, and it is one sentence: dictatorship as a *condition* must not be confused with dictatorship as a *form of government*, which alone is the subject of dispute in our ranks.

Set that in plain words. A condition is a description of who, in fact, holds social power. A form of government is a description of the machinery — who may speak, who may vote, who may be arrested and by what procedure. Marx, on Kautsky's reading, was describing a condition: the situation in which the working class, being the majority, rules. He was not prescribing a machine. And Kautsky's evidence is the case Marx himself pointed to as the thing coming into view — the Paris Commune, which was constituted on the basis of universal suffrage. Not the suspension of the vote. The vote.

From which everything follows. If what you are after is the rule of the majority class, then democracy is not a bourgeois decoration you discard once you are inside the building. Democracy is the *instrument* — the only mechanism by which a majority can be shown to be a majority, and, less comfortably, the only mechanism by which it can be shown to have stopped being one. His banner sentence is four words long, and it is one of the very few things any of these men wrote that has never needed revising: *socialism without democracy is unthinkable.*

He framed the split, correctly, as the clashing of two fundamentally distinct methods — that of democracy and that of dictatorship — and followed it a year later with *Terrorism and Communism*, calling what the Bolsheviks had done a coup.

Now Lenin, at full strength, because this is the point where it would be very easy, and very cheap, to give him a villain's voice.

Lenin answered within weeks, in a pamphlet whose title fixed a word to Kautsky for the rest of the century: *The Proletarian Revolution and the Renegade Kautsky*. And his argument is not stupid. It is this.

Your "pure democracy" is a fiction. In a society where one class owns the newspapers, the printing presses, the meeting halls, the land the meeting halls stand on, the courts, and the officer corps, a formally equal vote is equal in nothing but form. It is absurd, he says, to talk about pure democracy in a class society; the democracy you are defending is bourgeois democracy, which is to say the political shell of the rule of the owners — and you are asking a revolution to conduct itself according to rules written by the people it is overthrowing. And add the practical half, which is the argument I put in an aristocrat's mouth last episode and which is genuinely powerful: there is a civil war on, there are foreign armies on Russian soil, and a revolution that observes due process against enemies who observe nothing will simply be killed, along with everyone who trusted it.

That is the strongest form of it, and if the argument stopped there, this would be a hard call.

It does not stop there, because Lenin then wrote the sentence that settles it. Dictatorship, he says, is rule based directly upon force and unrestricted by any laws.

Read that again as a thing a person chose to put into print, in defence of his own government, as a definition and not as an accusation. Not "in the emergency we have suspended the following protections." Not "until the war ends." A definition: power resting on force and restrained by no law whatever. Kautsky had said that unlimited power is the death of socialism. Lenin agreed about the description and disagreed about the value. He treated it as the precondition.

Here I stop being a referee, because I do not think this one is close.

Kautsky was right. He was right in 1918, with no evidence available to him except an argument, at a moment when being right cost him his standing in the international movement he had spent forty years building. Both wings disowned him. The revisionists had never forgiven him for defending the orthodoxy; the Bolsheviks made "renegade" his permanent surname; the Communist tradition wrote him out. He died in Amsterdam in 1938, by which point the thing he had predicted was killing people at a rate nobody in that argument could have imagined.

And notice what he had arrived at. A state seized in the name of a class, administered by a minority who become a permanent apparatus, restrained by no law, and impossible to dislodge by the people it claims to embody. That is, almost point for point, what a Russian anarchist had published in 1873 — and I laid it out last episode. Bakunin got there from outside Marxism by reasoning about power, and Kautsky got there from deep inside it by reasoning about democracy. There is no debt between them that this argument needs. Two men walked at the same wall from opposite directions and found the same wall.

## The delayers   {tone: curious, note: "Fabians — method given room, institutional inventory NAMED and left; the Fabius epigraph hedged; permeation-runs-both-ways is the carrier of the episode's second question"}

Cross the Channel now, because the same argument was running there, and the British were conducting it with a total absence of metaphysics that is either refreshing or maddening, depending on your temperament.

The Fabian Society was founded in London in January 1884. It was not a workers' party and never pretended to be. It was a small circle of middle-class intellectuals — writers, civil servants, researchers — and they took their name from a Roman general, which tells you the entire strategy before you have read a word of them.

Quintus Fabius Maximus, called Cunctator: the Delayer. Hannibal was in Italy, undefeated. Rome had lost army after army marching straight at him. Fabius refused to give battle. He shadowed, harassed, cut supply, wore him down, and declined every opportunity to lose gloriously. It was deeply unpopular. It worked. The Society's first tract carried a motto to that effect: wait for the right moment, as Fabius did, most patiently — but when the moment comes, strike hard. Worth flagging that the epigraph is semi-legendary, and that the historical Fabius never actually delivered a decisive strike; the waiting was the whole method. Which is either a bad omen for the people who chose the name, or an unusually accurate one.

The figures: Sidney and Beatrice Webb, an extraordinary partnership of social investigators who produced between them a shelf of research on trade unionism and industrial organisation that people still cite. George Bernard Shaw, playwright, propagandist, and editor of the movement's manifesto, *Fabian Essays in Socialism*, 1889. Graham Wallas. Annie Besant. Edward Pease keeping the minutes.

Their central tactic they called **permeation**, and it is the opposite of everything in this series so far. Do not build a mass party. Do not raise a barricade. Do not wait for a crisis. Instead, go to the people who already hold power — Liberal ministers first, later Labour ones, but also civil servants, journalists, town clerks, academics, the people who actually draft things — and *convert them*. Feed them research. Write their policy for them. Sit on the committee. Socialism arrives not by seizure but by adoption, one department at a time, and ideally without anyone quite noticing it has happened.

Underneath the tactic, a method: go and count. No dialectic, no collapse theory, no class war, no philosophy of history. Facts, surveys, statistics, expert design. Their model was municipal — what got nicknamed gas-and-water socialism. You do not need to nationalise the commanding heights of the economy in order to socialise the water supply. The town council does it. It builds the trams and the housing and lights the streets, and nobody calls it a revolution, because everybody just wants clean water and a tram that runs. Reform as engineering.

The label everyone remembers is "the inevitability of gradualness," and Sidney Webb did not say it. What he said, chairing the Labour Party conference in June 1923, was "the inevitable gradualness of our scheme of change" — and his reason mattered more than the phrase. His argument was arithmetic. In a country with something like ten or fifteen million electors, any change whatsoever — including one made by a revolutionary — has to be made in a way those millions can be brought to consent to. That is not a preference for moderation. It is a statement about what a ballot box mechanically *is*. Once the working class has the vote, gradualism stops being a temperament and becomes the shape of the only door available.

What they built I will name and leave, because the inventory is not the point. The London School of Economics, planned by four Fabians and opened in 1895, funded by a legacy whose size the sources disagree about. The Labour Representation Committee in 1900, which became the Labour Party — and Clause Four of its 1918 constitution, the commitment to common ownership, drafted by Sidney Webb, though some sources credit both Webbs. The *New Statesman*, 1913. And Beatrice Webb's Minority Report to the Poor Law Commission in 1909, which called for breaking up the Poor Law altogether and securing what she called a national minimum of civilised life — a document with a young researcher's fingerprints on it. His name was William Beveridge, and he will be back before this episode ends.

Now, the question I planted at the start, because the Fabians are where it bites hardest.

Permeation assumes influence runs one way. You go into the ministry, and the ministry becomes a bit socialist. But you have just placed your most capable people inside institutions with their own culture, their own timescales, their own idea of what is realistic, and their own extremely effective machinery for making ambitious people feel unserious. What if it runs the other way? What if the department converts *you* — not by corruption, nothing so crude, but by the slow, reasonable pressure of what is administratively possible this year? You would never feel that happening. You would experience it as *maturity*.

Nobody in that society ever answered it, and I am not sure anybody since has either. Anyone listening who has joined an organisation in order to change it can check the answer against their own experience, which is the only place the answer actually lives.

## The question mark they removed   {tone: somber, note: "the Webbs on the USSR — land it as a real failure of their OWN method; do not use it to void the tradition; keep it short and cold"}

And then there is the thing that has to be said about them, and I am not going to bury it in a subordinate clause.

In 1935 Sidney and Beatrice Webb — by then elderly, immensely distinguished, the most respected social investigators in Britain — published a two-volume study of the Soviet Union. It was called *Soviet Communism: A New Civilisation?* The question mark is doing work in that title, and it is the last honest thing about the book.

They found a great deal to admire. They were shown a great deal, by people who were extremely good at showing things. And in 1937 the second edition appeared with the question mark deleted. *Soviet Communism: A New Civilisation.* Full stop.

By 1937 the famine in Ukraine had killed millions. The camps were operating. The purges were underway. The show trials were in the newspapers of the country the Webbs lived in.

There is no version of this that is a footnote. These were the people whose entire claim on your attention was that they went and *counted*. Their method was the answer to metaphysics: never mind your philosophy of history, here is the survey, here are the returns, here is what is actually the case. And on the largest factual question of their lifetime, presented with a regime that described itself as the future they had worked for, they stopped counting. One later critic called the book Soviet propaganda at its most mendacious, and it is difficult to argue.

Take the honest lesson rather than the cheap one. The cheap one is that this discredits Fabianism, and it does not — the LSE still stands, and so does the health service. The honest one is that their failure was the exact failure their method was designed to prevent, which is what makes it damning. Empiricism protects you from wishful thinking only for as long as you remain willing to be told something you do not want to hear. The Webbs wanted a working example. They had spent fifty years arguing that socialism was administrable, and here was an entire country claiming to administer it. So they looked at the administrators, and saw administration, and did not look at the graves.

Wanting evidence is not the same thing as being willing to have it.

## Five giants   {tone: measured, note: "the vindication — Beveridge as a LIBERAL flagged plainly, Godesberg with the translation flagged; build momentum, this beat pays off the whole reform road"}

Now the payoff — because for all of that, the reformists won. Not the argument. The world.

December 1942. Britain is three years into a war it is not obviously winning. A civil servant produces a report titled *Social Insurance and Allied Services*, and it does something no government report is supposed to do: it sells. People queue for it. It declares war on what it calls the five giants standing across the road to reconstruction — Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, and Idleness — and proposes a system of universal social insurance to fight them. It became the blueprint of the British welfare state and the foundation of the postwar settlement, and it is the reason a great many people listening to this were born in a hospital that did not send their parents a bill.

Two things about William Beveridge that the story usually skips.

He was not a socialist. He was a Liberal — Director of the London School of Economics for nearly twenty years, later a Liberal member of Parliament. The welfare state is not a socialist trophy; it is a convergence, the place where Fabian collectivism met the new liberalism and both discovered they wanted mostly the same institutions for different reasons. Anyone who tells you the welfare state is socialism, or that it is liberalism, is telling you half.

And the second thing is a line Beveridge himself gave, which this whole episode has been walking toward: that the report stemmed from what all of us had imbibed from the Webbs. The researcher on the Minority Report in 1909 wrote the blueprint in 1942. Thirty-three years of permeation, arriving.

Then Germany, and this one is almost too neat.

Bad Godesberg, November 1959. The German Social Democratic Party — the party of Bebel and Kautsky, the party that condemned revisionism at Hanover in 1899 and again at Dresden in 1903 — adopts a new programme. It drops Marxism. It drops the class-struggle framework. It accepts the market economy, in a formula still quoted in the English translation: as much competition as possible, as much planning as necessary. And it stops being a class party at all: from being a party of the working class, the programme says, the Social Democratic Party has become a party of the people.

Set that against the vote at Hanover.

Two hundred and sixteen to twenty-one, they condemned him. And sixty years later, in a spa town on the Rhine, in language a good deal blander than his, they adopted his position in full and called it a new beginning. Bernstein died in 1932 and never saw it. The party spent six decades becoming the thing it had excommunicated, and then held a congress to announce that this is what it had become.

That is Bernstein's posthumous victory. He lost the argument on paper. He won it in practice, everywhere, in every advanced democracy on the continent — and the shape of the state you live in is substantially his.

## Triumph, or surrender   {tone: contemplative, note: "the central tension at full strength on both sides; do not manufacture parity; the narrow verdict is split, the deep question stays open and is honestly handed forward"}

So here is the question this episode has been carrying, and the reason it is not a victory lap.

Was that socialism's triumph, or its quiet surrender? {pause: 1.4}

Take the surrender case first, because it is the one nobody makes at the prize-giving, and Luxemburg wrote it in 1899, before there was a welfare state to test it on.

You set out to end a relationship — the one where most people spend most of their waking lives executing decisions made by somebody else, in an enterprise they do not own, for a return set by a market they do not control. You did not end it. You improved its terms, considerably, and the improvement was real, and then the improvement became the whole of what you were for. The party that was going to abolish the wage relation became the party that negotiates it. And notice how the horizon behaved. It receded at exactly the speed you approached it, and at Godesberg you formally agreed to stop walking toward it and to call the walking itself the destination — which is precisely what Bernstein said in 1897 and then admitted was a mistake, because a movement without a goal drifts about without a compass.

And there is a sharper version. What if the concessions were never really yours? What if the eight-hour day and the pension and the insurance scheme were granted, in the end, because they were affordable, and because the alternative on offer in the East frightened people who had a great deal to lose — and were therefore always retractable on the same terms? A gain you did not take but were given can be taken back, and anyone who has watched a social settlement being renegotiated over the last few decades has some evidence to bring to that. That is my reading rather than a documented finding, and weigh it as such. But it is the surrender case at its strongest, and it is not weak.

Now the other side, and it is stronger than the romantics have ever allowed.

Count what the reform road actually delivered to the people it was for. Not to a theory — to people. The shorter working day. The insured wage. The pension that meant old age was no longer a synonym for destitution. Medicine you receive because you are ill rather than because you can pay. Schooling past the age of twelve for children whose grandparents were in a mill at nine. The workhouse abolished. And it delivered all of it without a civil war, without a secret police, and — this is the part that is easiest to overlook, because you have never once had to notice it — while leaving in place the machinery by which the people who did it could be voted out. And they were, repeatedly, and they handed power over, and they came back.

Set that against the other road, where the other road actually won. I spent the end of last episode on what the seizure of the state produced. The reformists' worst crime is that they governed capitalism competently. The revolutionaries' worst crime, where they won, is a different order of thing entirely, and manufacturing a tidy symmetry between the two would be a lie.

So, where I come down — and I will keep it narrow, because a hundred and twenty years have not closed this and I am not going to pretend an hour has.

On the facts, Bernstein was substantially right and his opponents substantially wrong: the collapse did not come, the immiseration did not come, and a movement waiting for either was going to wait a very long time. On ownership he was half right and too cheerful by half — spreading the shares out is not spreading the control out, and the twentieth century made that plainer than he could have known. On his deepest move, cutting away the guarantee and grounding socialism in an ought, I think he was simply correct, and correct in a way that has long outlived the argument he was having: if your politics is only as strong as its prediction, then it dies the day the prediction fails, and it deserves to.

On Kautsky there is no equivocation available, and I have already given it. Socialism without democracy is unthinkable; he said it when saying it was expensive; the century has not produced a single counter-example.

On the Fabians — they built more of the world you live in than anyone else in the last four episodes, Marx included, and their catastrophic failure was the exact failure their method existed to prevent, which ought to keep everyone honest who imagines that being empirical is a personality trait rather than a discipline you have to keep choosing.

And on triumph or surrender, I am going to leave it open, deliberately, because the honest answer needs a tool nobody in this episode had. Every person in this hour argued about reform as a matter of *will* — courage or cowardice, faith or betrayal. Whether reform was the *rational* choice for people in that position, and what that choice permanently costs, was answered much later, with game theory, by a man born in occupied Warsaw whose father was killed at Katyn. He has a companion episode of his own, and it is the answer to the question I am declining to close here.

## The bill, again   {tone: contemplative, note: "ENDING — bookend on the vote and on the good news from the open; recast as beginning; direct address, then stop"}

Go back to the chamber, and the bill.

Shorter working day. Inspectors in the factories. It passes only with your votes. And you now know things the delegate in 1899 did not: that the collapse the theory promised was not coming; that the party which voted to condemn the man who said so would spend sixty years becoming him; and that the road you take by voting yes leads to a report about five giants and a hospital that does not bill you — and also to a party congress in a spa town where the destination is quietly retired.

Vote. {pause: 1.4}

I think you vote for the bill. I think almost everybody does, once it is a real chamber and not a diagram, and I do not think that is weakness. But be clear about what you have chosen, because Luxemburg was right about this even where she was wrong about the rest: voting yes is not a neutral act of decency. It is a bet — that a thousand improvements accumulate into something, rather than merely making an unjust arrangement comfortable enough that nobody can be bothered to end it. Nobody has settled that bet. You are living inside the experiment right now, and so is everyone you know.

And go back to the good news, because that is where we started.

Wages rose. That was the fact that broke a prophecy. It was also — and this is worth saying plainly, because the people in this episode were so busy being appalled by its theoretical implications that they occasionally forgot it — an enormous, real, concrete good, arriving in the lives of people who had been told for a century that nothing whatever could be done for them. A man found evidence that the world was getting better, and it wrecked his standing in the only community he had. He published it anyway. He was mostly right. They condemned him, and then they did what he said.

Which is roughly the best you can hope for, if you are the sort of person who tells a movement something it does not want to hear.

The argument is yours now. It runs every time a party that meant to transform something settles for administering it well. It runs every time somebody explains to you that the responsible thing is the achievable thing. And it runs in the oldest form of all, the one this whole hour has been circling: whether you can get to a different world one bill at a time, or whether the bills are how the world you already have keeps you busy.

## The men who did not care who owned the mill   {tone: curious, note: "HANDOVER — the pivot across the Channel and out of the ownership question entirely; Bentham named last, on the auto-icon"}

One last thing, and it is the reason this arc ends here.

Look at every road this series has walked down for the last four episodes. Marx's. Engels's. The utopians building their model villages. The anarchists federating from below. Bernstein's ballot, Kautsky's majority, the Fabians permeating the ministry. They fought each other viciously, they expelled each other, they wrote pamphlets designed to end each other's careers — and not one of them ever questioned what the argument was *about*. Every single one takes it as given that the deep question of politics is who owns the means of production. Get the ownership right, by whatever route, and justice follows.

Cross the Channel now, and go back a century, because a tradition had been running there that would have heard this entire quarrel with polite bafflement.

Not because it took a side about the mill. Because it did not think ownership was the question at all. It proposed to judge a law, a punishment, a constitution, an entire government by one quantity and one only: add up the pleasure, subtract the suffering, and count every person exactly once — the beggar and the duke on the same line of the same ledger, which in the seventeen-eighties was a far more subversive proposition than anything in this episode.

And one man wanted that *calculated*. Not gestured at — worked out, on paper, by a procedure, the way you would size a load-bearing beam. He went at the prisons and the poor laws and the courts and the whole criminal code with that ledger. And he went at his own corpse with it too: he left his body to be publicly dissected and then preserved and put on display, on the grounds that a body which merely rots in the ground is a waste of a perfectly good resource.

His name was Jeremy Bentham. And he is next time.
