## The commission   {tone: curious, note: "brisk, direct, slightly conspiratorial; a job being handed over"}

The whole thing is yours to design.

Not a policy. Not a reform. The frame — the deep rules that everything else sits on top of. Who owns what, and by what right. What a court may do to you and what it may never do. Whether the schooling a child gets tracks the accident of which house they were born in. How much of what you earn stays yours, and how much is claimed, and for whom. What happens to the person who cannot work — and to the person who could and won't. Who may speak, who may worship, who may be locked up, and on what showing. One document. It binds everyone. There is no appeal and no second draft.

Take the commission. There is only one condition attached.

Picture the room they put you in to do the work. No windows. No clock. And no mirrors — none anywhere, and you'll see why in a moment. Because before you write a word, some things have to go.

Your bank balance goes. Your job goes, along with the qualifications that got it. Your parents go — who they were, what they had, what they knew, what they were able to give you. Your country goes, and your century. Your health goes: you don't know whether you'll wake up in a body that runs marathons or one that needs a machine to breathe. Your mind goes, in the sense that matters — you don't know if you'll be quick or slow, whether you'll find reading easy or find it a wall. Your face goes. Your name goes. Your religion goes, and so does the question of whether the people in charge will find your religion tolerable. Your talents go, all of them, along with the thing that talent turns into, which is money. And the last one to go is the deepest and the strangest: your own idea of what makes a life worth living goes too. You don't know whether you'll want a family or solitude, faith or science, wealth or a quiet life spent making something nobody buys.

What's left? Quite a lot, actually. You still know everything general — how economies work, and what shortages do, and how people behave under pressure, how power gets abused and how institutions rot. You know that resources will be tight but not desperate. You know human beings and what they're like. Every fact about the world stays.

Only the facts about *you* are gone.

So you're standing there, holding the pen, knowing everything except which of the several billion positions inside your own design you are about to occupy. When the rules take effect, the mist lifts, and you find out who you are: the surgeon, the woman who cleans the ward, the child with the rare disease, the heir, the migrant, the person the majority has decided it doesn't much like the look of.

Write the rules. {pause: 1.5}

Take a second and actually do it. Not the whole document — one or two choices. How much inequality do you allow? How hard do you make it to be poor? Do you protect the minority religion at some cost to the majority's convenience, or not bother? {pause: 1.2}

Now, whatever you just chose — the question that matters is not whether it was clever. It's whether it was *different*. Different, I mean, from what you'd have written if you'd known.

## The offer   {tone: measured}

Almost everybody, doing this honestly, drifts the same direction. Not toward some perfect flat equality — most people don't want that, and can see the reasons not to want it. But toward insurance. You find yourself thinking: I could be the one at the bottom. So the bottom had better not be a pit. Make sure the floor holds. Make sure the person nobody chose to be can still live a life.

That's a real intuition and it does something no argument had managed to do for about a century, which we'll come to. But before we go anywhere near a theory, let's put pressure on it, because a conclusion that hasn't been tested isn't yours yet.

Change one thing. Suppose, as you pick up the pen, you're handed the odds. You are told — reliably — that there's a ninety per cent chance you land in the top fifth of whatever society you design. Nine times out of ten, you're the surgeon.

Do you write the same rules? {pause: 1.2}

Be honest. A lot of people, at that point, start to gamble. Push the ceiling up, let the floor sag a little, play the odds. And notice what that means: the moment you're given any information about your own likely position, the design starts bending toward it. Which is a slightly humiliating discovery about how human beings arrive at their sense of what's fair.

Now change one more thing, and this is the one that stings.

Somebody sidles up and offers you a look. Not for everyone — just for you. A glance at your own card. You'll know exactly who you're going to be before you write a single clause, and nobody will ever find out you looked.

Do you take it? {pause: 1.5}

Of course you do. Almost everybody does, and the reason is that we all know perfectly well that knowing your position changes what you call justice — and that's exactly why the offer feels like cheating. There's a confession buried in the temptation. If knowing who you'll be makes no difference to a fair rule, looking is harmless. If it does make a difference, then the rules people write while knowing who they are — which is to say, every rule anybody has ever actually written — are contaminated at the source.

That's the whole intuition, and it was in the room before any philosopher arrived. It's the reason we make one child cut the cake and the other choose the slice. It's the reason a judge who owns shares in the company recuses herself, even if she's honest — *especially* if she's honest. Not because we think she'd lie, but because we've grasped something about the relationship between knowing your own stake and being able to see straight.

What you've just done has a name, and the name belongs to an American philosopher who, in 1971, took that homely little trick with the cake and built out of it the most influential work of political philosophy of the twentieth century. The condition you were working under, with your identity stripped away, he called the *veil of ignorance*. The situation you were standing in — free, equal, and blind about yourself — he called the *original position*.

His name is John Rawls. And to see what he did, you have to see what he did it *against*, because when he sat down to write, the question you just answered was not supposed to be answerable at all.

## The dead subject   {tone: contemplative}

Consider the state of the argument at mid-century, because it's the strangest moment in this whole series.

Twenty-two episodes. Twenty-four centuries. Every one of them a version of one question: when does raw power become rightful authority, and by what standard? Thrasymachus said there was no standard, only the interest of the stronger dressed up in a robe. Plato went looking for justice inside the soul. Aristotle found it in nature, and then baptised slavery as part of the same nature. Augustine said no earthly city could be just, and Aquinas said an unjust law was hardly a law. Hobbes grounded it in fear, Locke in consent, Rousseau in a general will, Kant in reason itself. Hegel put it in history, Marx underneath history, in the ownership of the means of production. Bentham said the standard was measurable — add up the happiness. Tocqueville worried that the standard could be voted away. Nietzsche said the standard was a weapon forged by the weak and we'd have to live without it.

And then, in the generation before Rawls, the question was closed. Not answered. *Closed.*

Two things closed it, and we met one of them last episode. Weber's move: the scholar's job is to explain why people obey, never to pronounce on what deserves to be obeyed, because the second thing isn't knowledge — it's a choice of which god you serve. And alongside that, from a different direction entirely, the hard-edged philosophy of science that came out of Vienna and colonised the English-speaking universities, which held that a sentence only means something if you can say what would confirm it. Run "the tax is unjust" through that machine and nothing comes out. There's no observation that settles it. So the sentence isn't false — it's worse than false. It isn't a claim at all. It's a noise you make when you disapprove, with a grammatical structure borrowed from real statements.

The result, in the philosophy departments, was decades of extremely careful work on what political words *mean* and almost nothing on what political arrangements *deserve*. As one editor put it, introducing a collection in the mid-fifties: political philosophy was dead. Not ailing. Dead. If you wanted to argue about justice, you were welcome to — in a newspaper, or a pulpit, or a party meeting. Just not in a seminar, where the grown-ups were.

Now sit with the size of what that means, because it isn't an academic curiosity. It means that at the exact moment in human history when the largest questions about justice were being settled by force — total war, industrial extermination, empire coming apart, a colour bar written into the law of a democracy — the discipline whose entire subject was justice had declared itself professionally unable to comment.

Into which walks a very quiet man who had seen some of it from the inside.

## The infantryman   {tone: somber}

Here I have to be careful, and I'd rather tell you why than pretend.

The bare facts of Rawls's life are not in dispute. Born in 1921. Educated at Princeton. He served in the United States Army as an infantryman in the Pacific in the Second World War. He taught at Harvard from the early sixties for the rest of his working life. He was, by every account of everyone who knew him, an almost pathologically modest man — a stammerer, uninterested in fame, who wrote drafts for twenty years before publishing the book.

The inner story is thinner and rests largely on his own much later reflections, so take it as that. He had been religious as a young man — seriously so, to the point of considering the ministry. And what's widely reported, and what he indicated himself in writing found after his death, is that the war took it from him: the specific horror of the war, and then the Holocaust, and the impossibility of holding together an omnipotent benevolent God with what had just happened to Europe and to the men beside him.

I'm not going to embroider that, because the record won't carry the weight and the temptation to make it a neat origin story is exactly the temptation to resist. But I'll point at one thing, because it's not speculation about his soul — it's visible in every page he wrote.

An infantryman learns something about fate that is very hard to learn anywhere else. The shell lands on the man to your left. Not because he was worse than you, or lazier, or less brave — because he was standing on the left. Nothing about the distribution of who came home and who didn't tracked anything about the merit of the men involved. Spend a year inside that and you may come out with a permanent allergy to a particular kind of sentence — the sentence that begins *well, people generally get what they deserve*.

Rawls spent the rest of his life building a machine to test that sentence. The machine is the room you were just standing in.

And when he published, in 1971, the effect on the subject was immediate and total. Not because everyone agreed — almost nobody agreed with all of it. Because he'd demonstrated, in six hundred pages of unglamorous argument, that you could reason about justice at full rigour without either preaching or giving up. The dead subject sat up on the table.

The claim lands like this: he says, right near the front, that justice is the first virtue of social institutions, as truth is of systems of thought. First. Not one consideration among several to be traded off. The thing an institution has to get right before its efficiency or its grandeur counts for anything at all.

And then he goes after the reigning theory of his day. Not Marx, not Nietzsche. The theory that everyone sensible in the English-speaking world had quietly settled into as a default, because it was clear and modern and could be done with numbers.

## Adding up the wrong thing   {tone: lucid}

Four episodes ago we watched Bentham build his measuring device. The greatest happiness of the greatest number: the only rational test of any law, any institution, any government. Push the pleasure up, push the pain down, count everybody equally, and let the sum decide. Mill made it more humane, roped off a zone of liberty and insisted some pleasures were higher than others — but the engine stayed the same. Aggregate, and maximise.

Take a case.

Suppose there's a policy that would make a very large number of people modestly better off. Cheaper energy, say — millions of households, a real difference. And suppose the cost falls on a small, identifiable group who happen to live where the thing must be built, or who belong to a community whose interests nobody will lose an election over. Their lives are made materially worse, permanently, and they will never be compensated, because compensating them would eat the gain.

Run the sum. The many small benefits outweigh the few large harms. The arithmetic is clean. The policy passes.

And most people, hearing that, feel something snag — a sense that something has gone wrong which the arithmetic can't see. But it's genuinely hard to say *what*, because the calculation counted everybody. Nobody was left out. Each person's pain went in at full weight. That's the pride of the theory: it's scrupulously impartial. Everyone counts for one.

Rawls locates the flaw, and when you see it you can't unsee it.

The problem isn't that utilitarianism ignores people. It's how it *combines* them. The whole apparatus takes the model of one rational person planning one life — where trading off a bad afternoon for a good decade is not merely permissible but wise — and applies that same model to a society. But a society isn't one person having a mixed day. When you accept a painful dentist's appointment for the sake of your own later comfort, the one who suffers and the one who gains are the same one, and that identity is what makes the trade legitimate. When a policy makes them different people, the compensation happens nowhere. There is no vessel in which the community's pleasure and the sacrificed family's misery can be poured together and stirred. The gain is in one life; the loss is in another. And the sum silently pretends otherwise.

Rawls's phrase for the failure is exact, and I'll give you his words rather than a paraphrase, because philosophers have been quoting them for fifty years: utilitarianism does not take seriously the *distinction between persons*.

And out of that comes the sentence that is the true foundation of the book: each person possesses an inviolability founded on justice that even the welfare of society as a whole cannot override.

Not *usually* cannot. Cannot.

You'll notice we've been here before, from the other side. Marx looked at exactly this liberal picture of separate individuals holding rights against each other and called them rights of separation — the freedom of a man walled off from other men, the liberty of the monad. What Marx read as a symptom of a broken society, Rawls treats as the bedrock moral fact. You are not a portion of a larger thing. You are not a district in the accounts of the general welfare. You have one life, it is yours, and nobody gets to spend it for the greater total.

Fix that phrase in your mind now, because in about half an hour it's going to be picked up, turned around, and driven straight back into Rawls's chest. Philosophers usually call the idea the *separateness of persons*. Rawls's own words were "the distinction between persons." The man who's going to do the driving has his own formulation, and we'll get to it.

## The oldest device in the book   {tone: curious}

If the sum won't do it, what will?

Rawls reaches back — a long way back — for the answer, and picks up the most disreputable tool in the shed.

Go back fourteen episodes and you're standing in Hobbes's state of nature, where the terror is so complete that people will trade everything they have for peace. Thirteen episodes ago: Locke, insisting you could only be governed by a power you'd consented to. Twelve: Rousseau, hunting for a form of association in which you obey only yourself. And then eleven episodes ago, Hume, taking the whole tradition apart with about three pages of plain historical common sense. What contract? When? Signed by whom? Show me the document. Governments were founded by conquest and consolidated by habit, and the pretence that you personally agreed to anything is a fiction that flatters everybody involved.

That objection has never been answered on its own terms, because it can't be. Rawls doesn't try. He does something cleverer, and the move comes straight from Kant.

Kant, ten episodes ago, had already said the contract was not an event. It's an *idea of reason* — a test you apply. Not: did they agree? But: is this the kind of arrangement they *could* have agreed to, as free and equal beings? Rawls takes that and makes it operational. The original position isn't a moment in prehistory. It's not a place. It's a piece of apparatus — a device of representation, he calls it — that you can step into any afternoon, with a pen, to find out what a rule looks like when nobody's stake in it is visible.

And what's it for? To make the *procedure* fair, so that whatever comes out the other end inherits the fairness. That's the reason the theory has the name it has: not "justice as equality," not "justice as welfare." *Justice as fairness.* Rig the choosing situation so that no one can tilt it toward themselves, and then whatever they'd choose is what justice is. There's no independent standard sitting outside, waiting to be discovered, the way Plato thought. Cut the cake blind and there's no further question about whether the slices are right.

This is where the veil earns its keep, and it's worth saying precisely what it's doing. It isn't there to simulate ignorance for the fun of it. It's there to strip out exactly the information that could be used to write a rule biased in your favour — and nothing else. That's why you keep all the general knowledge. Rawls isn't asking you to choose stupidly. He's asking you to choose *without a thumb on the scale*, and the discovery at the heart of the book is that if you take away the thumb, everyone behind the veil reasons identically, because there's nothing left to distinguish them. Which produces a strange and rather beautiful consequence: unanimity comes for free. You don't need to negotiate with the others behind the veil. Any one of them, thinking straight, reaches the same conclusion you do. The original position has a population of billions and, for practical purposes, exactly one occupant.

So what does that occupant choose? This is the part where it gets hard, and I'd rather say so than pretend the next stretch is easy. Stay with it, because this is where a felt intuition turns into something you can argue with — and where the trouble starts.

## The two principles   {tone: lucid, note: "the central exposition; patient, building, one moving part at a time"}

You're behind the veil with the pen. You need a shortlist of what to secure, before you know what you'll want. Rawls calls the things on that list *primary goods*: whatever any rational person needs whatever else they turn out to want. Rights and liberties. Opportunities and powers. Income and wealth. And one more, which is easy to skip past and which matters enormously — the social basis of self-respect. The standing to look other citizens in the eye.

Now the choosing. Two principles come out, and the order they come in is not decoration — it's the load-bearing structure of the whole thing.

The first: each person has the same claim to a fully adequate scheme of equal basic liberties. Vote and stand for office. Speak, assemble, publish. Freedom of conscience and of thought — worship or don't, believe or don't, and no one may compel you either way. Freedom of the person, and freedom from arbitrary arrest and seizure. The right to hold personal property. Due process. Not a wish list; a schedule, and equal for everybody.

Rawls's word for the claim is the one I'd underline: *indefeasible*. Not "very important." Not defeasible-in-an-emergency. It can't be traded away, and specifically it can't be traded for money — not for a lot of money, not for an enormous amount of money spread across an enormous number of people.

That's the first principle. Here's the priority rule that hangs off it, which people miss and then misread the whole theory.

The principles are ranked in what he calls *lexical* priority. Like alphabetical order: you settle the first letter completely before the second letter gets any say at all. Which means the liberties are satisfied *first*, in full, and only then does anything about the distribution of money get considered. You may not buy a poor country's basic freedoms with growth. You may not suspend the free press to raise the standard of living, however genuinely it would rise.

Pre-empt the obvious misreading here, because it's everywhere: Rawls is not a philosopher who cares about equality and treats liberty as negotiable. He's a philosopher for whom liberty comes first and is not up for auction. He wrote in the country and the decade in which that mattered, when the argument for trading freedom for bread was being made in earnest all over the world.

Right — that's the first letter. Now the second, and it has two parts of its own, also ranked.

Part one: *fair equality of opportunity*. Careful with this, because it's much stronger than the version you've heard. The weak version — the one every modern society claims — is that offices are formally open to all: no legal bars, apply if you like, may the best candidate win. Rawls says that isn't enough, and the reason is obvious the moment it's stated. If two children have the same native talent and the same willingness to work, but one is born to a family with money, books, connections and expectations, and the other isn't, then the race was decided before the gun. Formal openness is a starting line painted at different distances from the finish. Fair equality of opportunity demands that people with the same abilities and the same ambitions have the same *prospects*, regardless of the class they were born into.

That is a fiercely demanding standard, and no society on earth meets it. Nowhere close.

And then, underneath it, part two. The famous one. Inequalities in wealth and income are permitted only if they work to the greatest benefit of the *least advantaged* members of society. Rawls calls it the *difference principle*.

Let me restate that in plain words and then let it sit, because the next twenty minutes turn on it. {pause: 0.8}

You may be richer than me — much richer — but the arrangement that lets you be richer has to be an arrangement that leaves the least advantaged better off than any alternative arrangement would.

And be careful with "least advantaged," because there's a misreading here that trips people constantly. It means a *position* in the social structure — the people at the bottom of the distribution, taken as a group, the representative worst-off citizen. It is not a hunt for the single most unfortunate human being alive, and that person does not hold a personal veto over the design of a society. The test is aimed at the floor, not at an individual.

So: inequality isn't forbidden. Inequality has to *pay rent*, and the rent is paid to the bottom.

Now three things about it, because it's routinely caricatured, and the caricature makes the rest of the argument unintelligible.

First: this is not a demand for equal outcomes, and Rawls is not a leveller. If a market economy with sharp differentials in pay generates innovation and productivity that make the poorest people materially better off than they'd be under a flat distribution, the difference principle *requires* the differentials. It is entirely open to the empirical answer that inequality is good for the bottom. It just insists on asking, and on holding the answer to that test rather than to the size of the total.

Second: notice what it does to the utilitarian sum. The difference principle refuses to look at averages or totals. It looks at the floor. A society could double its national income and fail this test, if the doubling came from the top and left the bottom where it was. Rawls has replaced *how much is there* with *how are the people at the bottom doing* — and that substitution is either the great moral advance of modern political thought or an arbitrary fixation on one end of the distribution, depending on who you ask.

Third, and honestly: the argument that the person behind the veil would reason this way — attend to the worst case rather than to the best average bet — is contested, and it was contested immediately, by very serious people. The objection is that a rational chooser facing unknown odds should maximise their *expected* position, not fortify against the worst one. Behind the veil, the reasoning goes, you should take the gamble with the best average return, which is not obviously a society built around its floor. There's a technical literature on this and I'm not going to formalise it, because the interesting part isn't technical. Rawls's reply is that this isn't an ordinary bet. There's one draw, it's your entire life, the stakes at the bottom are catastrophic and irreversible, and — his most human argument — you have to be able to *live* with the rules whichever body you wake up in. He calls it the strains of commitment: you can't sign a constitution you might not be able to keep faith with once you find out you're the loser. Whether that's a proof or a psychological guess wearing a proof's clothing is a fight that has never been settled. Say that plainly and move on, because underneath the argument about betting there's something much deeper, and it's the real reason the difference principle has the grip it has.

## The ticket you were handed   {tone: contemplative}

Forget the veil for a moment. Look at yourself.

Whatever you are good at — take the best of it. The thing you can do that most people can't. The quickness, the ear, the hands, the memory, the nerve.

Where did you get it? {pause: 1.2}

Not: what did you do with it. That part is yours, or feels like it. Where did the raw material come from? You didn't choose your genes. You didn't choose to be born in a decade and a country where the thing you happen to be good at is lavishly rewarded rather than useless. And which era you land in is pure luck: a talent for reading weather and animals was worth everything for most of human history, and is worth nothing much now. You didn't choose the parents who read to you, or the school, or the illness you didn't get at seven.

You were handed a ticket. You didn't buy it and you didn't earn it, and the number on it is doing a great deal of the work in explaining where you've ended up.

Now the argument itself. Rawls says the distribution of native endowment is *arbitrary from a moral point of view*. Not unfair — nobody's to blame for it, there's no culprit; the natural lottery isn't a crime. Arbitrary. It doesn't track anything about desert, because it happens before there's anyone there to deserve anything. And a social order that simply reproduces that arbitrary distribution in the distribution of income, honour and power hasn't done anything just. It has just let the lottery through, uninspected.

Now for the part that makes people genuinely angry, and I think it should be met head-on rather than softened.

The obvious reply is: fine — I didn't earn the talent, but I earned what I *did* with it. Nobody handed me the ten thousand hours. I got up early. I worked when others didn't. Whatever the ticket said, the effort was mine.

Rawls goes after that too. His claim is that even the willingness to make an effort — to try, to keep at it, to be deserving in the ordinary sense — is itself shaped by fortunate family and social circumstances. The capacity for discipline isn't self-generated either. It was built in you, mostly early, mostly by people and conditions you didn't pick.

Test it against a case, because it's easy to wave away as a philosopher's trick. Two children. One is raised in a household where adults model postponement — where finishing things is normal, where the future is a real place you can invest in because the adults around you have futures. The other is raised where nothing lasts, where planning is punished, where the future is a rumour. At twenty-five, the first has the work ethic and the second doesn't, and everyone praises the first for their character. But nobody chose the household. And if the trait we praise most — the trait that supposedly redeems the whole story and makes success *earned* — was itself installed by circumstances the person didn't select, then the appeal to effort doesn't escape the lottery. It just relocates it.

Sit with that, because it's vertiginous, and if you feel resistance, hold onto the resistance — it's about to be someone's argument. {pause: 1.0}

What Rawls builds on that is the sentence that will detonate the second half of this episode. The difference principle, he says, amounts in effect to an agreement to regard the distribution of natural talents as a *common asset*, and to share in the benefits of that distribution however it happens to fall.

A common asset. Your gifts, considered as a resource the society has, whose yield everyone has some claim on. Not seized — the talented person keeps their liberties, keeps their choices, is nobody's property. But they may draw the extra reward their gifts command only on terms that also lift the people at the bottom.

And now, at last, we can put the whole thing together, and it's a genuinely magnificent structure. Start from the separateness of persons — you are not a portion of a total. Refuse the arithmetic that spends one life for another's benefit. Ask what free and equal people would agree to when nobody can game the terms. Get out: inviolable liberties for everyone first, then a fair start regardless of birth, then inequality only where it lifts the floor. Twenty-four centuries of argument, given a modern engine and a test you can actually run.

Which is the point at which somebody down the corridor picked it up, examined it, and found — as he thought — the fingerprints of the very thing it was built to prevent.

## Down the hall   {tone: tense}

Same university. Same decade. Same faculty.

Three years after *A Theory of Justice*, in 1974, a younger colleague of Rawls's at Harvard published a book called *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, and it opens with an assertion so flat it reads like a fist on a table.

Individuals have rights. And there are things no person and no group may do to them without violating those rights. So strong and so far-reaching are these rights, he says, that they raise the question of what, if anything, the state may do at all.

His name is Robert Nozick, and he is the most formidable thing Rawls ever ran into: a first-rate philosopher who had read him with total attention and admired him without reserve. That last part is worth dwelling on, because it's rare and it makes the duel serious. Nozick wrote of *A Theory of Justice* that it was a powerful, deep, subtle, wide-ranging, systematic work of political and moral philosophy the like of which hadn't been seen since John Stuart Mill — if then. Political philosophers, he said, must now either work within Rawls's theory or explain why they don't.

And then he spent the better part of a chapter explaining why he didn't.

Start with his own foundation, because the whole shock of what follows comes from where he starts.

Nozick's rights are what he calls *side constraints*. Take a second on that phrase, because it's the technical heart of the book and it's usually skated over. There are two utterly different ways to build morality out of rights. You can treat rights as a *goal*: minimise the total number of rights violations in the world. That sounds admirable, and notice where it takes you — if violating one person's rights would prevent two other violations, you must do it. Rights as a target to be maximised licenses trampling one person for the sake of the score. It's the utilitarian machine with rights substituted for happiness, and it has the same structure and therefore the same disease.

Or you can treat rights as *constraints on what you may do* — boundaries around each person that your actions may not cross, whatever good lies on the other side. Not to be maximised. Not to be traded. Simply not to be crossed. You may not use a person as a resource. Full stop, even if the arithmetic is glorious.

Why should morality have that shape? Here is Nozick's answer, and here's where the floor gives way.

Because there is no social entity, he says. No collective being with a good of its own that suffers a sacrifice for its own greater benefit. There are only individual people, different individual people, each with their own individual life. To use one of them for the benefit of others is to use *him*, and to benefit *them*, and nothing more. The reason nobody may be sacrificed for the whole is what he calls, in a phrase on page thirty-three of that book, "the fact of our separate existences."

Read that again and hear what's happened.

That is Rawls's premise. Word for word, in substance — the exact objection Rawls made to Bentham, the inviolability of the person, the refusal to treat separate lives as one ledger. It's the thing Rawls built his cathedral on.

And Nozick is about to use it to argue that Rawls's cathedral is a machine for using people.

## Two tickets   {tone: curious, note: "the Chamberlain argument; lively, almost playful, then the trap"}

He gets there by way of a basketball player.

Start wherever you like. That's the generosity of the argument, and its danger. Pick any distribution of goods you consider perfectly just — by *your* standard, not his. Flat equality, if you want. Or the full Rawlsian arrangement: equal liberties, fair opportunity, every inequality earning its keep by lifting the worst off. Whatever your ideal is, imagine it in place, everything exactly as it should be. Call that the just starting point.

Now put a real man into it. Wilt Chamberlain — and it matters that Nozick reached for a real one, the outstanding basketball player of the era, a man people would genuinely cross a city and pay to watch. Chamberlain is in enormous demand. Teams want him. So he signs a contract with terms of his own devising: for every home game, twenty-five cents from each ticket sold goes directly to him, separately, into a box.

Nobody is deceived. It's written on the ticket. And a million people, over a season, decide that watching this man play is worth a quarter more than whatever else that quarter was going to do for them, and they drop it in the box, and they go in, and they have a wonderful time.

At the end of the season Chamberlain has two hundred and fifty thousand dollars — in 1974, an amount vastly above any average income, more than anyone else in the picture has.

Your perfectly just distribution is gone. In its place is a sharply unequal one.

So: where, exactly, did the injustice happen? {pause: 1.5}

Walk it. The starting point was just — you chose it. Every single transfer afterwards was one adult voluntarily handing over a quarter that was indisputably theirs to spend, in exchange for something they wanted more, from a man who was under no obligation to play at all. No fraud. No coercion. No hidden term. Nobody's rights crossed, on anybody's account of rights. If the holdings were just at the start, and every step from there was legitimate, then by what possible argument is the result illegitimate?

And if the result *is* illegitimate — if the pattern must be restored — then something has to be done about it. Which means somebody has to be stopped, or something has to be taken back. Either the fans are prevented from giving Chamberlain their quarters, or the quarters are removed from him afterwards. There's no third option.

Nozick's conclusion is the sentence that made this the most-argued page in modern political philosophy. *Liberty upsets patterns.* Any pattern. Choose any picture of how holdings ought to be arranged — equal shares, shares by merit, shares by need, or Rawls's arrangement where every gap is justified by what it does for the bottom — and free people, doing nothing wrong, will wander out of it within a generation, by ordinary voluntary acts. So no patterned principle of distribution can be *continuously* realised without continuous interference in people's lives. Not one intervention. Continuous. Forever. Because the pattern is not where free people naturally come to rest.

Now — is that a knockdown? Not yet, and I'll put the honest objection in a moment. But feel the force of it first, because the force is real, and it comes from a place that isn't about economics at all. It's about which question you think you're asking.

## The photograph and the film   {tone: lucid}

Because Nozick's deeper claim is that Rawls has the whole shape of the question wrong.

Suppose I show you a photograph of a distribution. Ten per cent of these people hold sixty per cent of the stuff. Is that just?

Rawls's method can work on the photograph. Check it against the pattern: are the liberties equal, were the positions open, does the gap lift the floor? Answer yes and it passes; answer no and it doesn't. The photograph contains what you need.

Nozick says the photograph contains nothing at all. You cannot possibly tell whether a distribution is just from looking at it, any more than you can tell whether a chess position is legal from looking at the board. What you need is the *record of the game*. Not a snapshot — a film. How did each piece get where it is?

That's the *entitlement theory*, and it has three parts.

Justice in acquisition: how something previously unowned may legitimately become someone's in the first place. Nozick takes over Locke's problem from thirteen episodes ago, and Locke's hedge with it — that your appropriation must not worsen the position of others, which he treats as a real constraint and which turns out to be devilishly hard to specify.

Justice in transfer: how a holding may legitimately move from one person to another. Voluntarily. Gift, trade, wager, inheritance. Whatever both parties agree to.

And justice in *rectification*: what is owed when the first two were violated — when something, somewhere back down the line, was taken by force or fraud, and the whole chain of transfers since then runs out of a theft. He names it as the third part of his own theory, and then, notoriously, he doesn't build it. Hold onto that. It's the weak joint in his own structure, we will come back to it, and it may matter more than anything else in the book.

The first two parts, though, give him his engine, and the engine is one line: whatever arises from a just situation by just steps is itself just. That's it. Justice is *historical* — a property of how things came about, not of how they look — and it's what he calls *unpatterned*: there is no shape the outcome has to have. The result of countless free choices doesn't have to look like anything. It just has to have happened rightly.

Which means the phrase we use for this whole area of philosophy is already loaded against him, and he says so. "Distributive justice" — the *distribution* of wealth. The word conjures a pile in the middle of the room and someone handing out portions, and a fair question about whether they handed them out fairly. But there is no pile and no hander. In a free society, goods come into the world already attached to somebody — made by this person, grown by that one, paid for by a third out of what she already had. There is no moment when the social product sits unowned in the middle of the room awaiting allocation. Rawls's difference principle, Nozick thinks, treats it as though there were.

And now we can answer the question he opened the book with — what, if anything, may the state do at all. His answer is: a little, and only a little. A state that protects people against force, theft and fraud, and enforces the contracts they entered freely, is justified. That's the *minimal state*, and that's the whole of the permitted list. Anything past it — a state that redistributes income, or forbids you things for your own good — is not justified, because it has to cross somebody's boundary to function. And notice who he argues the first half of that against: not the socialist, but the individualist anarchist standing on his other side, who says even a minimal state must violate rights to exist at all. Nozick spends the opening third of the book showing how private protective associations could grow, by steps nobody could object to, into something with a state's authority. He spends the closing third sketching what such a framework would leave room for — a world of voluntary communities, where anyone who wants to live communally may, provided nobody is conscripted into it.

But the part everyone remembers is the middle. And the middle is about tax.

He takes it to the place most people would rather he didn't, and I'll give it to you at full strength, because a steelman that flinches is worthless. He writes, flatly, that taxation of earnings from labour is on a par with forced labour.

Hear the argument before you reject the phrase. Two people, equally well off. The first prefers going to the cinema, so she takes on extra hours to pay for the ticket. The second prefers watching the sunset, which costs nothing, so he doesn't. Now the state needs revenue for redistribution and takes a portion of earnings. It takes from her, because she earned. It takes nothing from him, because he didn't. Both had a taste; hers was one that had to be paid for in money and his wasn't; and the system has therefore reached into her extra hours and not into his afternoon. Why should the person whose preferences happen to require earning be conscripted, and the person whose preferences happen to be free of charge go untouched? What the state has taken, on this reading, is not a number in an account. It's a claim on part of what she did with her hours — a portion of her labour, directed by someone else, to purposes she didn't choose.

You may think that's a wild overstatement of what a tax return is. I think it is. But notice what it's doing — it's putting a *person* where Rawls's argument had a distribution, and asking what is owed to her, by name. Which is exactly the move Rawls himself made against Bentham.

And that's the trap closing.

## The premise, turned around   {tone: dramatic, note: "the crux of the whole series; give both voices full weight"}

So here is the crux, and it's the reason this is the last episode.

Rawls says: society may not sacrifice one person for the benefit of others, because persons are separate and each has a life of their own. Therefore we reject the utilitarian sum. Therefore we build institutions that guarantee the people at the bottom the best floor available.

And Nozick says: look at what you just built.

Look at the talented woman under the difference principle. She may develop her gifts, and she may profit from them — but only, in your own words, on terms that improve the position of others. You called the distribution of natural talents a common asset. You said her willingness to work was not really hers either, since it was installed in her by circumstance. What is left of *her* in this description? Her abilities have become a resource that the society draws on; the gap between what she produces and what she keeps is set by reference to somebody else's welfare. She is permitted to flourish to the extent that her flourishing is useful to the badly off.

That, Nozick says, is using a person as a means. Not crudely. Not violently. Institutionally, politely, with excellent reasons — which is the only way it's ever done. And the objection Rawls made to Bentham applies unchanged: someone's life is being drawn on for a benefit that lands in a different life, and the compensation happens nowhere, because there is no vessel in which her forgone earnings and their improved floor can be poured together and stirred.

He presses one more question, and it's the sharpest thing in the chapter. Why should the social product be treated as though it fell from the sky? The difference principle asks how the joint product ought to be divided, as though the goods arrived unattached and the only question was allocation. But things arrive attached. If they arrive attached, the question isn't *how shall we divide this*. The question is *by what right do you take any of it from the person it arrived attached to*.

The separateness of persons cuts both ways. Rawls used it to say: you may not spend the individual for the sake of the aggregate. Nozick uses it to say: and that includes spending the able individual for the sake of the least advantaged. You cannot invoke the inviolability of the person as your foundation and then treat their capacities as a collective endowment. Pick one.

That is a genuinely great objection. It's the best objection in modern political philosophy, and it's aimed at the joint of the argument, not the surface.

Now — Rawls has a reply, and I don't think most people who love the Chamberlain argument have really taken its measure, so let's give it properly.

The reply comes in two parts, and the first is about *where* the principles apply. The difference principle is not a rule for judging transactions. It isn't a licence for an official to inspect your quarter on its way to Chamberlain. It applies to what Rawls calls the *basic structure* — the constitution, the property system, the tax code, the schools — the background frame that everyone operates inside and that is known to everyone in advance. Nobody is commandeered. Nobody's earnings are reviewed for social usefulness. The talented woman is entirely free to do whatever she likes, and she does it under publicly known rules, and she keeps whatever those rules assign to her. That's a different thing from being conscripted, in the same way that playing chess under the rules of chess is different from having your hand moved for you.

And the second part goes right to the root, and it's the deepest disagreement in the whole quarrel. Nozick's argument requires that we know what's *yours* before we ask what the state may take. His entire case runs on a prior fact of ownership: this is hers, therefore taking it needs justification. Rawls denies that there is any such prior fact. Your pre-tax income is not a natural object that exists and then gets taxed. It's a number produced by a legal and institutional order — property law, contract law, corporate charters, patents, currency, courts, the police who keep other people from taking it, the roads and the schools that made the market you sold into. Change any of those and the number changes. There is no pre-institutional income sitting there with your name on it, waiting for the state to arrive. Which means "how much of my money may they take" is not the first question. The first question is what set of institutions, including the ones that define ownership, could be justified to everyone. What's mine is an *output* of the answer, not an input to it.

And there, precisely, is bedrock, because Nozick has an answer to that too, and it isn't weak. If property is nothing but whatever the collective decides it is, then the individual has no standing against the collective at all. Your holdings exist at the pleasure of the arrangement, revisable whenever the arrangement is revised — and the separateness of persons, which was supposed to be the foundation, has been reduced to a decoration on the front of a building that the community owns outright.

That is the deadlock. It's not a misunderstanding. Both men accept that persons are separate and inviolable; they disagree about what a person *is*, for purposes of justice. For Rawls, you are a being with one life to lead whose entire starting position — body, mind, family, era — was handed to you by a lottery with no moral authority, and justice consists in not letting that arbitrariness set your fate. For Nozick, you are the author of a history of choices, and justice consists in not overriding those choices for the sake of a picture somebody prefers.

Two accounts of the same word. And the whole modern argument runs down that crack.

Before we leave it, though — one thing must be said about Nozick's own position, because a fair fight means the other side's weak joint gets named too, and this one is not a detail. It's the piece he left unbuilt.

Remember his three principles. Acquisition, transfer — and *rectification*. Because his theory is historical, it has a devastating implication that he saw clearly and stated himself: if the actual past contains injustice, and the present grew out of that past, then the present holdings are not just, and something must be done to put them right.

Now look at the actual past. Conquest. Enslavement. Expropriation. Land taken by force from people who were then killed or driven off it. Labour extracted from human beings who were themselves treated as property. Fortunes built on all of it and passed down through inheritance — which is to say, through the transfer principle, cleanly, generation after generation, from a starting point that was theft.

By Nozick's own logic, an enormous proportion of what anyone holds anywhere fails the test. And what does he offer to fix it? He tells you, candidly, that he isn't going to provide a theory of rectification — that the subject hasn't been worked out and he doesn't know of a satisfactory treatment of it. He gestures at what might be involved and leaves it there. And then he says something extraordinary, which his admirers rarely quote: that perhaps the best way to view patterned principles of distribution — of the very kind he has spent the chapter demolishing — is as rough rules of thumb, approximating what a proper principle of rectification would deliver.

Read that slowly. The most rigorous case ever made against redistribution contains, in its own author's hand, the concession that something like a redistributive pattern might be the best available approximation of justice, given the history we actually have.

The critics' point is not that this is a slip. It's that applied honestly, with a real history rather than an idealised one, the entitlement theory may be the most radical doctrine in this entire series — because almost no title on earth is clean. Nozick built a machine to protect holdings from the state and never installed the part that decides which holdings qualify. It's an unfinished building. He said so. And whether the missing part could be built, or whether the whole structure would come down with it, has never been resolved.

He never went back for it. His later career is one of the odder facts about this duel: having written the book that defined a position for a generation, Nozick declined to spend his life defending it, and moved on to write about other things entirely — decision theory, the examined life, the nature of consciousness. In a book at the end of the eighties he wrote that the libertarian position he had once propounded now seemed to him seriously inadequate. He later told an interviewer that reports of his apostasy had been much exaggerated — that he'd stopped being as hardcore as he was, not stopped being libertarian. Both of those are on the record and they don't sit comfortably together. What can be said fairly is that he did not treat his own most famous argument as a fortress he was obliged to hold.

Rawls, by contrast, spent thirty years going back over his own building — rewriting the theory to face the fact of deep religious and moral disagreement among citizens, working out how a society of people with irreconcilable convictions could still share principles of justice, and asking, at the end, what any of it meant for relations between peoples rather than inside one. The finale of his career was not a defence. It was a renovation.

## Where I come down   {tone: measured, note: "step from referee to advocate; earned, specific, no hedging"}

Twenty-three episodes of holding the ring. So — my own view, for whatever it's worth, and I'll be specific rather than grand, because a vague verdict is worth nothing.

Three things I think are settled, and one that isn't.

First: Rawls asked the right question, and asking it was an act of intellectual courage that we underrate because it succeeded. He walked into a discipline that had declared his subject unanswerable — Weber's silence, the positivists' shrug, an entire generation's conviction that "this is unjust" was a noise and not a claim — and he demonstrated by doing it that you can reason about justice at full rigour without smuggling in your prejudices as the structure of reality. Whatever survives of his conclusions, that stands. He got the argument out of the pulpit and back into the seminar, and everybody arguing about justice today, including everyone who despises his answers, is working in the space he cleared.

Second: I think the natural lottery is the most important thing in the book, and I don't think it can be walked back. Nobody arranged to be born clever, or healthy, or into a house with books, or in a decade that pays well for what they happen to be good at. Nozick has a real reply here — that a thing needn't be deserved all the way down for it to be yours, that you don't have to have earned your hands to own the painting you made with them — and it's a better reply than Rawls's followers usually concede. But it doesn't get anywhere near the claim it needs. It might establish that you may keep what your gifts produce. It does nothing to establish that the arrangement which decides how enormously those particular gifts get rewarded, and which faces you had to be born with to have them noticed, is beyond question. The lottery has no moral authority. A society that simply transmits it, unexamined, hasn't done justice; it has just got out of the way.

Third: Nozick landed one blow that is permanent, and anybody on the redistributive side who hasn't felt it isn't paying attention. There's no such thing as costless equality. Every pattern you want to maintain has to be maintained *against* the free choices of actual people, continuously, and those people are owed an answer — not the aggregate, not the average, not the deserving poor in the abstract. The individual woman whose extra hours are being drawn on is owed a justification in her own person. You cannot invoke the inviolability of the individual on Monday to demolish the utilitarian sum and then treat her as a line in the national accounts on Tuesday. If your egalitarianism can't look her in the face and explain itself, it hasn't answered its own premise. Rawls, to his enormous credit, understood that — it's why he built the original position in the first place, so that the justification would be one she could have accepted before she knew who she'd be.

And where I actually stand, since I've asked you to stand somewhere: closer to Rawls. Not because the veil produces certainty — I don't think the reasoning behind it delivers the difference principle with the tightness he claimed, and I said so. Because of the pre-institutional property assumption, which is where I think Nozick's case has its weakest joint. His whole argument needs there to be a fact about what is yours that exists prior to and independent of the political order, so that taxation is a subtraction from something already settled. And I don't believe there is one. Your holdings are not a natural object. They are the output of a scheme of rules — property, contract, inheritance, currency, courts — and once that's true, the question of what the rules should be cannot be answered by pointing at the results of the rules you happen to have. Add the unbuilt rectification wing, and the entitlement theory looks to me like something other than what it's usually taken for: a superb critique, and not a blueprint.

But here's the fourth thing, and it's why this series ends without a winner declared.

The master question is not settled, and I'm not going to pretend a verdict I don't have. That's not a dodge, and I'd rather defend it than have you take it for one. The reason the argument doesn't close is that both men *are working from the same premise*. This isn't a case of one side seeing something the other is blind to. They agree, completely, on the thing that matters most: that you are not a portion of something larger, that your life is yours, that no total however glorious licenses spending you. Then they discover that this single shared conviction points in two directions — protect the person from the arbitrariness of fate, or protect the person from the arrangements that would correct it — and there is no third premise, held by both, that can break the tie. That's not a gap in the literature. That's the shape of the problem.

And a tradition twenty-four centuries long that can't resolve its central question is not thereby a failure. Look what it *has* done. Thrasymachus said there was no such thing as justice, only the interest of the stronger with a robe on. Everything since has been an attempt to escape that conclusion, and the fact that we're still arguing about *which* escape works — rather than whether there's anything to escape from — is the measure of how far the argument got.

## What waits past the veil   {tone: contemplative}

We stopped at 1974, and it was a choice, so let me say what it cost.

Two men. Both American, both writing in English, both at the same university, both taking for granted a modern constitutional state with a market economy, an individual rights-holder as the basic unit, and reason as the court of appeal. Their disagreement is real and deep — and it takes place inside a room, and the room has walls.

There are people standing just outside it who would say the argument we've been having is the wrong argument.

Edmund Burke, who'd have looked at the original position — a man in a chair, reasoning his way to a constitution from first principles with everything actual stripped away — and said that this is precisely the intellectual vice that soaks a country in blood; that societies are inherited, not designed, and abstract reason applied to living institutions is a wrecking ball held by someone who thinks it's a scalpel.

Mary Wollstonecraft and Thomas Paine, who'd ask about the walls. The whole grand tradition of consent and rights and the free individual was, for most of its history, written by men who did not mean it to include their wives, their servants, or the people on the other side of the water — and it took people standing outside the door to point out that the door was there.

Hannah Arendt, who'd say that politics has been reduced here to the distribution of goods, as though citizens were claimants at a counter; that the veil takes away everything that makes a human being *particular*, and politics is what happens when particular people appear before each other and act — and you can't get that back from a theory of shares.

Isaiah Berlin, who'd want to know which freedom we've been talking about all along: freedom as being left alone, or freedom as self-mastery, the two of them not merely different but capable of ending in opposite places — one of them, historically, in a cell.

Michel Foucault, who'd go after the tool itself, and ask what power is hiding inside the word "reason" — whose norms get called rationality, who gets classified, examined, corrected, and by what authority the examiners examine.

And Frantz Fanon, who'd point out that the whole liberal tradition sat on top of an empire, and that the freedoms being carefully partitioned in the seminar were financed by people who had no vote in the matter, and that what the colonised faced was not a question about the fair distribution of primary goods.

Every one of those is a real argument with a real thinker behind it, and none of them has been made here. I'm naming them as a promise, not as coverage — that's a different set of episodes, and they deserve their own.

But you should hear what it means that they're all standing outside the door. The long argument doesn't end here. It gets louder.

## Two tickets, again   {tone: measured, note: "bookend; return to the room, land it, hand it over"}

So: back to the room with no mirrors. You've still got the pen.

Nothing has changed and everything has. The commission is the same, and your ignorance about yourself is the same, and the document still binds everyone forever with no appeal.

What's different is that you now know what you're holding.

There are two tickets in this story, and the whole quarrel is about which one is decisive.

The first is the one you were handed. You didn't buy it, didn't choose it, and it had your body on it, your brain, your parents, your century, your talents and the market price of your talents. Rawls's entire life's work is the claim that a ticket nobody earned cannot be allowed to write anybody's fate — that the arbitrary has no authority, that justice is what we'd agree to before we saw our numbers, and that a society is to be judged by how it treats the people who drew the worst cards.

The second is a quarter, dropped in a box by somebody who wanted to watch a man play basketball. Freely. Knowingly. With money that was hers. Nozick's entire case is that the second ticket is sacred too — that a society which has to keep confiscating that quarter, or forbidding it, to hold a shape somebody prefers is not respecting persons, it's arranging them; and that if you take seriously that each of us has one separate life, you have to take seriously that she gets to spend hers, including the hours she worked for that quarter.

Both tickets are real. But real is not the same as equal, and I told you a few minutes ago where I came down — I'm not going to soften it now just to sound even-handed. The first ticket has the better claim. The second one leans on a single word — *mine* — that turns out to mean nothing until a society has already built the rules of property that decide it; and you cannot end an argument about what those rules should be by pointing at a holding the argument is about. Rawls has the better of it. I'd rather tell you that than leave you with a tie I don't believe in.

What I am *not* claiming to have closed is the older question the two of them *share*. Both start from the same conviction — that you are not a means, that no total, however glorious, licenses spending you — and that single premise is exactly what drives them apart. So the veil stays down — not over Rawls and Nozick, where I've placed my bet, but over the thing underneath them that this whole series has been circling: the standard by which any arrangement of power is rightful in the first place. Twenty-four centuries haven't closed that one, and the thinkers waiting past the door are about to pry it back open. *That* is the argument that stays alive — and a series that tied it off to sound finished would have been lying to you on the way out.

Which leaves it with you, and that's not a rhetorical flourish — it's the literal situation. You live in a society whose basic structure was designed by people who could see their own cards. Every tax rate, every inheritance rule, every school catchment area, every question about what the state may take and what it must guarantee, is a live vote on the argument you've just heard. You will express an opinion on it this year, whether you mean to or not. The only question is whether you'll do it with a position you could defend to the person on the other side of it.

Both of them died in 2002, in the same corner of Massachusetts where they'd spent thirty years disagreeing. Neither convinced the other.

So the pen's yours, and I'll leave you where the whole thing started, twenty-three episodes and twenty-four centuries ago, with the question Thrasymachus threw down and nobody has managed to pick up cleanly since. When does power become authority — and by what standard?

You're behind the veil. You don't know who you'll be.

Write the rules. {pause: 1.5}
