## The drawing   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "cold open on an object, described flatly and precisely; admiration first; no thinker named, no doctrine, no second-person address until the very end of the beat"}

There is a drawing.

It was made in the seventeen-nineties, in England, and it shows a building that was never built. The drawing is beautiful, in the cold way that engineering drawings are beautiful — a plan and a section, ruled lines, careful hatching, everything to scale.

The building is a circle.

Around the outer wall, stacked in tiers, run the cells. Each cell goes clean through the thickness of the wall, so it has a window on the outside — which means daylight comes in from behind the occupant and throws him forward as a silhouette. Each cell is open on the inside face, barred, looking inward across a well of empty air.

And at the centre of that circle stands a tower. A lodge, the drawing calls it. From the lodge, a single person can see into every cell in the building at once.

Now the refinement, which is the whole invention. The lodge is fitted with blinds and screens and baffled entrances, so that whoever is inside it cannot be seen from the cells. Not dimly seen. Not seen at a distance. *Not seen at all.* The man in the cell looks at the tower and sees a shuttered dark. He cannot tell whether anyone is in there. He cannot tell whether the eye is on him at this moment or resting on the man four cells down. He cannot tell whether the lodge is occupied at all.

So he must assume it always is. {pause: 1.0}

That is the point. The designer understood, better than anyone before him, that you do not need to actually watch a man every hour of his life in order to obtain the effect of watching him. You need only to make it impossible for him to know that you aren't. The uncertainty does the work, continuously, for free, while the guard sleeps. The designer's own phrase for what his building achieved was this: a new mode of obtaining power of mind over mind, in a quantity hitherto without example.

He called it the Panopticon. The all-seeing place.

And in the paper he wrote advertising it, he laid out what it would deliver, in a rising line, like a man selling a patent medicine: morals reformed, health preserved, industry invigorated, instruction diffused, public burdens lightened — all, he said, by a simple idea in architecture.

He spent something like twenty years of his life trying to get it built.

## The most humane sentence anyone ever wrote   {tone: curious, note: "the paradox; the through-line question; do not resolve"}

Now the thing that makes this episode necessary.

The man who drew that building is the same man who gave the English language a phrase you have almost certainly used, or heard used, and probably approved of without a second's hesitation.

*The greatest happiness of the greatest number.*

Same pen. Same decade. Same brain. He is not a jailer who happened to dabble in philosophy — he is one of the great reformers of the modern world, the man behind a wave of British legal reform, an enemy of the death penalty, an enemy of cruelty in every institution he could get his hands on, and, as we'll see, the author of arguments so far ahead of his time that they could not be printed for a hundred and fifty years.

And out of that same mind came a machine for making a human being feel watched at every hour of his existence.

That is not a coincidence, and it is not a scandalous little footnote. It is the key to the whole thing. Understand *why* the philosophy of maximum happiness produced the blueprint for total surveillance, and you will understand what is genuinely powerful in this idea, and what is genuinely dangerous in it, and why we still cannot get free of either.

So the question in front of us. Every thinker in this series has answered the same challenge — what makes a government's authority legitimate? — and we have had gods and nature and covenants and consent and reason and spirit and class. This tradition sweeps the lot away and offers one standard, and it is a standard you can *count*. Judge every law, every institution, every act of state by one test: how much happiness does it produce, and for how many people?

Should we? {pause: 1.0}

And if the answer is anything other than an immediate yes or an immediate no, there's a second question waiting behind it, and it's worse. *Can* happiness be measured? Can my suffering be weighed against your delight and the two of them added together into a number that a Parliament could act on?

Last episode a stateless man in a reading room told us that all our talk of rights and consent was paint on a house. This episode brings you the other great demolition of that talk — from a completely different direction, with completely different tools, arriving at the same place by a road nobody expected.

## The hermit of Queen's Square Place   {tone: measured, note: "Bentham's human anchor; the oddity of the life; concrete"}

Jeremy Bentham was born in London in 1748, the son of a prosperous attorney, and he was one of those children who frighten the adults around them. Latin at three or four. Queen's College, Oxford, at the age of twelve. A bachelor's degree at fifteen. Trained for the bar, as his father intended, and then — the swerve that made him — he refused to practise.

He had sat in court and listened to English law being administered, and what he heard was not majesty. What he heard was a monstrous accumulation of jargon, fees, delay, fictions and rubbish, maintained by men who profited from every hour of it. His father wanted him to make his fortune in that system. He decided instead to spend his life attacking it.

And attack it he did, in a way no one had before: not by arguing that this or that statute was unjust, but by asking what the *whole apparatus* was for.

He lived most of his adult life in a house in Westminster, in a routine of near-monastic strangeness. He walked, at speed, in his garden. He wrote. He wrote and wrote and wrote — on law, punishment, evidence, prisons, poverty, colonies, religion, language, logic, sex, the constitution of Greece, the constitution of Portugal. The bulk of it he never published; a great deal of it he never finished. His manuscripts at University College London run to something like sixty thousand folios, boxes of them unread for well over a century, and scholars there are *still* transcribing them today, volume after volume, two hundred years on.

He gathered around himself a circle of disciples who took his ideas out into the world and made policy out of them — the Philosophic Radicals, who wanted universal suffrage, annual parliaments, the secret ballot, and the abolition of most of what England considered its glorious constitution.

He is one of the strangest men in this series and one of the most consequential. And he built everything he built on a single idea that a bright child could grasp in ten seconds.

## The child's question   {tone: lucid, note: "INTUITION BEFORE THEORY — the Bloody Code and Blackstone; make the listener feel the power of Bentham's question before it is named"}

To feel why the idea landed like a bomb, you have to hear what it was fired at.

Eighteenth-century England had a criminal law that historians would later call the Bloody Code. Over the course of that century the number of offences carrying the death penalty climbed into the hundreds. Not hundreds of kinds of murder — hundreds of *offences*. Stealing goods above a modest value from a shop. Poaching. Being out at night with a blackened face. Damaging a fishpond. Cutting down an ornamental tree. Forgery, which was hanged with particular enthusiasm because it threatened the credit of trade.

In practice, juries fiddled the valuations to save people's necks, and judges recommended mercy, and many of the condemned were transported instead. But the machinery stood, and it was used, and children were hanged under it.

Now, put yourself in front of the great legal mind of that England: Sir William Blackstone, whose lectures on the laws of England were the most admired legal work of the age — and whose lectures the young Bentham sat and listened to at Oxford.

Ask Blackstone: why is it lawful to hang a man for this?

And you will get, in the most elegant prose imaginable, an answer assembled out of the materials this series has been collecting for fifteen episodes. Because it is the ancient custom of the realm. Because the common law has grown from immemorial usage and carries the accumulated reason of generations. Because the law of England conforms to the law of nature and the revealed law of God. Because the constitution is a delicate inheritance in which each part supports the others, and the wisdom of our ancestors is not to be lightly disturbed by the speculations of a clever young man.

Bentham listened to all of that, and he did not argue with it on its own terms. That refusal is the whole of his originality. He didn't produce a rival reading of the ancient constitution or a better account of natural law.

He asked a question so simple it sounds like a child interrupting.

*What good does it do?* {pause: 0.8}

Who is happier because this man is hanged? Who is safer? By how much? Compare that against what it costs — his terror, his death, his family's ruin, the coarsening of the crowd that watches. Put the two amounts side by side. If the killing produces more misery than it prevents, then it does not matter how ancient it is, how many judges have blessed it, or how beautifully Blackstone writes about it.

It is a stupid rule and it should be abolished. {pause: 0.6}

Feel what that question does. It cuts straight through the entire fog. Custom, precedent, dignity, the wisdom of the ancestors, the mystery of the common law — none of it is *evidence*. You cannot answer "what good does it do" by saying "we have always done it." Bentham later said that his purpose was to lay the axe to the root, and this is the axe. One question, asked of everything, by anyone, without qualification and without needing a lawyer's licence to ask it.

## The two masters   {tone: lucid, note: "NAME AS PAYOFF — the principle of utility; define plainly; short and clean"}

That question has a name, and a doctrine standing behind it.

Bentham's great book — the one he actually finished — is *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, published in 1789, the year the Bastille fell, though he had had it printed and sitting about for most of the previous decade, which tells you something about the man. It opens with a sentence that functions like the first line of a scripture:

Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do.

Two claims are stacked in there, and it's worth pulling them apart.

The first is about how people actually behave: we are moved by pleasure and pain, and by nothing else. Every motive you have ever had reduces to seeking one and avoiding the other.

The second is about what is actually *good*: pleasure is the only thing good in itself, and pain the only thing bad in itself, and everything else — money, liberty, justice, virtue, this building, that law — is good or bad only as a means to those.

From which follows the **principle of utility**. And "utility" here is not the modern office word meaning usefulness. Bentham defines it: the property in any object whereby it tends to produce benefit, advantage, good, or happiness — or, on the other side, to prevent mischief, pain, or unhappiness. The principle of utility says: approve or disapprove of every action whatsoever, and above all every action of government, according to its tendency to increase or diminish the happiness of the parties whose interest is in question.

That's it. That is the entire foundation.

Notice what has just been thrown out of the building. There is no natural law here — no order of the cosmos telling us what is right, as Cicero had it in Episode 4 and Aquinas in Episode 6. There is no covenant, no state of nature, no original contract that binds us because our ancestors are imagined to have signed it. There is no general will. There is no rational will legislating for itself, as Kant had it three episodes ago — and remember that Kant's whole insistence was that the state must *not* try to make you happy, that it must secure your freedom and let your happiness be your own business. Bentham reverses that completely and without embarrassment. Happiness is the *only* business. There is nothing else for a government to be for.

## Nonsense upon stilts   {tone: wry, note: "the demolition of rights; genealogy back through Hume/Beccaria; the Marx rhyme"}

And there are no rights.

I mean that flatly, because he meant it flatly. When the French revolutionaries published their Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen — natural, imprescriptible rights, held by every human being simply as a human being — Bentham took it apart clause by clause in a document dripping with contempt, and produced the sentence he is most quoted for:

Natural rights is simple nonsense. Natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense — nonsense upon stilts.

His argument is not sneering, though the delivery is. It's this. A right is a creature of law. Where there is a legal system that will actually protect your possession of a thing, you have a right to it; that is what the word *means*. To say you have a right that no law recognizes is to say you have a promise from nobody, enforceable by nobody. It is a wish dressed in a uniform. And dangerous, in his view — because once every man can announce that his private preferences are natural rights, argument stops. You can't weigh a right. You can't measure it. It just sits there and refuses.

Whereas you can always ask: what good does it do? {pause: 0.6}

Hold that next to last episode. Marx said the rights of man were rights of separation, an ideology thrown up by a particular economy to make its transactions look like freedom. Bentham says they're gibberish, because rights come from statutes and statutes come from legislatures. Two men who would have agreed on almost nothing else, coming at the same target from opposite sides, both concluding that the noblest vocabulary of the modern world is a fiction.

Except that Bentham has a much older lineage than that makes it sound, and this series' oldest lesson holds here too — nobody thinks from nowhere.

Go back to Episode 12, to David Hume, who demolished the social contract and argued that justice is an artificial virtue, a convention we maintain because of its *usefulness* to society. Bentham read Hume's *Treatise* as a young man and said afterwards that the scales fell from his eyes. The phrase "the greatest happiness of the greatest number" was not his either — versions of it were already floating in the eighteenth century, in Francis Hutcheson in Scotland and in the Italian penal reformer Cesare Beccaria, whose great little book against torture and the death penalty Bentham revered. What Bentham did was take a moral sentiment other people had expressed and turn it into a *machine* — something you could actually operate.

Which brings us to the strangest and most revealing thing he ever built.

## Seven dimensions   {tone: curious, note: "DWELL — the felicific calculus, taught on one small running example; charm first, concede that Bentham offered it as a standard not a desk procedure, then the political turn"}

If pleasure and pain are the only things that ultimately count, and if the job of a legislator is to produce more of the one and less of the other, then a legislator needs to be able to *measure* them. Otherwise the principle is a sentiment, not a method — and Bentham had no interest in sentiments.

So he sat down and worked out how to measure a pleasure.

He called it the felicific calculus, sometimes the hedonic calculus, and it has seven dimensions. Let's take them one at a time on something small, and I'll keep the same small thing in play for the rest of the episode, so hold onto it.

You have an evening free. Two options in front of you. Option one: go out and play a game with friends — a few hours of easy, laughing, entirely uncomplicated fun. Option two: stay in with a difficult book of poetry that you have been meaning to get through, which will be hard going for an hour and then, possibly, will open up.

Bentham says: measure them. Like this.

**Intensity.** How strong is the pleasure? Not how worthy, not how respectable. How *strong*.

**Duration.** How long does it last?

**Certainty.** How likely is it to happen at all? A guaranteed small pleasure can outrank a doubtful large one.

**Propinquity** — his word for nearness. How soon? Pleasure tonight counts for more than the same pleasure a year off, because a year off it may never arrive.

**Fecundity.** How fertile is it — how likely is this pleasure to be followed by more pleasures of the same kind? An evening that leaves you with a taste for the thing scores higher than one that leaves you flat.

**Purity.** How likely is it to be followed by pain? The night out that ends in a hangover and an argument is an impure pleasure. The mark isn't against the fun. It's against the wreckage.

And then the seventh, which is a different animal entirely, and I'll come back to it in a moment.

Six dimensions. Score each option on each, add and subtract, and take the bigger number. Bentham was so serious about this that he set the list to verse, so that a working legislator could memorise it — *intense, long, certain, speedy, fruitful, pure* — the sort of doggerel a man writes when he genuinely expects clerks in a government office to be using his system by Thursday.

Though in fairness to him, he said the opposite himself, in plain prose, and it matters: it is not to be expected, he wrote, that this process should be strictly pursued previously to every moral judgment. He is not issuing a form to be filled in. He offers the calculus as the standard that practice should *approximate to* — the thing your rough judgment is a rough version of. Which is a considerably harder position to knock over, as we'll see.

Because your first reaction is probably that this is absurd. That nobody can put a number on an evening. That the whole thing has the air of a very clever man who has never in his life simply enjoyed something.

Hold that reaction. It's the right instinct and we're going to need it. But notice, before you spend it, that you have in fact just been doing exactly what he described. When you weighed the night out against the book, you *did* think about how much fun, for how long, how sure you were of it, and what state you'd be in tomorrow. He hasn't invented a foreign procedure. He's written down, in order, the one you already run — and then insisted that a government should be made to run it out loud, in public, where anyone can check the arithmetic.

## The seventh   {tone: lucid, note: "extent — the hinge where the calculus becomes politics; each to count for one; the democratic dynamite"}

The seventh dimension is called **extent**, and it means: how many people are affected?

That single word is where a theory of personal choice turns into a theory of government, and it is worth stopping on, because everything difficult in the rest of this episode comes out of it.

The first six dimensions weigh a pleasure. Extent *sums* pleasures — across persons. It says: when you're deciding a public question, don't stop at your own ledger. Count everyone the sum touches, run the calculus for each of them, and add it all up.

And now the crucial rider, which is the moral core of the whole tradition. When you count them — every single one of them counts *the same*. Mill would later report the doctrine as a dictum of Bentham's, in words that have stuck to him ever since: everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one.

That sounds mild. In 1789 it was dynamite. {pause: 0.8}

Because consider what the alternative was. This is a society organized top to bottom by rank: a duke, a bishop, a gentleman, a freeholder, a labourer, a pauper. The whole architecture of English politics assumed that some people's interests simply *weighed more* — that they had a stake in the country and others did not, that a peer's estate was a matter of national importance and a weaver's family was not. Every argument for the existing distribution of power ran through some version of that.

Bentham's calculus doesn't recognize rank at all. It cannot. It has no slot for it. A duke's pain is one unit of pain. A pauper's pain is one unit of pain. There is no dimension in the list called *quality of the person* — and there is nowhere in the arithmetic where you could smuggle one in.

Which is why the utilitarians, for all their strangeness, ended up on the radical side of nearly every fight of their century. If everyone's happiness counts equally, then a Parliament elected by a tiny sliver of property-holders cannot possibly be tracking the greatest happiness of the greatest number, and the vote has to be widened — Bentham came to argue for something close to universal male suffrage and the secret ballot, which in his lifetime was the position of a dangerous extremist.

## What the arithmetic could see   {tone: warm, note: "STEELMAN the calculus at its strongest, with real cases; the listener must be genuinely persuaded before the complication"}

And before we take the machine apart, let me show you what it could see that nobody else could.

Start with punishment, since that's where he started. Bentham writes, in the middle of the great book: all punishment is mischief; all punishment in itself is evil. Not "punishment is regrettable." *Evil* — because punishment is the deliberate infliction of suffering, and suffering is the one thing that is bad in itself. So on his principle, punishment can only ever be justified as the purchase of a greater good: it is admissible exactly so far as it excludes some greater evil, and not one lash further.

Run that against the Bloody Code and it is a wrecking ball. Retribution — the idea that a wrong simply *deserves* an answering pain, quite apart from any good the pain does — has no standing in the theory at all. Neither does the majesty of the law. Every gallows must now show its working.

Then take a harder case, and this is the one that shows you the reach of the thing.

Around the seventeen-eighties, Bentham wrote an essay on the criminal punishment of consensual sex between men — which in the England of his day was a capital offence, and men were hanged for it. He ran his question over it. Where is the harm? Who is made unhappy? He examined every justification on offer — that it was unnatural, that it offended God, that it weakened the nation, that it threatened marriage — and found each of them to be either empty or unsupported by any evidence of actual suffering. And he concluded that the law was producing enormous misery and preventing none, and that it should go.

He did not publish it. He was not a brave man in that particular way, and he knew exactly what would happen to him. The essay sat in his papers, unprinted, until the second half of the twentieth century — by which time the argument he had made alone in a room in Westminster had finally become the law of the country.

And one more, from a footnote — perhaps the most famous footnote in the history of moral philosophy. Bentham asks what qualifies a creature for moral consideration at all, and he answers that the question is not whether they can reason, nor whether they can talk, but whether they can *suffer*. Which is the founding sentence of the modern argument about animals, written by a man in the seventeen-eighties, and it follows with total inevitability from the principle: if pain is what's bad, then pain is what's bad, and the species of the creature feeling it is not one of the seven dimensions either.

That is the tradition at its best. One question, asked without fear or favour, dissolving cruelties that had been protected for centuries by nothing but the dignity of their age. If you have ever heard someone defend a practice by saying that it's traditional, and felt your patience go — you are Bentham's heir, whether or not you'd like to be.

So. Everyone counts for one. Maximize the total. It's clean, it's countable, it's democratic, it's merciless with cant.

Now let's build the prison. {pause: 1.0}

## The machine in the yard   {tone: grave, note: "COMPLICATE — the Panopticon as the calculus made of brick; the failure; the real objection surfaces from inside the theory"}

Because the Panopticon is not a betrayal of that principle. It is the principle, executed.

Look at the problem as Bentham found it. England in the seventeen-nineties disposed of its criminals by hanging them, by transporting them to the other side of the world, or by keeping them in gaols and rotting prison hulks where they were held in filth, mixed together, unfed unless they could pay, and where a sentence of two years was quite frequently a sentence of death by fever.

Now run the calculus on that. It is a catastrophe on every dimension. Enormous pain, wide extent, no reformative fecundity whatsoever — you put a petty thief in and take a hardened criminal out — and monstrous expense to the public on top of it.

Against which, the circular building. Nobody is hanged. Nobody is shipped to Australia. The inmates are clean, fed, healthy, sober, kept apart from the corruption of other prisoners, taught a trade, made to work at it, and released with a skill. Bentham proposed to make it self-financing, and to run it himself as contractor-governor, with his own income tied by contract to the outcomes: he would be paid according to the labour produced, and *penalised* for every prisoner who died on his watch. Read that again — he designed a system in which the keeper's profit rose and fell with his prisoners' health, because he did not trust benevolence and did trust arithmetic.

And how is the whole thing supervised, at a fraction of the cost of a conventional gaol? By the geometry. By the invisible eye in the lodge, and the apparent omnipresence of an inspector who might, at any moment, be looking at you.

Score it. Against the hulks, it wins on every dimension, and it isn't close. It is more humane, more effective, and cheaper.

He was right about that. {pause: 0.8}

So what, exactly, is wrong with it?

Sit in the cell for a moment and try to say. You are not being beaten. You are not starving. You are not going to die of gaol fever. You will come out with a trade. And yet something is being taken from you, continuously, every waking hour — something for which the calculus has no column.

The state of never being unobserved. The absence of any moment that is yours. The condition of always performing, because you can never confirm that no one is watching, and so you must behave, always, as though someone is. The building does not discipline your body. It reaches inside and installs the guard in your own head, permanently, and lets you carry the cost of your own supervision. That is precisely what its designer promised it would do: power of mind over mind.

Now, could you get that into the ledger? Possibly — you could call it a pain and try to weigh it. But here is what makes the theory frightening rather than merely incomplete: whether the objection survives depends entirely on the *size of the number.* If the eye in the tower is unpleasant but the savings are large and the reform rate is high and the alternative is a hanging, then the sums come out in favour, and there is nothing in the theory — nothing whatsoever — that stands up at that point and says *even so, no.* No right to be unobserved. No dignity that a person carries and a legislature may not touch. Only a total, and whichever way the total falls.

The building was never built, incidentally. Bentham lobbied for it for the better part of twenty years; Parliament passed an act and voted money; a site was pursued near the Thames at Millbank; and then it died in the way these things die — landowners, delays, ministers who stopped answering letters, a government at war with France and thinking about other matters. In the end he was bought off with a large compensation payment, something over twenty thousand pounds, which was a fortune and no consolation at all. He believed to the end of his life that the King had personally strangled it, and he never forgave anyone involved. A prison was eventually built on the ground he had wanted. Not his.

But the idea outlived the drawing by a very long way. A century and a half later a French philosopher named Michel Foucault made the Panopticon the emblem of the whole modern condition — the argument that we no longer need the tower, because the watching has been distributed everywhere and the guard is now inside all of us. That is an argument for another series and another episode. What matters here is that he did not have to distort Bentham to make the point. Bentham drew it, described exactly what it would do to the mind of the man inside, and considered the description a selling point.

## Can it even be added up?   {tone: measured, note: "the measurement objection, honestly; then the modern stakes — this is done to you now"}

There's a second objection, and it's the one you probably had first, back at the seven dimensions. Not *should* we sum happiness. *Can* we?

Take it seriously, because it's harder than it looks in both directions.

The problem isn't measuring my own pleasures against each other; roughly, I can do that. The problem is measuring *across people*. I feel my pain. I do not feel yours. There is no instrument anywhere that can register that your grief is nine units and my inconvenience is three, and therefore that the law should fall on the side of your grief. The two quantities are locked inside two separate skulls with no channel between them, and the addition sign Bentham puts between them is doing work that nothing in the world underwrites.

And there's a nastier version. Whose pleasures? If a large majority takes real, intense, durable satisfaction in the humiliation of a small minority — and history says they can and do — then those pleasures are units like any others, and they go into the sum on the positive side. The calculus has no filter for the *content* of a pleasure. It only counts.

That's a serious objection and Bentham never fully answered it. Bentham, to his enormous credit, saw the shape of it himself — he worried, later in life, about his own most famous slogan. "The greatest happiness of the greatest number" contains two different targets that can come apart: the greatest *total* happiness, and the happiness of the greatest *number of people*. And he noticed that the formula, taken literally, could license the sacrifice of a minority whenever the majority's gain was large enough. He fretted about it. He fiddled with the wording. He did not solve it, because it cannot be solved from inside the theory — the theory is a summing machine, and a summing machine does not care where the sum comes from.

And yet. {pause: 0.6}

Before you dismiss the whole enterprise as arithmetic that can't be done — your government did it this morning.

Every health service on earth that decides which treatments to fund is trading years of one person's life against years of another's, on a scale, with a number attached. Every transport department that decides where the road goes, every regulator setting the permissible level of a pollutant, every treasury weighing a lockdown against an economy — all of them are running some descendant of the felicific calculus, usually with a spreadsheet and always with a figure that quietly represents a human life. They may not use his vocabulary. They are doing his procedure. And notice that they are doing it the way he actually recommended it — not as a form filled in before every decision, but as the standard the decision is supposed to approximate.

That is Bentham's real monument. Not a building. The cost-benefit analysis. He is the reason a modern state believes it owes you a *reason* in the form of a consequence, and the reason that reason so often arrives shaped like a number.

Which means the objection you're holding isn't an academic one. When the sum says your village gets the runway, you are the minority in somebody's calculus, and the only question that matters is whether there is anything you can say back to it other than *my number is bigger than you think.*

That is exactly the hole that the second man in this episode spent his life trying to fill. And to understand what he did, you have to know what was done to him.

## The experiment   {tone: measured, note: "James Mill and the education; keep it factual and cold; the boy as the doctrine's vessel"}

James Mill was a Scot who came south — a hard, formidable, self-made man of letters who made his living by writing and editing, and who produced a monumental history of British India. He entered the East India Company's India House in 1819 and spent the last seventeen years of his life there, in the correspondence of empire, until his death in 1836. Somewhere around 1808 he had fallen in with Bentham, become his closest disciple and his great organizer, and adopted the principle of utility as the total framework of his life.

If it was true, it was true of everything. Including child-rearing.

His eldest son, John Stuart Mill, born in 1806, was raised as a demonstration. Not a metaphor — a demonstration, deliberately undertaken, to show what the correct education could produce and thereby to prove a doctrine about human nature: that the mind is formed by what is put into it, and that if you controlled what was put in, you could manufacture a superior instrument.

Greek at three. The boy is learning vocabulary cards before he can properly read English. Aesop, Herodotus, and then the whole Greek historical library through childhood. Latin at eight, at which point he begins teaching it to his younger siblings, because the household is also a school and he is also the staff. Logic at twelve. Political economy at thirteen, taken as a course of instruction from his father on their walks, where the boy is required to give an account of what he has read and be corrected on it, point by point.

No school. Almost no other children — his father deliberately kept him away from boys his own age, to prevent contamination by ordinary schoolboy stupidity. No religion. And, by John's own later account, almost no praise, because praise was not thought useful.

He was extraordinary. By his late teens he was editing Bentham's manuscripts for publication, writing for the reviews, founding debating societies, running the machinery of a political movement. He described the moment he first read Bentham's doctrine — he came to it in a French edition assembled by Bentham's editor Dumont — as the arrival of a religion. It gave him a creed, a doctrine, a philosophy, an object in life. He knew what everything was for.

And when he was twenty years old, in the autumn of 1826, the whole thing came down.

## Autumn, 1826   {tone: somber, rate: slow, note: "the crisis; use Mill's own account; do not sentimentalize; this is the hinge of the episode"}

He calls the chapter of his autobiography "A Crisis in My Mental History," and what he describes is unmistakably a depression — a state in which nothing whatever gave pleasure, in which the things that had moved him left him unmoved, in which he went about his work and his family and felt nothing at all.

And it began, he says, with a question he put to himself. He was in a dull state, and he asked himself directly:

Suppose that all your objects in life were realized. Suppose that all the changes in institutions and opinions that you are looking forward to could be completely effected, at this very instant. Would this be a great joy and happiness to you?

And an irrepressible self-consciousness, he writes, distinctly answered: *No.* {pause: 1.2}

Sit with what that means for the man it happened to. He is twenty. He has been built, from the age of three, as an instrument for a single purpose: the reform of the world in the direction of greater happiness. And he discovers, by direct inspection, that the achievement of that purpose would not make *him* happy. The end for which the whole machine was constructed turns out to be empty at the moment of arrival.

It lasted months. He got no help from the philosophy — his own analysis told him that the pleasures had been drained out of things by the very habit of analysis, that his education had trained the dissecting faculty to such a pitch that it had worn away the feelings it was supposed to serve. He had been made, he thought, into a reasoning machine, and machines do not want anything.

What broke it was not an argument. He was reading a French memoir — Marmontel's — and came to a passage where the writer's father dies, and the family are left destitute, and the boy, still a boy, suddenly feels that he can be everything to them, that he will take the father's place.

And Mill wept. {pause: 0.8}

And in the weeping he understood that he was not made of wood after all — that the capacity for feeling was still in him, damaged but alive. The recovery took time, and the thing that carried it was poetry: Wordsworth, above all, which he read in 1828, and which gave him what he had never been given — not new opinions, but the cultivation of feeling as an object in itself, a source of inward joy that had nothing to do with the improvement of anybody's condition.

Now. Two conclusions came out of that breakdown, and they are the whole of the rest of this episode.

The first he called the anti-self-consciousness theory, and it's a paradox worth naming: happiness, he decided, cannot be got by aiming at it. Those are happiest who have their minds fixed on some object other than their own happiness — on the good of others, on an art, on a pursuit followed not as a means but as an end in itself. Ask yourself whether you're happy, and you cease to be so. Which is a strange and destabilizing thing for a utilitarian to conclude, because it means the goal of the entire theory is a thing that vanishes when looked at directly.

The second was simpler and more consequential. His father's system, and Bentham's, had a hole in it exactly where the poetry goes. It could account for pleasure as *quantity*. It could not account for the difference between the states of a human soul.

So he set about fixing it — and the fix nearly broke the doctrine.

## Pushpin and poetry   {tone: lucid, note: "the revision; return to the free evening; Bentham's ACTUAL sentence, then the received formulation; define higher pleasures and the competent-judges test plainly"}

Back to your free evening. The game with friends, or the difficult book.

Bentham's position on that choice is not implied or reconstructed; he wrote it down. Pushpin, in his day, was a trivial pub game — a children's amusement played by adults with pins on a table, about as intellectually demanding as flicking beer mats.

His own sentence, from a book of his on rewards, is this: prejudice apart, the game of push-pin is of equal value with the arts and sciences of music and poetry.

The doctrine is usually summarised in a tighter form that he did not quite write — quantity of pleasure being equal, pushpin is as good as poetry — and that version has stuck, because it states his position exactly.

And you should hear it as the democratic boast it was meant to be, not as philistinism. He is saying: I refuse to tell you that your enjoyments are inferior. I have no throne to judge from. If the game gives you as much pleasure as the poem gives the gentleman in his library, then it is worth as much, and any legislator who spends public money elevating the gentleman's taste over yours is stealing from you to flatter himself. There is something genuinely noble in that, and it comes from exactly the same place as everybody-to-count-for-one.

Mill — who had been rescued from the pit by Wordsworth, and knew that a poem had done for him what no calculation could — said no.

His revision is set out in *Utilitarianism*, published in the early eighteen-sixties, and it is subtle, so let's take it slowly.

He does not abandon the principle. He restates it, faithfully: actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse; happiness is pleasure and the absence of pain.

Then he adds one sentence that changes everything. It is quite compatible with the principle of utility, he says, to recognise that some *kinds* of pleasure are more desirable and more valuable than others.

Kinds. Not amounts. He is claiming that pleasures differ in **quality**, and that a higher-quality pleasure can outrank a lower one *even if the lower one is larger in quantity* — even if it is more intense, longer, surer and purer, and would therefore win on every dimension of Bentham's calculus.

Which raises the obvious question, and Mill knows it's obvious: says who? By what standard do you rank one pleasure above another, when your entire philosophy says pleasure is the standard?

His answer is ingenious, and it is the piece worth remembering. He does not appeal to a scripture, or to nature, or to the good taste of educated persons. He appeals to a *poll*, with a strict rule about who is eligible to vote.

The only competent judges of two pleasures, he says, are the people who have genuinely experienced *both*. Not people who have heard about one. People who know both from the inside. And if all or almost all of those who have knowledge of both give a decided preference to one — a preference they hold irrespective of any feeling of moral obligation to prefer it, not because they think they ought to, but because they simply do — then that pleasure is the more desirable one, and its superiority is a fact, established the only way facts about pleasure can be established: by the testimony of those who have felt it.

And when you run that poll, Mill says, you find something striking. The traffic is one-way. Nobody who has developed the higher capacities consents to sink into the lower ones, not even for a large increase in contentment. No intelligent human being would agree to be turned into a fool, no person of feeling and conscience would agree to be made selfish and base, even on the promise of being perfectly satisfied with their lot afterwards.

Which produces his most famous line:

It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. And if the fool, or the pig, are of a different opinion, it is because they only know their own side of the question.

## Who appointed you judge?   {tone: tense, note: "STAGE THE CLASH — the Benthamite case against Mill, explicitly labelled as a reconstruction; italics only on the first and last sentence of each side; steelman both hard; do not resolve"}

Now put the two of them in a room, because this is a real fight and both of them can punch.

The Benthamite case first, and I'll give it its best form, because it's stronger than Mill's admirers like to admit. What follows is my reconstruction, not his words — Bentham was twenty-nine years dead when Mill published the revision. But it is his case, and he made every piece of it in print.

*You were taught Greek at three years old.* You were kept from other children so you would not learn their amusements. You were given the Athenian historians before you were given a toy. And now, in middle age, you have conducted a survey of everyone qualified to judge between poetry and pushpin, and — extraordinary result — the qualified judges turn out to be people exactly like you, and they have voted for the pleasures of people exactly like you.

Call it what it is. It is the oldest move in politics: the man at the top explains that the tastes of the people below him are lower tastes, and that they would agree with him if only they were capable of understanding. Every aristocracy in history has said this about every commoner it has ever ruled. I built a system with no seat in it for that argument, on purpose, and you have put the seat back and upholstered it.

And there is a worse problem than the snobbery. The test is not really a test of pleasure at all. When you ask whether a competent judge would consent to become a satisfied fool, and he says no — what is he refusing? Not less pleasure. He is refusing to be diminished. He is protecting something he values more than his own contentment: his mind, his character, what he is. That is a standard from outside pleasure. You have smuggled in a doctrine of the higher and lower nature of man — Plato's doctrine, fourteen episodes back — and you have painted "utility" on the crate. *Either pleasure is the measure, in which case quantity is all there is, or something else is the measure, in which case you are no longer one of us.*

That is a genuinely serious objection, and philosophers have been pressing it ever since. Mill wants both halves — the hard-headed hedonism and the dignity of the higher faculties — and it is not obvious the two can be held in one hand.

Now Mill's reply, which is also better than his critics allow, and which he does make himself, in his own text.

*The accusation would land if my test were about taste.* It is about experience. I have not said that the pleasures of the educated are superior; I have said that whenever we can find people who have lived on both sides of the line, they report, consistently, that they will not go back. That is not a prejudice, it is evidence — the only evidence there could be. And notice who is disqualified from voting: the connoisseur who has never played the game is as ineligible as the labourer who has never read the poem. My poll excludes the snob.

And the politics of this runs precisely the other way from the charge. What follows from my position? Not that the poor should be governed by their betters. It follows that the higher pleasures must be put within reach of every single person in this country — that we build the schools, the libraries, the institutes, that we shorten the working day so a man has an evening left to use, that we stop pretending a mill hand's capacity for Wordsworth is smaller than a duke's. The rival position, for all its egalitarian swagger, tells the labourer that pushpin is all he needs. *Mine says he is being robbed of half his nature and we are going to give it back to him.*

And there — {pause: 0.6} — is the honest shape of the disagreement. It isn't a snob against a democrat. It is two democrats disagreeing about what equality requires: equal respect for whatever people happen to enjoy, or equal access to what people could become.

I'll tell you where I land at the end. First, the other half of Mill's rescue — the part that reaches beyond pleasure entirely.

## The tyranny of the neighbours   {tone: measured, note: "INTUITION FIRST — the shift of the enemy from the state to opinion; Tocqueville NAMED here as someone the listener already knows"}

Consider what a person in a small English town in 1859 could be destroyed by, without a single law being broken.

Not going to church. Living with someone you are not married to. Holding an opinion about religion that you are prepared to say out loud. Leaving your husband. Any of those and your custom dries up, your children are not invited, the shop assistant is a fraction too slow to serve you, and you find that a whole town has quietly closed like a door — and there is no court in the land you can take that to, because nothing illegal has occurred.

Mill's great insight in *On Liberty* is that the danger to freedom had *changed address*.

Every thinker in this series so far has worried about power in the form of a person or an office: the tyrant, the king, the sovereign, the state. Fine, says Mill — but look at what happens once the people rule themselves. The old worry was that the government's interests might not be the people's interests, and democracy was supposed to solve that by making the rulers and the ruled the same body.

Except that the people who exercise the power are not the same as the people over whom it is exercised. The will of the people means, in practice, the will of the most numerous or the most active *part* of the people — the majority, or those who succeed in making themselves accepted as one. And a majority can oppress. That is what Mill means by the **tyranny of the majority.**

He did not invent the phrase, and he said so. He took it from a book about America by a French aristocrat you have met already in this series — Alexis de Tocqueville, whose two volumes Mill reviewed, twice, with enormous admiration, a quarter of a century before *On Liberty*. We will go back to him, and to why he thought Mill's fence would never be enough.

But Mill pushes the idea somewhere new. The tyranny he most fears is not the majority passing bad laws. It's the majority not needing to. Social tyranny, he says, is more formidable than many kinds of political oppression, because it leaves fewer means of escape — it penetrates much further into the details of life, and it enslaves the soul itself. A government can only reach your body and your property. Opinion reaches into what you dare to think, and it operates twenty-four hours a day, in your street, in your family, at your own table.

And this is what makes it a *utilitarian* problem rather than a change of subject. Mill has watched conformity from the inside; he knows what a mind looks like when it has been fully formed by other people's certainties, because he grew up in one. A society that succeeds in crushing eccentricity produces people who no longer have desires of their own — who ask themselves not "what do I prefer?" but "what is usually done by persons in my station?" And such people are not merely less free. They are less *capable of happiness*, because the machinery that generates happiness — wanting things, choosing, making a life that is recognizably yours — has been allowed to atrophy from disuse.

## The one sentence   {tone: lucid, note: "the harm principle in Mill's own words; the free-speech case; then the honest limits, including India"}

So *On Liberty*, published in 1859, does something no previous utilitarian had attempted. It draws a line and says: on this side, the collective may not go. Whatever the sums say.

The sentence is this, and it is worth hearing exactly, because almost everyone paraphrases it wrong:

That the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant.

That's the **harm principle**. And the second half of it is the radical half. Not "we may coerce you for good reasons and not bad ones" — but specifically that *your own good is not one of the reasons.* You cannot be compelled to do a thing because it would be better for you, or wiser, or healthier, or because it would make you a finer person. You may be reasoned with, argued with, pleaded with, entreated. You may not be compelled. Over himself, over his own body and mind, Mill says, the individual is sovereign.

And free speech is the case he presses hardest, with an argument that runs, roughly, on four legs: the silenced opinion may be true, and to deny that is to assume our own infallibility; if false, it very often contains a portion of truth we need; even a wholly true opinion, if it is never contested, decays into a dead dogma held as a formula, its grounds forgotten; and in that state it loses its power over conduct entirely. Which is why he pushes it to the limit case — if all mankind minus one were of one opinion, mankind would be no more justified in silencing that one person than that person, if he had the power, would be justified in silencing mankind.

Now the question that decides what this sentence is really worth. *Is it still utilitarianism?* Because it looks awfully like a right — a fence with a "no entry" sign on it, of exactly the kind Bentham called nonsense upon stilts.

Mill saw the objection and met it head-on, in the same chapter. He forgoes, he says, any advantage that could be derived to his argument from the idea of abstract right as a thing independent of utility. He regards utility as the ultimate appeal on all ethical questions — but it must be utility in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of a man as a progressive being.

Grasp what he's doing there and you have the whole strategy. He is not smuggling in natural rights. He is arguing that in the long run, over the permanent interests of a species that has to *develop*, a society that leaves individuals free to experiment with their own lives — to try things, to be odd, to be wrong in public — will discover more, correct itself faster, and produce more happiness than one that keeps everybody in line. Liberty isn't a barrier to the calculus. Liberty is what the calculus recommends, once you stop running it on this afternoon and start running it on centuries.

Whether that works is one of the great unsettled arguments in philosophy. Because if liberty is only justified by its long-run yield, then in principle a case could arise where the yield goes the other way — and Mill's fence comes down after all. His defenders read him as building the protection into the *rules* rather than case by case; his critics say a fence that stands only as long as the arithmetic favours it is not a fence. I'm not going to pretend that's resolved.

And two limits in his own text should be stated plainly, because they are in the same chapter as the noble sentence.

The first is reasonable: the principle applies to human beings in the maturity of their faculties. Children are excepted, and no one much objects.

The second is not reasonable, and it is not a footnote. Mill excludes what he calls backward states of society in which the race itself may be considered as in its nonage — and writes that despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians, provided the end be their improvement.

He was not being careless. John Stuart Mill spent thirty-five years as an official of the East India Company, the corporation that governed a subcontinent; he entered India House as a boy of seventeen, in his father's institution, and rose to the top of its correspondence department; he was writing the dispatches. The greatest defender of individual liberty in the English language earned his living administering an empire over people to whom, he argued, the principle did not yet apply.

You are allowed to hold both of those facts at once. That the harm principle is one of the most valuable sentences ever written, and that the man who wrote it carved out of it, in advance, the several hundred million people whose government paid his salary — as it had paid his father's. The carve-out is not a stain that spoils an otherwise clean argument. It is a demonstration of the thing this whole series keeps finding: that a thinker's circumstances get into the theory, right down at the level of who counts as a person in the maturity of his faculties.

## The woman on the title page   {tone: warm, note: "Harriet Taylor, honestly, with the attribution scope flagged as contested; the suffrage petition (1866) and the amendment (1867) kept distinct; Representative Government gets its one clause"}

One more presence in that book, and she is not incidental.

Mill met Harriet Taylor in 1830, when she was married to another man. What followed was a twenty-year intellectual partnership conducted in the full glare of Victorian disapproval, which cost them both dearly in a society that had precisely the powers of social punishment *On Liberty* describes. Her husband died in 1849; they married in 1851; she died in 1858, at Avignon, before *On Liberty* was published.

He dedicated it to her memory, and he did not hedge. He calls her the inspirer, and in part the author, of all that is best in his writings.

How literally should we take that? Honestly — nobody knows, and it has been argued about for a century and a half. One tradition treats it as the extravagance of a grieving husband; another has argued that her contribution was systematically written out of the record by scholars who could not credit it; and the manuscript evidence, where it exists, shows collaboration without settling the proportions. What is not in doubt is that Mill said it in print, repeatedly, over decades, and considered the liberal argument a joint production.

What is also not in doubt is where his own thinking went, and what he did with it when he got the chance. He was elected to Parliament in 1865 — having already published his case for what a decent constitution looks like, *Considerations on Representative Government*, four years earlier, a book that argued for a franchise extended to women and also, less comfortably, for giving the educated more than one vote each. Take that as the fight we just watched, in statutory form.

In June 1866 he rose in the House and presented a petition for women's suffrage, got up by a committee of women who had gathered nearly fifteen hundred signatures — the first time the demand had been laid on the table of the Commons as a serious parliamentary proposal.

Then, on the twentieth of May 1867, with the great Reform Bill going through, he moved his amendment: strike out the word "man" and put in the word "person." It was the first time the House of Commons ever divided on votes for women. He lost. One hundred and ninety-six against.

Seventy-three voted with him. {pause: 0.8}

Which nobody had expected, including him. And in 1869 he published *The Subjection of Women*, which argues that the legal subordination of one sex to the other is wrong in itself and ought to be replaced by a principle of perfect equality — and which reads, in places, exactly like the harm principle turned on the household.

## The hole that stays open   {tone: contemplative, note: "the sacrifice objection; Mill's own answer; the Rawls foreshadow without naming the argument"}

So: has Mill fixed it?

Return to the man in the circular cell, because the test of the repair is whether it protects him.

Mill's answer to the sacrifice problem is real and it is his fifth chapter of *Utilitarianism*. He argues that our sense of justice — the fierce, immediate feeling that a person has been *wronged*, not merely that a total has been reduced — is not an illusion and not a rival to utility. It tracks the rules whose observance matters most to human wellbeing: the rules of security, without which no other good is safe, because security is the one thing no human being can do without and the thing every other benefit depends on. So the rules of justice feel absolute because they are, of all the rules of utility, the most vital ones. That is why we mark them off with the special vocabulary of rights.

It's a good argument. It gives you a utilitarian explanation of why rights feel non-negotiable.

But look at what it doesn't give you. It explains the *feeling* of inviolability. It does not deliver the inviolability. On this account you have a right because, and only because, and for exactly as long as, protecting you serves the general welfare. The fence is real, and it is made of the same material as the thing it's supposed to be a fence against.

And that leaves the foundational objection standing, the one that has been waiting since the seventh dimension. In this theory, persons are containers. What matters is the total quantity of happiness in all the containers; the boundaries between them have no moral weight of their own. Which means that a loss to you and a gain to me cancel out, cleanly, as though we were the same person — as though your suffering could be compensated by somebody else's satisfaction, in the way this afternoon's pain can be compensated by this evening's relief inside one life.

But we are not one life. Nobody experiences the total. There is no being anywhere who feels the sum, and therefore the argument that justifies my sacrifice by pointing at the sum is pointing at a quantity that nobody will ever enjoy in the way I will suffer the loss.

That objection, made with a rigour that will take an entire episode to unfold, is what a philosopher in the twentieth century will use to knock utilitarianism off the throne it had occupied in the English-speaking world for a hundred and fifty years. He will accuse it of failing to take seriously the distinction between persons, and he will spend a very large book constructing an alternative. He is where this series is going, and he is a long way off yet.

## The man in the case   {tone: contemplative, note: "ENDING — bookend to the drawing via the auto-icon; the verdict; recast as beginning; handover to Tocqueville as a RETURN — the listener met him earlier in the series, in 1848; we now go back to the young magistrate on the prison mission"}

Bentham died in June of 1832, at eighty-four, in the house in Westminster. The day after he died, the Great Reform Act — the first serious widening of the British franchise, the thing he and his radicals had hammered at for decades — received the royal assent. He missed it by about a day.

And then he did the last and most characteristic thing of his life.

His will directed that his body be dissected, publicly, for the instruction of medical students — at a moment when English anatomy schools were so short of legal corpses that they were buying them from men who dug up graves and, in at least one notorious case, from men who simply produced them. A public dissection of a famous philosopher was an argument in favour of reform, and the reform passed that same year.

Then the will got stranger. What remained was to be preserved, dressed in his own clothes, seated in his own chair with his own walking stick, and kept. He called it an **auto-icon** — a self-image, a man serving as his own monument. The preservation of the head went badly, and a wax one was made instead. The figure passed to University College London, and it is there now. You can go and look at him. He is sitting up, in a case, with his hat, on a Tuesday afternoon in Bloomsbury, in the middle of a building full of students who mostly walk straight past.

It is easy to file that under eccentricity. It isn't. It is the last application of the principle, run on himself. A corpse is a resource. Rotting in the ground it produces nothing; dissected it teaches surgeons; preserved and displayed it goes on making the argument after the arguer is dead. What good does it do? — asked, finally, about his own body, and answered with the same cheerful ruthlessness he had applied to the gallows and the poorhouse and the men in the cells.

So let me say where I come down. {pause: 0.8}

I think the calculus is indispensable and I think it is not enough, and I don't think those two statements can be reconciled as neatly as either side would like.

Indispensable, because we have not found anything better for the actual work of government, and we are not going to. Every alternative in this series tells you what may not be done; almost none of them tell you what to *do* when a real minister has a real budget and two hospitals and one of them can be built. Bentham's question — what good does it do, and to how many — is the question a state owes its citizens, and a politics that refuses to count is not thereby more moral. It is just less accountable. Whatever your principles, somebody at some point has to add up the consequences, and the demand that they show the arithmetic in public is one of the great civilizing achievements of the modern world. It came from this man.

And not enough, because of the tower. Because everything humane the theory produced — the reprieve from the gallows, the argument for the men who were being hanged for their private lives, the footnote about the animals — arrived by the same route as the building with the invisible eye, and there is nothing inside the theory that distinguishes them. Only the sums, and which way they happen to fall this year. A person protected by nothing but a favourable calculation is not protected. He is *currently convenient.*

As for Mill: I think he is right about the higher pleasures and wrong to have believed he could get there from where he started. He saw something true — that a life spent entirely in easy satisfactions is a smaller life, and that the man in the cellar and the man in the cell are being deprived of something that no amount of contentment replaces. That is not snobbery; it is the thing his own breakdown taught him, at a cost. But he could only say it by reaching for a standard his philosophy had abolished — a picture of what a human being is *for*, which is Plato's question and Aristotle's, come back around after two thousand years to collect. And I would rather have Mill inconsistent than Bentham tidy.

Which is the same verdict as *On Liberty*, really. The harm principle is a fence that Mill's own foundations cannot quite hold up. It is also, on the historical record, one of the most useful sentences anybody has ever written, and I notice that every time somebody proposes to run my life for my own benefit, that sentence is what I reach for.

But don't let me close it. {pause: 0.8}

Because you are living inside this argument in a way you probably haven't noticed. Every time a government tells you that a restriction is justified by the aggregate benefit, that is Bentham with a spreadsheet, and the only question that matters is whether you have anything to say back to him besides *recount it*. Every time somebody insists that what you do in private is nobody's business, that is Mill's sentence, doing its work a hundred and sixty years on. And every time you catch yourself thinking that some pleasures are worth more than others — and then hesitate, because you're not sure you have the right to say so — you are standing precisely where Bentham and Mill are shouting at each other, and you are going to have to decide.

And there's one more thing in this episode that opens a door.

The Panopticon was never built, but the century that followed was obsessed with prisons — with the idea that you could design a building that would remake a soul. Which is why, in 1831, the French government sent two young magistrates across the Atlantic on an official mission to study the American penitentiary system: the new prisons where inmates were kept in silence and solitude, which the whole of Europe wanted to hear about.

You already know one of those two men, though you met him at the wrong end of his life and in the wrong street. Earlier in the series he was a deputy in his forties in the National Assembly in the summer of 1848 — the awkward witness, the liberal who voted for the state of siege and walked among the government's troops while Paris was taken street by street. Alexis de Tocqueville. That is where he ends up.

Go back seventeen years and he is twenty-five: a junior magistrate on a boat, with a prison report to write, and something else entirely on his mind.

They did write the prison report. But he had used the journey for something far larger. He spent nine months travelling through a country that had done what no country in the history of the world had done — abolished rank, dissolved the aristocracy he had himself been born into, and built itself from the ground up on the proposition that people are equal — and he came home convinced that he had seen the future, and frightened by something inside it.

The danger he'd identified had a name, and Mill would borrow it from him: the tyranny of the majority. But where Mill treated it as a problem to be fenced off, this man thought it was something worse. Not a flaw in democracy.

Democracy's own disease, growing out of its own virtues, and very possibly incurable.

Next time, we get on the boat with him.
