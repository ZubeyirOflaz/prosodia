## Two lists   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "cold open on vocabulary; no thinker, no doctrine, no second-person address until the end"}

Humble.

Meek. Selfless. Patient. Obedient. Mild. Modest. Long-suffering. Kind.

Now a second list.

Proud. Hard. Commanding. Splendid. Fierce. Contemptuous. Magnificent. Cruel.

You did not need to be told which of those lists was the moral one. The first list is what we call virtue. Not in a particular country, not in a particular century — that is simply what the word *virtue* points at, for you and for almost everyone you will ever meet. The second list is a description of a bully, or possibly a tyrant, or on a generous day an unusually charismatic villain in a film.

Both lists are old. Both lists are very old. {pause: 0.8}

And yet the lists have not always been in that order. There was a time, in the ancient world, when the second one was the moral list. When *proud*, *hard*, *commanding* and *splendid* were what a good man was, and *humble*, *meek* and *patient* described what a good man was emphatically *not* — described, in fact, the wretched, the base, the beaten, the sort of person about whom no song would ever be composed. And then something happened. The lists swapped places. The words changed sides, and the winning side won so completely that we no longer experience its victory as a victory. We experience it as morality. As what any decent person can see.

Something happened, then, that we have forgotten happened. And there is a man who claimed to have found out what it was — who said he could reconstruct the crime, name the perpetrators, and show you the fingerprints still sitting on the words in your own mouth.

He said the weak won. Not on the battlefield. On the battlefield the strong always win, that is what strong *means*. He said the weak won somewhere far stranger and far more permanent.

They won in the dictionary.

## What the words remember   {tone: curious}

Before we go anywhere near him, try the trick yourself, because the trick is doable and it is oddly addictive.

Take the word *villain*. It arrives in English out of Latin, through French, and at the bottom of it is the *villanus* — the man attached to the *villa*, the farm. A farmhand. A serf. That is all the word originally meant: a person who worked someone else's land. Somewhere along the way, "agricultural labourer" turned into "moral monster," and nobody voted on it. The word simply carried the opinion of the people who owned the land, and then the opinion evaporated and left the insult behind.

*Vulgar.* From *vulgus*: the crowd, the common people. Now it means coarse, tasteless, obscene.

*Generous.* From *generosus*: of good birth, well-bred, of a distinguished family. Now it means open-handed — because of course a nobleman gives freely, that is what noblemen are like, and the assumption slid so smoothly into the word that the word forgot it was ever an assumption. The same journey made *gentle* out of *gentle-born*, and *noble* — well, *noble* still wears both meanings openly, high-born and high-souled, as if they were obviously the same thing.

So your ordinary moral vocabulary, the one you use to praise your friends and condemn strangers, is not a neutral instrument. It is a battlefield after the battle. It is *sediment*. Every one of those words was, at some point, a claim someone made about who was better than whom, and the claim won, and then the claim went quiet and became a mere fact about the English language.

{pause: 1.0}

Which raises the possibility this whole episode is going to live inside. Suppose that is not just true of small words like *vulgar*. Suppose it is true of the big ones. *Good.* *Evil.* *Justice.* *Equality.* Suppose those are sediment too — the fossilised outcome of a fight so old that the fighting has been forgotten and only the verdict is left, sitting in our mouths, feeling eternal.

That suspicion, pursued with a philologist's patience and a prosecutor's malice, has a name. Its author called it a *genealogy* of morality. And a genealogy is not an argument about whether a value is true. It is an investigation into where a value came from, who it served, and what it has done to the people who hold it.

## Not where it came from, but who it was for   {tone: lucid}

Set that method beside everything this series has done so far, because the difference is the whole point.

Kant asked what reason itself commands, and answered that a moral law worth the name must be one you could will for everyone, everywhere, without contradiction. Mill asked which arrangement produces the most happiness across the greatest number, and tried to make suffering count wherever it occurred. Rousseau asked what a free people, legislating for itself, genuinely wills in common. Every one of them, however much they disagreed, was doing the same *kind* of thing: arguing about the content of morality, on the assumption that morality is the sort of thing you can be right or wrong about. They were all, in that sense, on the same side of the table.

Our man walks in and refuses to sit at the table at all. He does not say Kant's law is invalid, or that Mill's arithmetic is wrong. He asks a question none of them thought to ask about themselves.

*Why does a professor in Königsberg feel, in his bones, that every human being is an end in himself? Where did that feeling come from? Whose interests does it serve? What kind of creature needs to believe it?* {pause: 1.0}

That is a change of subject so total that it can look like cheating. He is treating a moral conviction the way a doctor treats a symptom — not as a claim to be assessed but as evidence about the organism producing it. He said his questions were not *is this true* but something closer to: under what conditions did human beings invent these value judgements, and — the sharpest turn — what is the *value* of the values? Do they help us flourish, or are they a very slow poison we have learned to call medicine?

And now the objection you should already be forming, because it is the right one, and because if it stands, the whole episode collapses.

## The oldest objection   {tone: measured, note: "pre-empt the misreading squarely; concede the strong form"}

Where an idea came from has nothing to do with whether it is true.

That is not a quibble. It is a rule, and it is a good one. The chemist who dreamed the structure of the benzene ring did not thereby make organic chemistry a dream. A theorem discovered by a scoundrel for scoundrelly reasons is still a theorem. Philosophers gave this error a name — the *genetic fallacy* — reasoning from the birth of a belief to the worth of a belief. And if that is all a genealogy of morality amounts to, then it is a long, beautifully written mistake. You can grant that "humble" has a shabby history and still think humility is a virtue, just as you can grant that "generous" started life as snobbery and still think generosity is good.

So let me give the argument its stronger form, which is the form it actually has. {pause: 0.8}

The genealogy is not aimed at the *content* of a moral claim. It is aimed at the claim's *credentials*. Some values present themselves as ordinary human preferences, revisable, up for discussion — those have nothing to fear here. But our central moral values do not present themselves that way. They present themselves as *discovered*: as written into the structure of the world, or of reason, or of God's will. Unconditioned. Not from anywhere. Binding on you whether you like it or not, precisely because no one made them up.

Against a claim like that, a birth certificate is lethal. You have not shown that mercy is bad by showing that it was invented in a particular place by particular people for particular reasons. But you have shown that it was *invented*. And a value that was invented cannot go on collecting the special obedience owed to something eternal. It has to come down off the pedestal and argue with the rest of us.

That is the move. Not *this value is false*, but *this value has a history, and things with histories can be judged, and might have been otherwise*. Everything difficult about the man we are about to meet lives in the gap between those two sentences — including, as we will see at the very end, the question of whether he himself managed to stay on the right side of the gap.

His name was Friedrich Nietzsche. And before we take the argument apart, you should know something about the circumstances in which it was written, because they are almost unbearably ironic.

## The professor who resigned   {tone: contemplative}

He was appointed to a chair of classical philology at the University of Basel in eighteen sixty-nine, at the age of twenty-four. That is not a distinguished early career; that is an anomaly. He had not finished his doctorate. Leipzig awarded him the degree without examination, on the strength of work he had already published, because his teacher's recommendation was so extravagant that the university simply took his word for it. Twenty-four. A full professor. Philology in the German nineteenth century was not a soft subject — it was the queen of the sciences, the discipline that taught Europe how to read a manuscript, catch a forgery, date a text, hear the difference between a word's meaning and its ancestry. He was trained, then, as precisely the kind of specialist who can hear a fossil in a word. He never stopped being one, and it is worth remembering that when the rhetoric gets loud.

The next year the Franco-Prussian War broke out, and he went to it as a medical orderly. He was in the field a matter of weeks. He came back with dysentery and diphtheria, and something in his constitution never recovered. What followed was a decade of collapse in slow motion: migraines that flattened him for days at a time, vomiting, eyesight failing so badly he could barely read the texts he was employed to teach. In eighteen seventy-nine, aged thirty-four, he resigned the chair and took a small pension.

And then, for ten years, he had no home. {pause: 0.8}

He moved between rented rooms according to the weather and the pressure in his skull. Summers up in the thin bright air of Sils-Maria in the Swiss Alps; winters down south — Genoa and Rapallo on the Italian coast, then Nice on the French, and finally Turin. A trunk of books, a boarding-house table, chloral hydrate to sleep. He wrote in the intervals between attacks, which is why the books read the way they do: aphorisms, bursts, sudden altitude — a man working fast because the window is about to shut. *On the Genealogy of Morality* came out in eighteen eighty-seven and was written, essentially, in weeks.

Nobody read it. That is not a figure of speech. His books sold in the dozens. He had part of one of them printed at his own expense in a run of about forty copies and could not find forty people to give them to. The most explosive thinker in Europe was, in his own lifetime, a semi-invalid on a pension whose collected sales would have embarrassed a poet.

Now hold that man next to his argument. He is about to tell you that morality was built by the weak — by the sick, the powerless, the excluded, the ones who could not act — as a way of getting revenge on the strong without ever having to fight them. And he is writing it in a rented room, half-blind, unread, unmarried, dependent on a pension, in physical pain, at the age his contemporaries were running institutions.

You are entitled to notice that. Remember it. We are coming back for it, and I do not intend to let him off.

## Achilles has nothing on his conscience   {tone: curious, note: "steelman master morality from inside; do not launder the slavery"}

To feel what he thought had been lost, go to the poem he had spent his professional life inside.

You are in the world of the *Iliad*. What is the highest word of praise available to you? It is not *good* in our sense. The Greek is *aretē*, and we translate it "virtue," which is nearly a mistranslation, because *aretē* in Homer means something closer to *excellence* in the way a blade is excellent: effective, formidable, splendid, superbly functioning at what you are. A fast runner has *aretē*. A well-made ship, in a sense, has *aretē*. And so does Achilles — who in our terms is a magnificent liability: proud, vengeful, sulking in his tent while his allies die, dragging a corpse behind a chariot.

What Achilles conspicuously does not have is a conscience. He has honour, and rage, and grief, and shame — shame is enormous in that world — but he does not lie awake wondering whether he is a good person. The question would not parse.

And notice what the word for the opposite is. The aristocrat of that world calls himself and his kind *agathos* — well-born, capable, admired, the sort of man who matters — and the people below him *kakos*: base, lowly, wretched, unimpressive. Nietzsche's claim, and this is the hinge of the whole book, is that this pair of words is not the pair we use. The noble scale runs *good* against *bad*. Ours runs *good* against **evil**. In his own German: *gut* against *schlecht* — good against shabby, poor, of low quality, the way you would say a badly made shoe is *schlecht* — where our scale runs *gut* against *böse*, good against wicked. And those are not the same distinction at all.

Give those two scales their names now, because you will meet them in every book ever written about this man. The first — the one that starts from the noble's *yes* to himself and ranks everyone else downward as an afterthought — is what Nietzsche calls **master morality**. The second, the one we are about to watch being assembled from below, out of the only materials available to people who have no other materials, is **slave morality**. Two scales, and one of them beat the other so thoroughly that you were raised inside it without ever being told it had a rival.

Start with the master's. *Bad*, in the noble mouth, means *contemptible*. It carries no accusation. The nobleman does not hate the peasant. Hatred is effortful; hatred requires you to take someone seriously. He looks past him. The peasant is not wicked, he is merely of no account — a fact about the landscape, like weather. Which means the noble's self-esteem is not built out of anybody else. He says *yes* to himself first, and the low ranking of everyone else is an afterthought, a shrug.

Now steelman this, honestly, because there is something in it and you will not understand the next hour if you refuse to feel it. There is a real appeal in a valuation that begins with affirmation — that says yes to a life, to a body, to strength, to appetite, to the world, without first requiring a permission slip; that is grateful for existence rather than suspicious of it; that does not need an enemy in order to know what it is. Nietzsche thought the noble type was often stupid — politically naïve, historically doomed, incapable of seeing what was being built underneath it — but he did not think it was contemptible. He thought it was *healthy*, and he thought health had been driven out of Europe.

And now the part that must not be laundered. That world ran on slavery. It ran on conquest, on plunder, on the routine violence of the strong against the weak, on the settled conviction that most human beings existed as instruments for the few. Nietzsche knew this perfectly well. He was a philologist; he had read the sources; nobody had to break the news to him. He did not flinch from it, and he did not apologise for it either. Keep that in your pocket too. We will need it before the end, and it will not be the sister's fault.

## What the powerless can do   {tone: tense, note: "the reversal; the second-person hinge"}

So put yourself on the other side of it. {pause: 0.6}

You are in that world, and you are on the bottom of it. Occupied, enslaved, taxed, conscripted, dispossessed — pick your century, they are all available. The people who own your life are physically stronger, better armed, better fed, and organised. Every avenue of retaliation is closed. You cannot fight them; you would die. You cannot outwit them; they own the courts. You cannot leave.

But you have one faculty they cannot confiscate. You can *evaluate*. You can hold an opinion about them, and you can teach that opinion to your children.

And so — this is Nietzsche's story, and feel how ingenious it is before you decide whether it is true — you perform the only revolution available to a person with no power at all. You do not seize their property. You seize their *vocabulary*. You take the traits that make them them — the pride, the hardness, the appetite, the freedom to act, the sheer unembarrassed strength — and you do not merely resent them. You *reclassify* them. You declare them not enviable but **evil**. Wicked. Damned. Marked. And then, the second half of the operation, the part that is genuinely brilliant: you take your own condition, every humiliating feature of it, and you rename it.

You cannot strike back. Call it *forgiveness*.

You cannot rise. Call it *humility*.

You dare not act. Call it *patience*.

You must obey. Call it *obedience to God*.

You have no pleasures. Call it *purity*.

You wait, because waiting is all there is. Call it *hope* — and promise that at the end of it the scales come level, and the ones who are laughing now will not be laughing then, and the last shall be first. {pause: 1.2}

Nietzsche's word for the engine of this is French, not German: **ressentiment**. It is not simply resentment, which anyone can feel and then discharge by shouting or throwing a punch. *Ressentiment* is what resentment becomes when it *cannot* be discharged — when there is no outlet, no possibility of action, and the feeling turns back inside and begins to ferment. And his startling claim is that this fermenting, impotent, inward-turned hatred is not sterile. It is *creative*. It is one of the most creative forces in human history. It built a moral universe.

He called the result the **slave revolt in morality**, and he was not being coy about who he thought had accomplished it. He credits it to a priestly people — he names the Jews, in the *Genealogy*, as the ones who dared the great inversion of values — and then to Christianity as the vehicle that carried it to every corner of the West. I am telling you plainly what the text says because you deserve to know, and because later in this episode we have to deal with what was done with those sentences. Note two things now. In Nietzsche's own frame, this is not a slur but an astonished tribute: he thought it was the single most consequential act of spiritual power in recorded history, a two-thousand-year victory won by the militarily helpless over the militarily invincible, and he says so in the language of awe. And note also that awe is not innocence. He wrote sentences that a certain kind of reader would later be delighted to find.

## The lambs and the birds of prey   {tone: lucid, rate: slow, note: "name the difficulty; this is the hard step; checkpoint and a real counter before the turn"}

This next step is the hardest thing in the book, and it is the reason the book survives. Stay with me for it, because it is where the argument stops being a colourful story about ancient grievance and becomes something that can actually cut you.

Nietzsche puts a little fable in the middle of the first essay. Lambs, he says, dislike large birds of prey — no surprise there. But that gives us no ground for holding it against the birds that they carry off lambs. And then he lets the lambs talk. *These birds of prey are evil; and whoever is least like a bird of prey, whoever is rather its opposite — a lamb — surely he is good?* And Nietzsche says: there is nothing to object to in that. Nothing at all. Of course that is what a lamb would say. Except — and here he turns — the birds of prey may look on with a certain irony, and murmur: *we have nothing against these good lambs; in fact, we love them; nothing is tastier than a tender lamb.*

Now, why is this more than a joke at the lambs' expense? {pause: 0.8}

Because of what the lambs have to believe in order to say it. To *blame* the bird, you cannot simply dislike being eaten. You need the bird to have had a *choice*. You need there to be a bird sitting behind the swooping — a doer behind the deed, a soul behind the strength, an agent who could have refrained and did not. Nietzsche's line is that there is no such thing: there is no being behind the doing; the doer is a fiction added to the deed. Strength is not a possession that a strong thing chooses to use. Strength is what strength *does*. Lightning does not decide to flash.

And once you have installed that fiction — the free-floating soul that could always have done otherwise — look at what becomes possible. Two things, and the second is the payload.

The first: the strong become *guilty*. Not dangerous, not unlucky, not merely opposed — culpable. Which means you can hate them righteously, and hate is much easier to sustain when it is righteous.

The second, which is the deeper one: your weakness becomes an *achievement*. Because if strength is optional, then not being strong is a choice too. And a choice can be a merit. The inability to retaliate, which is a fact about your circumstances, is quietly re-described as the *decision* not to retaliate, which is a fact about your soul. You do not simply lack claws. You have *renounced* claws. You are not powerless — you are *virtuous*. {pause: 1.2}

Checkpoint. What he is really saying is this: the invention of the responsible self was not a neutral discovery about human nature. It was, at least in part, an instrument — the mechanism by which suffering was converted into superiority. And every time we say "he *chose* to be cruel" we are using a tool that, on this account, was forged for that purpose.

You can reject this, and there are two serious ways to do it. The first is to give him the metaphysics entirely and keep the practice anyway. Holding people responsible does not have to rest on a ghostly doer sitting behind the deed; it can be defended by what it *does* — praise and blame are how creatures like us shape each other's future conduct, how a community teaches itself what it will and will not tolerate. On that reading you can agree there is no soul behind the strength and still think blaming the cruel man is exactly the right response to him. The second is to notice how much of the argument is about motive. Suppose it is true that some people reach for the language of responsibility to flatter their own weakness. That is a claim about why they say it. It leaves the question of whether anyone is ever actually responsible precisely where it was — a psychological diagnosis is not a metaphysical refutation, and Nietzsche of all people knew the difference.

But notice that you cannot reject it by finding it distasteful, and notice how much of your own moral machinery it has its hands on.

## Two women who do not take revenge   {tone: measured, note: "commit-then-complicate; the running handrail"}

Here is a case, and it is going to follow us to the end of the episode.

Two people have been badly wronged. Publicly, unmistakably, by someone who got away with it. Neither of them retaliates.

The first could have. She had the means, the opportunity, and a fair chance of getting away with it, and she looked at the whole business and found she did not want it — not out of calculation, not out of fear, and not because she had talked herself round. It simply did not interest her. She has other things to do with her life than spend it on this. She thinks about the person who wronged her roughly twice a year.

The second could not have. No means, no opportunity, no chance. She has thought about it every day for six years. She has constructed, in enormous detail, an account in which she is the better person — morally cleaner, more sensitive, more wounded, more *deserving* — and the one who wronged her is not merely wrong but rotten, corrupt in essence, and will one day be found out. She has never lifted a finger. She has never stopped composing the indictment.

{pause: 1.0}

Now: from outside, describe the difference. Both of them refrained. Both, in our vocabulary, forgave — or at any rate did not seek revenge, which is the closest our vocabulary gets. And our moral language will praise them in the same words. *She rose above it. She took the high road. She didn't stoop.*

But you know they are not the same. You knew it before I finished the second description. One of those women is free and the other one is in a cell she built herself and calls a chapel.

That is Nietzsche's whole complaint compressed into a single case, and it is why he is worth an argument rather than a dismissal. His charge is not that mercy is bad. It is that our moral language is *systematically unable to tell those two apart* — and worse, that it was designed by the second woman, so it flatters her, and it will keep flattering her, and it gives her the one thing she wants most in the world, which is to experience her powerlessness as a form of excellence.

And once you have seen it, you will see it everywhere, including places you would rather not. You have watched a public argument in which the moral high ground was clearly not a position but a *weapon* — where the point of the outrage was the pleasure of the outrage, and the wound was too valuable to give up. You have felt the small warm flush that comes from being the injured party. Nietzsche's suspicion is not aimed at some historical peasant. It is aimed at that flush. We even have folk words for the phenomenon — *sour grapes*, *sanctimony*, *the moralised grudge* — which suggests that at some level we have always known.

## The herd   {tone: grave, note: "the political payload; turn on the series"}

Now he turns the machine on us, and this is where a story about antiquity becomes an attack on everything this series has built.

Because if that inversion happened, it did not stop happening. Nietzsche's claim is that slave morality *won* — completely, permanently — and that what we now call ordinary decency is its late, comfortable, deodorised form. He calls it **herd morality**. And his list of its manifestations is deliberately outrageous: Christianity, yes, but also the Enlightenment, also liberalism, also democracy, also socialism, also — with special contempt — the humanitarian sentiment that thinks the reduction of suffering is the highest political aim. He regards these as one movement in four costumes. Strip the theology out of Christianity and you do not get a neutral modern secular morality. You get Christian morality with the receipt removed: the same commitment to equality, to pity, to the primacy of the victim, now floating free of the God who used to justify it, and all the more unquestionable for having lost its author.

Which puts him in direct collision with almost every person we have spent twenty episodes with. Kant grounds politics in the equal dignity of every rational being. Mill counts every person's happiness once. Rousseau builds legitimacy on a will that belongs to all equally. Marx wants the last to be first in this world rather than the next. Every one of them takes the equal worth of persons as the *floor* of the argument — the thing you reason *from*.

Nietzsche says the floor is a symptom. {pause: 1.0}

His positive claim, and he states it in *Beyond Good and Evil* without hedging, is that every enhancement of the type "man" has so far been the work of an aristocratic society — that everything we point to when we defend the human record, the art and the thought and the daring, was produced by orders of rank, by distance between people, by some human beings being placed far enough above others to have the space to become something. And a politics organised entirely around the greatest happiness of the greatest number will not produce that. It will produce comfort. Safety. Insurance. Small pleasures, evenly distributed. He gives us a picture of the end state: the *last man*, who has made everything equal and everything mild and everything harmless, who says "we have invented happiness" — and blinks.

Hear the verb. Not *found*. **Invented**. Even the contentment is manufactured, and the last man is pleased with himself for the manufacture. That is a real indictment, and I am not going to pretend it can be waved off by pointing out that it is rude.

## The best case, and why it is still not good enough   {tone: measured, note: "steelman hard, then judge; refuse false parity out loud"}

So let me build the strongest version, the one his ablest defender would make.

Ask what a civilisation is *for*. Not what makes it fair — what makes it worth having existed. Because it is at least possible for a society to be humane, safe, decent, extraordinarily good at reducing pain, and also flat. Producing nothing anyone will care about in two hundred years. A society can optimise for the absence of suffering and forget to ask what the absence of suffering is supposed to make room for. And there is something genuinely uncomfortable near this thought, because the works we most admire were very often made by difficult people, under harsh conditions, at costs no humane administrator would authorise. If you had a committee with a mandate to minimise misery, and you gave it the eighteenth century to run, you might get a kinder century and no Beethoven. Nietzsche's question — *does this morality produce human beings capable of greatness, or does it produce a species of well-fed rabbit?* — is not a stupid question. It is a question that the whole utilitarian and egalitarian tradition finds it very hard to look at directly, because its own machinery cannot register the answer. That is the steelman, and it deserves your discomfort.

And now I am going to stop refereeing, because steelmanning a position is not the same as splitting the difference with it, and I do not think these two are equals. {pause: 0.8}

Where Nietzsche has a politics — and it is thin, he is a moralist and a psychologist far more than a political theorist — it is an aristocratic radicalism, and he is not shy. He writes that high culture requires a subject class; that slavery, in some form, has always been among the conditions of human enhancement; that the many exist for the sake of the few. Some of this is in early unpublished work and some of it is in the books he sent to the printer himself, and none of it is a slip of the pen. The contempt for pity is not a slip either. Nor is the loathing of the weak *as* weak.

So set the two claims side by side, and answer honestly. On one side: some human beings should be treated as raw material, because civilisation needs a pedestal and pedestals are made of people. On the other: the claim of the person who has been designated as pedestal.

And in his own century that person is not a hypothetical. She is in the mills and the mines and the back stairs of the houses where the culture he admired was being enjoyed, working the hours that made the leisure possible. And the arrangement is being defended, in his lifetime, on precisely the grounds that someone has to.

She also has, in his lifetime, spokesmen with names — which matters, because it means the argument is not Nietzsche against a category. In the years he was writing the *Genealogy*, German Social Democracy was an outlawed party: Bismarck's anti-socialist laws had driven it underground, its paper edited abroad by exiles — Eduard Bernstein among them, whom we met three episodes ago — and smuggled back across the border in luggage. At its head was August Bebel, a lathe-turner by trade, largely self-taught, who had already been tried for treason and imprisoned for it, and who spent his working life saying in public that the people at the bottom of that arrangement were the moral equals of the people at the top. Socialism, in his books, he named and attacked as ressentiment with a political programme. So the collision is not Nietzsche against Kant and Mill, who are safely dead and safely abstract. It is Nietzsche against Bebel: the invalid in the boarding house who wrote that the many exist for the sake of the few, and the turner who had stood in a courtroom for the proposition that they do not.

Those are not evenly balanced propositions between which a reasonable person hovers. The second is stronger, and it is stronger for reasons Nietzsche himself cannot dismiss — because the person on the bottom is not making an argument about metaphysics, she is telling you what it is like, and the whole force of Nietzsche's own method is that you should attend to what a valuation *does to the people inside it*.

And there is a further problem, which is that his weapon has no safety catch. Genealogy is an acid that dissolves whatever you pour it on, and nothing in the method exempts the genealogist. So: where does the contempt come from? Who benefits from a doctrine that ranks vitality above justice? A sick, sleepless, half-blind man in a boarding house, on a pension, unread, without a wife or a post or a following, writing hymns to health and command and the aristocratic body — what is the *psychology* of that? {pause: 1.0}

I have to be careful here, because this is exactly the cheap shot he taught us to take, and cheap shots do not refute arguments. His account of *ressentiment* is not false because he was ill, any more than equality is false because slaves wanted it. But he does not get an exemption. If the whole point of the method is that no value gets to float above its own history, then the value of *strength* goes into the acid with all the rest, and the sound you hear is his own case fizzing.

## The gods are dead, and the shadow is long   {tone: contemplative, note: "compress the metaphysics; hedge the will to power explicitly"}

Two more pieces, briefly, because they are the ones people expect and the ones this episode should not be about.

In *The Gay Science* he puts a madman in a marketplace who cries out that God is dead — God remains dead, and we have killed him — and the passage is almost always misquoted, because it is not a boast. It is vertigo. *Who gave us the sponge to wipe away the entire horizon? Where is it moving now? Are we not plunging continually?* He does not think the death of God is a liberation you can just enjoy. He thinks it is an event so large that Europe has not yet noticed it happened, and that our morality is the light still arriving from a star that has already gone out. We kept the commandments and lost the commander, and we have not begun to reckon the bill.

His answers to that, such as they are, we are going to name and set aside. There is the **Übermensch** — the overman, the human being who could create values instead of inheriting them. There is **eternal recurrence** — the thought experiment of a life you would be willing to live again, identically, forever. Both are more literary than argued, both are the parts of him his admirers find hardest to defend, and both belong in a different episode. This one is about what he did to morality and politics.

And then there is the **will to power**, and here I have to be straight with you about the state of the evidence, because the popular version is not on solid ground. In the books he published, the phrase does real work as a psychological thesis — that what drives human beings is not pleasure or survival but the drive to grow, to overcome resistance, to extend. Fine. But the grand version, the metaphysical one, the *world is will to power and nothing besides* — that comes overwhelmingly from unpublished notebooks. There is one published passage that reaches for the cosmic reading, in *Beyond Good and Evil*, and even there he builds it out of *supposing* — a chain of "if we grant this, then perhaps that," offered as an experiment rather than laid down as a doctrine. Whether Nietzsche ever actually held it as a claim about everything, or whether a thesis about human psychology was inflated into a cosmology by editors and enthusiasts, is genuinely contested among the people who know the material best. Brian Leiter, one of his most careful modern interpreters, reads it deflationarily. Others disagree. I am flagging that rather than smoothing it, and I am flagging it now, because the reason the inflated version became famous is the story we have to tell next.

## Turin   {tone: somber, rate: slow}

The third of January, eighteen eighty-nine. Turin.

He collapses in the street. What happened in the following days we can partly reconstruct, because he wrote letters — short, wild, exalted letters to friends and to strangers and to the King of Italy, signing himself *Dionysus* and *The Crucified*, announcing that he was having certain people shot, that all antisemites should be shot, that the world had been transfigured. His friend Franz Overbeck came down from Basel and brought him back by train, into a clinic.

There is a scene attached to that collapse that you have almost certainly heard: that he came out of his lodgings, saw a cabman flogging a horse in the piazza, ran across, threw his arms around the animal's neck, and fell. It is the most famous image in his biography, and I have to tell you that it may not have happened. The story surfaces late, from sources at some remove from the event, and historians treat it as embellished at best and legend at worst. It is *too* good — the philosopher of hardness undone by pity for a beaten animal — which is exactly the sort of story that gets written afterwards, backwards, out of the meaning rather than the facts. So I will leave it where it belongs: as something we cannot verify and should not pretend to.

What is certain is that he never wrote another word for publication, and lived eleven more years in that condition, dependent, silent or nearly so — the clinics first, then his mother in Naumburg, and then, after her death, his sister. What was wrong with him has never been settled. Syphilis was assumed for most of a century; other explanations have been argued since, and none of them commands the field. We do not know.

He died in nineteen hundred, having sold almost no books, having founded no school, having no idea what the century was about to do with his name.

## The sister   {tone: grave, note: "the mandatory reckoning; give it real time"}

Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche was two years younger than her brother, and by the time she took charge of him she had a project of her own.

She had married a man named Bernhard Förster, an agitator of the German antisemitic movement — a schoolteacher who had put his name to an antisemitic petition and lost his post over his activism. In eighteen eighty-six the two of them sailed for Paraguay to found a colony. It was called *Nueva Germania*: a pure German settlement in the jungle, an Aryan homeland, subscribers recruited on the promise of land. It went the way such things go. The land was poor, the settlers sickened, the money ran out, and in eighteen eighty-nine — the same year her brother collapsed in Turin — Bernhard Förster killed himself.

Her brother had been unambiguous about all of it. He had broken with Richard Wagner, the great friendship and formation of his life, and antisemitism was part of what he broke with. He wrote about German nationalism, and about the new Reich, with something close to disgust. He thought the antisemites of his day were exactly the phenomenon his own book described: mediocrities organising their resentment into a movement and calling it honour. In a letter to Elisabeth herself, as it comes down to us, he told her that being unequivocally opposed to antisemitism was for him a matter of *honour* — that her association with that world was a kind of foreignness that filled him with gloom. And in the deranged notes of January eighteen eighty-nine, when the mind was going, one of the things it still had a grip on was that antisemites should be shot.

She came home, and she took over. {pause: 0.8}

By the eighteen-nineties she controlled the copyrights, the manuscripts, the notebooks, the invalid, and the story. She founded the Nietzsche Archive, moved it to Weimar, installed it in a villa, and by all accounts displayed her brother to visitors — the mute, vacant philosopher in a white robe, the sage beyond words. She commissioned the portraits and curated the myth. And out of the notebooks — thousands of pages of fragments, drafts, discarded material, things he had written and set aside — she and her collaborators assembled a book.

They called it *The Will to Power*, and they presented it as his unfinished masterwork: the system, at last, the architecture beneath the aphorisms. It appeared in nineteen-oh-one and was enlarged in nineteen-oh-six, and for two generations it was read as the culmination of his thought.

It is not a book he wrote. That needs stating precisely, because the precision is the whole point. The individual fragments are genuine — this is not a case of invented text. But the *book* — the selection, the ordering, the numbered architecture, the very implication that these scraps were headed for a system — is editorial. Nietzsche had toyed with a project under that title and abandoned it. What his sister published was a mosaic assembled out of a wastebasket and framed as a testament. When Italian scholars — Giorgio Colli and Mazzino Montinari — went back to the manuscripts in the nineteen-sixties and seventies and produced a proper critical edition, they took the thing apart, and it has not been put back together. Along the way it also emerged that she had been willing to tamper with the correspondence when the correspondence was inconvenient.

And she was, all this time, a political operator. She courted the Italian fascists. She courted the Nazis. Hitler visited the archive; there are photographs of him contemplating the bust of a man whose books he shows little sign of having read, and the story goes that she pressed her brother's walking stick into his hands. When she died in nineteen thirty-five, Hitler attended the funeral. She had spent forty years converting her brother into a German prophet, and she lived long enough to hand him over.

## What was forged, and what was not   {tone: measured, rate: slow, note: "the three-way partition; do not collapse"}

Now the part where honesty costs something, because there are two easy stories available here and both of them are lies.

The first easy story is that the Nazi Nietzsche is entirely his sister's fabrication, and that the real Nietzsche was a gentle aesthete who has been unfairly slandered. The second easy story is that the Nazis read him correctly and he is essentially their philosopher. Neither survives contact with the material. Let me separate three things, and please keep them separate, because almost everybody collapses them.

**One: what was genuinely forged.** The systematic *Will to Power* as a book. And more importantly, the grafting of Nietzsche onto German nationalism, onto racial antisemitism, onto the mass movement — every one of which he attacked explicitly, in print, in the works he himself published. A philosophy whose central charge is that resentment organises itself into morality and calls itself justice was handed to the largest engine of organised resentment in modern history. There is no reading of the *Genealogy* on which the Nuremberg rallies are anything other than the herd in uniform. That appropriation is not interpretation. It is theft, with his sister holding the door.

**Two: what was genuinely his, and is genuinely disturbing.** The anti-egalitarianism. The contempt for pity. The claim that the many exist for the few. The relish for hardness. The conviction that the reduction of suffering is a low aim. None of that is a misprint, none of it needed a sister to invent it, and it is the part his admirers most want to explain away — usually by declaring it "merely cultural" and not political, which is a distinction he did not respect and neither should we. A man who says a high culture requires a subject class has said something political, and people looking for permission will find it.

**Three: why he was so *usable*.** He wrote in aphorisms, in masks, in voices, in deliberate provocations, in a style built to resist being turned into a system. That is a genuine intellectual virtue and it was also a catastrophic vulnerability, because a body of work with no load-bearing walls can be quarried by anyone for anything. Fascists took the hardness. Existentialists took the freedom. The literary left took the suspicion of every established value. All three found real stone. In *Ecce Homo*, written in his last productive months, he wrote that he had a terrible fear of one day being pronounced *holy* — and the fear was well placed, just in the wrong direction. He was not sanctified. He was conscripted.

So: the Nazis did not read him. They looted him. And the looting was possible partly because of what he actually wrote. Both of those sentences are true, and if you only ever say one of them you are not telling the truth about Friedrich Nietzsche.

## The children of the genealogist   {tone: lucid}

Here is the strange afterlife. The method escaped the man.

Because you have met the genealogical move before in this series, and you are going to meet it again. Marx does a version of it when he treats the ruling ideas of an age as the ideas of its ruling class — asking not whether the doctrine of free contract is coherent but whose factory it protects. Weber, next episode, does a cooler version, tracing the rationalised, disenchanted world back to religious impulses that no longer exist. And in the twentieth century Michel Foucault will name Nietzsche as his ancestor outright, and turn the same instrument on madness, on medicine, on punishment, on sexuality, showing that the categories we experience as simply *there* have birthdays, and authors, and functions.

That family shares a suspicion and a technique: that our most confident concepts are not discoveries but deposits, and that the way to understand them is to find out what they were *for*. It has become one of the standard tools of modern thought — used most often, and this is the irony, by people on the political left, against exactly the kinds of hierarchy Nietzsche wanted to preserve. The acid he mixed to dissolve equality has spent a century dissolving privilege instead.

And the family also shares a family weakness, which is worth naming here, because it is the one flaw the whole line inherits. If every value is a deposit left by a power relation, what is the genealogist standing on when he objects to anything? Critics have pressed exactly this against Foucault, and it is the same question I pressed against Nietzsche a few minutes ago. A method that unmasks everything has trouble explaining why unmasking is good.

## The bill   {tone: measured, note: "return to the through-line, then advocate; then bookend"}

So we come back to the question this series has been asking since a delegation of Athenians walked into a room on Melos and told the locals that justice is what happens between equals in power. What makes authority legitimate?

Nietzsche does not answer it. That is the thing to see. He does not offer a rival theory of legitimacy — a better contract, a truer general will, a sounder calculus. He does something more unsettling. He looks at the question and says: *listen to the words you are asking it in.* Justice. Equality. Rights. Dignity. Every one of them a deposit. Every one of them, on his account, the residue of an ancient inability to hit back. And if that is right, then twenty episodes of the most careful reasoning in the Western record are twenty episodes of very intelligent people finding elaborate justifications for a preference they inherited from people who had no other option.

This series will not resolve that. It cannot. I am going to tell you where I come down, and you should treat it as one reader's judgement rather than a verdict.

I think the genealogy can be *true* and the value can *survive* it. {pause: 1.0}

Suppose he is exactly right about the history. Suppose the doctrine that every human life counts equally really did come into the world through the back door — carried by the powerless because they had nothing else to carry, ideology before it was ever philosophy. That tells you something about the cause of the belief. It tells you nothing about whether the belief is worth holding. Ideas do not inherit the character of their parents. The person who first said that the powerful should not be able to do whatever they like to the weak may have said it out of pure impotent spite, and it would still be the single most useful sentence anyone has ever composed.

But do not let him off that lightly either, because he has taken something real, and I think he took it deliberately. He has taken away the *innocence*. Before the genealogy, you could hold your deepest moral commitments the way you hold the ground — as simply there, underneath, not chosen, not your responsibility. After it, you cannot. If equality was made rather than found, then it is not the floor, it is a *building*; and buildings are maintained by somebody, or they come down. You do not get to inherit it and call the inheritance a discovery. You have to choose it, and argue for it, and pay for it, in your own century, on the merits, without the guarantee.

That is a bill, and it is addressed to you. Which is, I think, a harder thing to be asked than anything Kant asked, and Nietzsche — who despised the destination — is the one who handed it over.

And so, at the end, the two women. {pause: 0.6}

The one who did not take revenge because she had better things to do with her life. And the one who did not take revenge because she could not, and turned that into a lifetime's moral superiority, and has never once been happy.

Our moral vocabulary praises them in identical words. Nietzsche's contribution — the durable one, the one that would survive even if every political sentence he wrote were struck out — is that you can now hear the difference. Not in other people. That was always easy. In the words you reach for when you are the one who has been wronged, and there is nothing you can do about it, and something in you begins, very quietly, to compose the indictment.

You know which of those two you are. You knew before I asked.

## What comes next   {tone: measured, note: "handover hook"}

He was a prophet, and prophets are bad at the day after. He announced that the old gods were dead and that our morality was their fading light, and then he offered us an overman and a myth of recurrence and a great deal of thunder, and died before anyone was listening.

The next man in this story inherits exactly the world Nietzsche described — disenchanted, unhaunted, stripped of sacred meaning — and refuses to shout about it. A German sociologist, sober to the point of coldness, who takes the fever out of the diagnosis and asks the practical question Nietzsche never bothered with. Not *what should we worship now that God is dead*. Something far more disquieting.

Why do people obey?

Not why *should* they. Why do they, actually, in fact, every day, in their millions, without being forced. He will find three answers, and then he will show you the machine those answers have built around your life — the most efficient, most rational, most inescapable structure human beings have ever assembled — and he will call it a cage of iron.
