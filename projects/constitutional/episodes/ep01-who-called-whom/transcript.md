## A phone, and a crowd {tone: measured, note: "Unhurried scene-setting; a story told plainly. Let each number land as a number."}

On the eleventh of August 2006, an Irish company called Digital Rights Ireland went to Ireland's High Court and told it about a mobile phone.

The company said it owned the phone. It said the phone had been registered that June. And it said it had used the phone ever since.

Every call made on it left a record with the operator. {pause: 0.5} Who called whom. When, and from where. And, over the months, how often.

A European law said those records had to be kept for months. It covered everyone who used a phone or the internet, {pause: 0.5} whether or not they had done anything at all.

The records held the traces of conversations, {pause: 0.5} and none of the words.

Some years later, in Austria, the same objection had become a crowd.

Austria had written that European law into its own telecommunications act. Before Austria's Constitutional Court, the government of the province of Carinthia challenged it. So did two men, a Mr Seitlinger and a Mr Tschohl. The judgment names them both. Then, for everyone else, it gives a number: {pause: 0.6} eleven thousand one hundred and twenty-eight others. Add the two men, and that is eleven thousand one hundred and thirty people, {pause: 0.5} against one law.

On the eighth of April 2014, in Luxembourg, the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled on the Irish case and the Austrian case together. The European law behind both was invalid. No limit was set on when that took effect, and so it was treated as if it had never validly existed.

One company with one phone. {pause: 0.4} Eleven thousand people in a single case. {pause: 0.4} A European law undone back to its first day.

{tone: pointed} And nobody ever had to read a word. {tone: measured}

{pause: 1.2}

## Where this series begins {tone: lucid, note: "Orientation; open in welcome and then settle into plain explanation."}

{tone: warm} This is The Constitutional Layer, and this is its first episode. It assumes nothing, so if that case is new to you, you are in the right place. {tone: lucid}

The series is about one kind of argument. A government, or the European Union itself, does something with data about people: {pause: 0.4} it keeps it, searches it, or hands it on. Someone says a right has been limited. And a court asks a fixed set of questions to decide whether the limit is allowed.

Those questions go by the name of *proportionality*. The word means little until you have watched a court use it. So today you watch one court use it on one law, from the first question to the one that decided the case.

The series takes its bearings from one question: {pause: 0.6} what survives when the rules meet real power? {pause: 0.8} In this case the rules won outright. That makes it the right place to learn how the test works, {pause: 0.4} and a poor place to judge how often it wins.

{pause: 0.8}

Two pieces of background, a sentence each. {tone: precise} The Charter of Fundamental Rights is the European Union's own bill of rights. And the Court of Justice, which sits in Luxembourg, says what Union law means, including whether a Union law is valid at all.

## How a crowd reaches Luxembourg {tone: curious, note: "Correcting a wrong picture; thinking aloud. The definition is even and plain."}

Start with the route, because it is easy to picture wrongly.

You probably picture an appeal, with challengers who lost at home climbing to a higher court. The route was stranger than that, {pause: 0.4} because both cases were still running at home.

Each national court paused and sent a question to Luxembourg, and both did so in 2012. In short, the question was whether the European law could stand with the Charter.

{tone: precise} That move is called a reference for a preliminary ruling, or a reference for short. A national court asks the Court of Justice a question of Union law, and Luxembourg answers it. Then the case goes home, and the national court decides it with the answer in hand. {tone: curious}

{tone: pointed} So the Court of Justice decided what the European law was worth, and handed that answer back. {tone: curious}

{pause: 1.2}

{tone: wry} Whether Mr Seitlinger won was for the Austrian court. {tone: curious}

The Austrian court had put its worry plainly. As the judgment records it, the Austrian judges took the view that the retention of data, and I am quoting:

> affects almost exclusively persons whose conduct in no way justifies the retention of data relating to them.

End of quotation. On that view, almost all the people whose records were kept had done nothing to justify it.

Notice what the challengers brought to court. They had a law, {pause: 0.4} and they had read it. Nothing in their case depended on showing that anyone's records had been misused.

## What the network already knows {tone: lucid, note: "The machinery as an engineer would explain it; plain and unhurried. No alarm in it."}

Now the machinery, described the way the people who run telephone networks would describe it.

When you make a call, the network must know where it comes from and where it is going. To bill it, the operator must know when it started and how long it lasted. And to reach a mobile phone at all, the network must know roughly where the phone is.

{tone: precise} So every call, message and connection leaves records on the operator's side, and those records are called traffic data and location data. {tone: lucid}

The Court's own account matches. In its description, the data show whom a subscriber was in touch with, and by what means. They show the time and the place. And over a period, they show how often.

Now listen for what is missing: {pause: 0.8} the words. What was said in a call is not traffic data. Neither is the text of a message, or the pages someone looked at online. The European law excluded content expressly.

These records are a by-product, made because the network cannot work without them. And before the retention law, Union law said what should happen to them next. Once they were no longer needed to carry the communication, they were to be erased or made anonymous, except for as long as billing required.

The default was *forgetting*.

{pause: 0.8}

In March 2006, the Union adopted a directive on the retention of data, and it reversed that default. Operators had to keep traffic and location data for between six and twenty-four months, so that national authorities could reach it to investigate, detect and prosecute serious crime. Which crimes counted as serious, {pause: 0.4} the directive left each country to define.

{tone: precise} A directive, in a clause: {pause: 0.4} a European law that binds the Member States, each of which must write it into its own national law. {tone: lucid} That is why the Austrians attacked an Austrian statute, and why the question still led back to the directive behind it.

The directive laid a legal duty on records the operators already held.

So the system is simple to state. The network already keeps a note of every connection it carries. {tone: pointed} The European law told it to stop throwing the notes away. {tone: lucid}

{pause: 1.2}

## Keeping is enough {tone: lucid, note: "The first real idea of the episode; patient. The retrieval question at the end is a real one."}

Two rights in the Charter are engaged, and the first is Article 7. In the Charter's words:

> Everyone has the right to respect for his or her private and family life, home and communications.

That is the whole article. The second right is Article 8, which begins:

> Everyone has the right to the protection of personal data concerning him or her.

End of quotation. The article goes on to require, among other things, fair processing for specified purposes and supervision by an independent authority. Keep hold of that independent authority, {pause: 0.4} because it returns before the end.

Two rights, then: one to a private life, and a separate one to have data about you protected.

Now the word on which this case could have stalled.

Interference.

{pause: 0.8}

In ordinary speech, an interference means someone did something to you: they opened your post, or listened in, and you were harmed. On that meaning this case would look premature, because nobody had to show that a single record had been opened, or that anyone had suffered.

The Court used the word in its own way. To establish an interference with private life, it said, and I quote:

> it does not matter whether the information on the private lives concerned is sensitive or whether the persons concerned have been inconvenienced in any way.

Those are the Court's words, and it drew the consequence at once. {tone: pointed} The duty to keep the data is itself an interference. {tone: lucid}

{pause: 1.2}

The rules letting the authorities reach the data are a second interference, on top of the first.

Put in ordinary words: {pause: 0.5} the *keeping* is the interference. Nobody needs to have looked.

{pause: 0.8}

{tone: contemplative} Remember the line the opening ended on, that nobody ever had to read a word. This is its first meaning. The interference happened the moment the records were kept. {tone: lucid}

An interference is where the argument starts, and all it does is oblige the Union to justify itself.

So the Court measured it. In the Court's phrase, this interference was wide-ranging and particularly serious, and it reached, in another of its phrases, {pause: 0.4} practically the entire European population. The Court added one observation, saying it followed its Advocate General, the adviser whose opinion it had before it. I am quoting the judgment:

> the fact that data are retained and subsequently used without the subscriber or registered user being informed is likely to generate in the minds of the persons concerned the feeling that their private lives are the subject of constant surveillance.

End of quotation. What that comes to is simple: {pause: 0.4} having your records kept, and never being told, is something people feel.

{pause: 0.8}

{tone: curious} So, the question this part exists for. Suppose records of your calls were kept for two years and never once opened. Had your rights been interfered with? {tone: lucid}

{pause: 2.5}

Yes. The keeping alone is an interference. And the rules that let the authorities in are a second one, whether or not anyone used them.

## A right that can be limited {tone: measured, note: "The turn from interference to justification; even and deliberate."}

If you came in thinking a right is either violated or it is not, you would now expect the challengers to have won. There was an interference, and a particularly serious one.

{tone: wry} Case closed? {tone: measured}

{pause: 1.0}

But the Charter allows its rights to be limited, and says so in the first paragraph of Article 52. I am quoting:

> Any limitation on the exercise of the rights and freedoms recognised by this Charter must be provided for by law and respect the essence of those rights and freedoms.

End of quotation. The next sentence sets the remaining terms. Again, the Charter:

> Subject to the principle of proportionality, limitations may be made only if they are necessary and genuinely meet objectives of general interest recognised by the Union.

That is where the quotation ends. The same sentence also allows limits that protect the rights and freedoms of other people.

{tone: precise} Stripped to its working parts, the clause says three things. A right may be limited. The limit must be written in law and leave the right's core intact. And it needs a good enough reason, {pause: 0.4} pursued no further than it has to be. {tone: measured}

From now on I will call this the limitation clause.

So the right was touched, and that much is settled. What remains is whether the touching was justified.

{pause: 0.8}

What does the clause do? It lets a lawmaker limit a right, and in exchange {pause: 0.4} it makes the lawmaker give reasons, in a set order, to a court.

{tone: precise} The order runs like this. Two gates come first. The limit must be in law, and it must leave the right's core intact, which the Charter calls its essence. Then come four questions. The first two are usually easy to pass. The third is where this Court tends to decide its cases. The fourth is a final weighing. {tone: measured} I will name each one as the Court reaches it.

Two misreadings need refusing early, because both are tempting. One hears a bag of separate rules, each passed or failed on its own. The other hears a decision tree: yes, go left; no, go right; arrive at an answer.

{tone: pointed} Both miss the dial: {pause: 0.5} how serious the interference is decides how hard the later questions are pressed. {tone: measured}

{pause: 1.2}

Whether a measure is necessary can be easy to show for a small intrusion and far harder for a sweeping one. The steps run in order, but they share a setting.

## Why one clause {tone: curious, note: "A step aside to ask why; thinking aloud. The trade-off lands quietly."}

There was another way to write this.

The European Convention on Human Rights is a separate treaty, made in the Council of Europe rather than the Union. It writes its limits right by right. Its article on private life ends with a closed list of aims for which that right may be restricted, national security and the prevention of crime among them, {pause: 0.4} and no other aim will do.

The Charter chose one general clause for all its rights. The same article adds complications, one of which ties certain Charter rights, private life among them, to the Convention's standards. {tone: wry} You can set that aside. {tone: curious}

Where did the clause come from? {pause: 0.6} The official Explanations to the Charter are guidance to which the courts must give due regard. They say its wording is based on the Court of Justice's own earlier case law. The Court's habits of reasoning became the general rule of a bill of rights.

What did the Charter gain? Flexibility, because one test can meet any right and any aim. What did it give up? {pause: 0.5} Predictability. A closed list tells you in advance which reasons count. A general clause tells you only that a court will judge them.

{tone: pointed} So everything depends on how hard that court looks. {tone: curious}

{pause: 1.2}

That is why the most important sentence in this judgment concerns the dial, and says nothing about telephones.

{tone: measured} So far, two things. Keeping records about everyone interferes with two rights, before anyone looks. And the Charter permits interferences through one clause, which asks for a law, an intact core, and a good enough reason.

{pause: 0.6}

Now watch the Court run it.

## Running the test {tone: measured, note: "The core apparatus worked through in order; steady with each step audibly a step."}

{tone: precise} The first gate asks whether the limit is provided for by law. The judgment does not dwell on that gate. The limit was written into a European law, and the case turns elsewhere.

The second gate asks whether the essence of each right survives, and the Court found that both did. For private life, the reason was that the directive let no one learn the content of communications. For data protection, it was that the directive at least imposed certain principles of data security. {tone: measured}

Then the dial. You might think a court has two settings: {pause: 0.4} defer to the lawmaker, or take over. The Court described something adjustable. The lawmaker's room can shrink, it said, depending on several factors, among them the nature of the right and how serious the interference is.

It looked at how much data protection matters to private life, and at how wide and serious this interference was. Then it set the dial in these words:

> the EU legislature's discretion is reduced, with the result that review of that discretion should be strict.

End of quotation. Said plainly: {pause: 0.4} the bigger the intrusion, the less benefit of the doubt the lawmaker gets, and the harder the Court looks.

{pause: 0.8}

That is strict review, and it is set case by case. A modest interference would leave the lawmaker far more room, and the size of this one is what turned the dial.

The dial is what makes the clause bite. Without it, the clause is a form any lawmaker can fill in. With it, {pause: 0.4} the form gets harder exactly where the most is at stake.

Now the four questions.

The first is the aim. You might expect a court to interrogate the government's motives, but this one asks something more modest. Does the measure genuinely serve an objective the Union recognises as being in the general interest? The bar is low. The Court found that the directive's real aim was fighting serious crime, which in its word served "ultimately" public security.

The aim passed.

{pause: 0.6}

The second question is the fit. In everyday speech, appropriate means wise, or suitable. In this test it means only one thing: {pause: 0.5} capable of contributing to the aim.

Two names from the Austrian crowd return at this point. Mr Tschohl and Mr Seitlinger argued that several ways of communicating escaped the directive, or allowed anonymity, and the Portuguese government argued the same. The Court accepted that this limited what the measure could achieve. It still held the measure appropriate, because the records gave investigators more ways into serious crime.

To fit, a measure only has to be able to help.

{pause: 0.8}

Two questions, two passes. Had the test stopped there, {pause: 0.4} the directive would have survived.

The third question is the need. First, a reasoning error, shown on something where nothing is at stake.

{tone: wry} A town could put a speed camera on every lamp post, {pause: 0.5} and it would catch more speeding drivers. That makes the cameras useful. It does not make them *necessary*. {tone: precise} Necessary asks something narrower: {pause: 0.4} does the measure go any further than the aim requires? {tone: measured}

{pause: 0.8}

The error is to slide from one to the other. It helps, so it must be needed.

Retention helped the police, and the Court refused the slide. It accepted that fighting serious crime was, in its phrase, "of the utmost importance". {tone: pointed} Then it held, in substance, that an aim, no matter how weighty, does not by itself make a measure necessary. {tone: measured}

{pause: 1.4}

The standard it applied came from its earlier cases. I am quoting:

> derogations and limitations in relation to the protection of personal data must apply only in so far as is strictly necessary.

End of quotation. In other words: {pause: 0.4} no wider than the aim requires. In this judgment, that meant asking whether the directive went wider than its aim in whom it covered, in what data it kept, or in how long it kept it.

That is what strictly necessary means in this Court. Useful is a different test, and a far easier one.

## Four defects, in two pairs {tone: lucid, note: "Structure first; each pair set out cleanly so it can be held."}

The Court found four defects. Three show the directive wider than its aim. The fourth shows it guarded too weakly. They come in two pairs, and they are easier to hold that way.

The first pair is about who gets caught.

Defect one: {pause: 0.5} everyone did. In the judgment's words, the directive covered:

> all persons and all means of electronic communication as well as all traffic data without any differentiation, limitation or exception being made in the light of the objective of fighting against serious crime.

That is the judgment. It applied even to people with no link to serious crime, not even an indirect or remote one. And it made no exception for communications that national law protects with professional secrecy.

That is: nobody's records were kept because of any connection to crime. *Everyone's* were.

{pause: 0.8}

Defect two is about getting in. Nothing in the directive limited access to crimes serious enough to justify it; it simply pointed to serious crime, as each country defined it. And above all, {pause: 0.4} access did not depend on a prior review by a court or an independent body.

So the first pair: everyone's records are kept, and nobody independent stands at the door.

{pause: 1.0}

The second pair is about how long, and how safely.

Defect three is time. The data were kept for six to twenty-four months, with no distinction between kinds of data or kinds of people. Why six and not three? Why twenty-four and not twelve? {pause: 0.6} The directive did not require the period to rest on objective criteria at all.

Defect four is protection. The directive let operators, in the Court's words, "have regard to economic considerations" when deciding how much security to apply. Nothing guaranteed that the data would be destroyed for good at the end. And nothing required the data to stay inside the Union, so supervision by the independent authority that the data-protection right demands could not, the Court held, be said to be fully ensured. That is where the independent authority returns.

{tone: warm} One thing in that may sound like a contradiction. The security rules found too weak here are the same ones that, at the gate, kept the core of data protection intact. On my reading, the two findings fit: {pause: 0.4} the gate asks whether any protection exists, and the test of need asks whether it is enough. The Court itself does not remark on it. {tone: lucid}

So the second pair: kept for an unexplained time, and guarded to a price.

{pause: 0.8}

Now turn the four defects around, and each tells the lawmaker what was missing. The first pair becomes: keep only what has some link to serious crime, and let nobody in without an independent check first. The second becomes: justify how long you keep it, and protect it properly while you do.

So the judgment does two things at once. It strikes the directive down. {tone: pointed} And it hands the lawmaker a design brief. {tone: lucid}

{pause: 1.2}

Hold on to that second thing. I will come back to it.

{tone: curious} Before going on, see whether the pairs stayed with you. What were they? {tone: lucid}

{pause: 2.5}

The first pair was who gets caught: everyone, with nobody independent at the door. The second was how long and how safely: no reason for the period, and protection set to a budget.

## The other side, at its best {tone: measured, note: "Give the opposing case its full strength; fair and unhurried with no irony."}

The case for the directive has a holder, and the judgment names it. The directive's own preamble records a conclusion of the Union's Justice and Home Affairs Council, from the nineteenth of December 2002. As the judgment sets it out, the Council concluded that, because of the growth in what electronic communications made possible, data about their use are, and I am quoting the judgment:

> particularly important and therefore a valuable tool in the prevention of offences and the fight against crime, in particular organised crime.

End of quotation. Put at its strongest, in my words: {pause: 0.4} investigators cannot know in advance whose records they will need, because next year's suspect is, {pause: 0.4} today, an ordinary subscriber. Keep only the records of people already linked to crime, and you have nothing on whoever becomes a suspect tomorrow. On that view, keeping everyone's records is the point.

{pause: 0.8}

The Court granted the value of the records. Then it named ways a scheme could be narrowed: tied to a particular period, a particular area, or a circle of people likely to be involved in serious crime. Any of those could still catch someone not yet suspected, and the directive used none of them.

Would a narrowed scheme catch enough? {pause: 0.8} This judgment does not settle that. {tone: pointed} What it settles is that the directive did not try. {tone: measured}

{pause: 1.2}

The conclusion followed. In adopting the directive, the Court held, the Union's legislature had exceeded the limits that proportionality imposes. The directive was invalid.

## The weighing that never came {tone: lucid, note: "A retrieval question first; then a correction made patiently."}

So which question decided this case? There are two candidates. Either the directive was a reasonable tool that cost too much privacy on a final weighing. Or it was wider than its aim required. Which?

{pause: 2.5}

The second.

{tone: pointed} It failed on need. {tone: lucid}

{pause: 1.2}

That matters because of a phrase you will meet everywhere in this subject: {pause: 0.4} proportionality in the narrow sense. It is easy to hear that as the name of the whole test, or as a word for fairness. {tone: precise} It names only the last question, the final weighing of what a measure costs against what it achieves. {tone: lucid}

The Court stated the test in two parts, appropriate and necessary, so in this judgment the weighing never gets a paragraph of its own. The Court went from the necessity defects straight to its conclusion. On my reading, there was nothing left for a weighing to decide.

So if you hear that this directive fell because the weighing went against it, correct the account: {pause: 0.5} it lost on need, and the scales never came out.

## Back to the first day {tone: measured, note: "The remedy stated plainly; hold the weight back for the final line."}

Now the remedy, and one more ordinary meaning to correct.

When a law is struck down, you might assume it stops applying from that day on, like a repeal. The judgment's ruling is a single sentence. It says the directive, and these are its words, "is invalid". It sets no limit in time.

The Court's press release spelled out what follows. In its words:

> Given that the Court has not limited the temporal effect of its judgment, the declaration of invalidity takes effect from the date on which the directive entered into force.

That ends the press release's sentence. It is the press office stating the consequence, and the judgment itself says nothing about time either way.

Meaning: {pause: 0.4} the directive is treated as invalid from its first day in force.

{pause: 1.0}

{tone: contemplative} Eleven thousand people in Austria had challenged one law. Their court asked whether the European law behind it was valid.

{pause: 0.8}

{tone: dramatic} The answer was that, as a matter of Union law, {pause: 0.7} it never had been. {tone: measured}

{pause: 1.6}

## Change one fact {tone: curious, note: "Experimental and light on its feet; change one variable and listen."}

The edge of a test becomes audible when you change one fact and listen for what moves.

First change. Rewrite the directive to keep records only for particular people, places or periods with some link to serious crime. The first defect disappears, {pause: 0.4} but the case does not flip. Access is still unchecked, the period still unexplained, the protection still priced.

Second change. Keep everything else, but let the authorities in only after review by a court or an independent body. The second defect disappears, and again the other three remain.

So the defects add up, and fixing one of them saves nothing. Fix all four, and you have the outline the Court's defects point to. Whether the Court would then have approved it, {pause: 0.4} this judgment does not say.

Each of those changes is the design brief, applied one line at a time.

{pause: 0.8}

Third change, {pause: 0.5} and this one is different in kind. Suppose the directive had required operators to keep what was said: {pause: 0.4} the content itself. The reason the Court gave for the core of private life being intact would have gone. And a measure that touches the core fails at the gate, with no question of need and no weighing at all.

{tone: pointed} The case would have changed category. {tone: curious}

{pause: 1.2}

A measure judged on its need would have become a measure that never reaches the questions.

## A budget line {tone: contemplative, note: "A second look at one defect; reflective. Let the one-word line stand alone."}

One defect deserves a second look, for the level it sits at.

Cost.

{pause: 0.8}

On its face, how much an operator spends on securing data is an economic question. The Court treated it as a rights question. The law let private firms set the protection of everyone's records partly by what that protection would cost them, {pause: 0.4} and a law like that had not protected those records enough.

{tone: pointed} A budget line inside a security scheme had become a constitutional question. {tone: contemplative}

{pause: 1.2}

{tone: curious} But economics is where the defect became visible, and something produced it. Push back one step. Why were private companies deciding how well these records were guarded? {pause: 0.6} Because of how the scheme was built. The state did not gather the records itself. It ordered the firms that already held them to stop deleting them, so the cost of keeping them, and their security, were decided where the records sat.

That is an organisational choice by the lawmaker, and the cost defect follows from it. One step back is enough.

{tone: measured} So far, three things. Keeping records is itself an interference. The limitation clause let the Court accept the aim and the fit, and then fail the directive on need. And the four ways it failed read, in reverse, as a design brief.

## What the test assumes {tone: measured, note: "Critical but even-handed; the map-of-a-life line is the narrator's own and should sound it."}

Every test carries assumptions about the thing it judges. This one carries two.

The first is about power. The test assumes power arrives as a text: {pause: 0.4} a law with a purpose, a period and a class of data, which a court can read for its limits. That is what the Court did. It examined the directive's words. It did not ask who had looked at whose records, in which country, or how often, and the test did not need to.

Remember the challengers, who had a law and had read it. The test suited them, because it reads design. It reads *only* design.

{pause: 1.0}

The second assumption is about the machine, and it brings back the opening line a second time. {pause: 0.5} Nobody ever had to read a word. That made the interference real without any looking. It is also why the core of private life was found untouched, because the directive let no one learn content.

If, when you heard that no words were kept, you thought the records must be harmless, this is the correction. Set that untouched core beside what the Court said these records reveal. I am quoting:

> Those data, taken as a whole, may allow very precise conclusions to be drawn concerning the private lives of the persons whose data has been retained.

End of quotation. The Court goes on to give six examples, and two will do: {pause: 0.4} where people live, and the relationships they keep.

{tone: contemplative} In my words, not the Court's: {pause: 0.5} a year of those records is a map of a life. {tone: measured}
