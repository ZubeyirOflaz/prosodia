*Planned 9 October 2026 from `projects/constitutional/research/`. `01_operative_text.md` (the Charter, the Convention and Regulation (EU) 2024/900) outranks everything else. `02_the_surveillance_cases.md` holds the seven surveillance judgments. The series is standalone: it presupposes nothing from Series A, B or D, and it re-teaches nothing from them.*

**Provenance markers.**
- **✓** — the docket holds the words verbatim, and they can be quoted.
- **▲** — the docket names or cites the item but does not hold its words. Use it as a pointer, never as a quotation.
- **°** — listed so that the map is complete, and not taught.
- **`[OUTSIDE DOCKET: …]`** — not in this docket at all. The bracket says what would confirm it. **The marker is never spoken, and no episode teaches an OUTSIDE DOCKET item as its substance until the docket holds it.**

**Three things this plan does not pretend.**
1. **The docket supports four episodes and part of a fifth.** It holds the operative text and the surveillance cases. Predictive policing, biometrics, elections, emergency powers and the proportionality debate appear only on the docket's own "still to research" list. This plan says so episode by episode: Episodes 1–4 are writable, and Episodes 5–9 are planned to structure and blocked on named docket requests.
2. **Where I name something the docket lacks, I located it on 9 October 2026 by web search against a gap the docket flags.** That makes it a lead for the docket, not docket material, and it is marked every time.
3. **The plan has nine episodes, not the roadmap's "~8".** The reason and the trade are given under the length arithmetic.

## The through-line

Each episode puts one exercise of public power over data in front of a court. It then asks what the proportionality test actually did to that power: **stopped it, reshaped it, or signed it off.** The test is taught once, properly, as the questions a court asks. After that, every episode re-tests one facet of it against a different kind of power: a legislature, a foreign intelligence service, a security state pushing back, a signals agency, a police camera network, a screening machine, an election and an emergency.

The held verdict is the roadmap's question: **is proportionality a real constraint on state power over data, or a form that ratifies whatever was going to happen?** It is held until **Episode 9**, and the listener is told so in Episode 1.

### The openings, one line each — the repeated-move check

1. A directive that eleven thousand people sued over is voided back to the day it took effect.
2. A regulator turns away a complaint because Brussels has already answered the question, and the Court rules that only the Court can answer it.
3. Governments that have lost twice argue the question is not the Court's at all, and get a ladder instead of a ban.
4. The people who exposed the programme win, and the judgment says the programme may continue.
5. The sentence that decides whether a state may keep everyone's records was drafted from a judgment about running a market.
6. A man stands alone in a metro carriage holding a cardboard figure, and days later the cameras find him.
7. Every air passenger is checked against a list before departure, and the Court lets the checking continue on condition that the list cannot rewrite itself.
8. A court cancels an election two days before the run-off, to protect the election.
9. A teacher convicted over an app on his phone wins on the one article the emergency could not suspend.

**Moves audited, not labels.**
- **Episodes 1 and 4 both open on a win.** In 1 the win is total. In 4 the win confirms what was challenged. The listener hears opposite things.
- **Episodes 3 and 7 both end in permission with conditions.** In 3 the move is the state's counter-attack on jurisdiction. In 7 the move is a machine's capacity being excluded.
- **No two openings share a move.**
- **Against Series B:** Series B's Episode 2 opens on a regulator going to court to stop a training run. Episode 2 here opens on a regulator refusing to act, the opposite move.

### Length arithmetic

**Planned lengths:** Ep 1: 40 · Ep 2: 36 · Ep 3: 38 · Ep 4: 38 · Ep 5: 26 · Ep 6: 32 · Ep 7: 36 · Ep 8: 38 · Ep 9: 44.

## The lens admission test, and why there is only one

**There is one lens episode in nine, below the one-in-four the ratio predicts.** In this series the conceptual machinery — the proportionality test — is *applied* by courts. It is therefore apparatus, and Episode 1 teaches it from a single case. What is genuinely a lens is the argument about whether that test protects rights at all, and that is **Episode 5**, whose admission test is worked inside its section.

**A second lens was considered and rejected.** The candidate was "what counts as an interference when nobody looks" — the chilling effect, and the harm of being retained rather than read. It would judge rather than apply, and Episodes 6 and 8 would invoke it. It fails condition two: *Digital Rights Ireland* illustrates it fully, so Episode 1 carries it. Whether anyone holds the opposing view in a citable place is also not in the docket. **It is folded into Episode 1** as the "interference" term, and recalled in Episode 6.

- **Allowed only under conditions:**
  - **"Big Brother"** — only inside the NGO's name, or inside a named critic's quotation.
  - **"Black box"** — only as a gloss on the French nickname *boîtes noires*, once.
  - **"Chilling"** — only as the legal term "chilling effect", after Episode 6 has earned it.
- **Inherited from Series B's prompt-echo findings, also off-limits:**
  - "before any rule touches"
  - "in the same breath"
  - "you do not need that today"
  - "I'm not going to read you"

- **Reserved:** the series question, *what survives when the rules meet real power*, may be spoken in Episodes 1 and 9 only.

**Required, and kept:** a plain restatement after every formulation from an instrument or a judgment. **Only its wording varies.** The same construction is never used twice in an episode, and never in two consecutive episodes.

**Numbers.** Case and instrument numbers are not spoken in the body. The renderer reads "slash" numbers inconsistently (`series-b-findings.md` §12). Speak the name of the case, the court and the year. In the closing sources, write any number in words or omit it.

**The `dramatic` tone, at most once per episode, where a right is lost or a power upheld:**
- **Ep 1** — the directive treated as never having been valid.

## Episode 1 — Who Called Whom

**Length:** 40 min. This is long because the episode carries the test itself: the questions, the dial that sets how hard a court looks, and the four defects every later episode checks a new measure against. It also carries ten load-bearing terms and the series' promise.

**Prerequisites:** none. The series starts cold. Each of the following is earned in a clause where it arises, never as a preamble:
- that the Charter is the Union's bill of rights;
- that the Court of Justice sits in Luxembourg;
- that a directive binds the Member States, which must write it into national law.

**Opening instance** — *type: a decided case.* ✓ (docket 02 §1)
- **The case.** *Digital Rights Ireland* and *Kärntner Landesregierung*, Court of Justice (Grand Chamber), 8 April 2014.
- **The challengers.** In Ireland, an Irish company, Digital Rights Ireland, against the Irish authorities. In Austria, the Government of the Province of Carinthia, and "Mr Seitlinger, Mr Tschohl and 11 128 other applicants". That is **eleven thousand one hundred and thirty people** suing over one directive.
- **The measure.** Directive 2006/24/EC required providers to keep traffic and location data for six to twenty-four months.

*The move: open on the size of the crowd and the size of the remedy. Eleven thousand named people in one country and one company in another bring a European law down so completely that it is treated as never having been valid.*

**Load-bearing terms.** For each, a listener who holds the ordinary meaning reaches a wrong answer on something this episode teaches.
- **Interference.** *Wrong: someone read my data, or I was harmed.* Right: **keeping** the data is itself an interference with private life and with data protection, and access by the authorities is a second interference. Nobody needs to have looked. The wrong reading makes the whole case look premature.
- **Traffic and location data.** *Wrong: harmless, because it is not what was said.* Right: the docket's field definition — who communicated with whom, by what means, when, from where, how often. Taken as a whole, it gives "very precise information on the private lives of the persons whose data are retained" ✓.
- **Limitation** (Art. 52(1) ✓). *Wrong: a right is either violated or it isn't.* Right: Charter rights may be limited lawfully. The question is never whether the right was touched, but whether the touching was justified. The wrong reading predicts that any interference wins the case.
- **Objective of general interest.** *Wrong: the Court second-guesses the government's aims.* Right: a low threshold, and it was passed here — fighting serious crime, and ultimately public security ✓.
- **Appropriate.** *Wrong: wise policy.* Right: capable of contributing to the aim. Passed ✓.
- **Strictly necessary.** *Wrong: useful, or important.* Right: no wider than the aim requires — differentiated by person, by data and by time. **Failed** ✓. The wrong reading concludes that because retention helps the police, it is necessary.
- **Proportionate in the narrow sense.** *Wrong: the name of the whole test, or "fair".* Right: the final weighing, which this case **never reached**. A listener who conflates the two will report the case as lost on balance, and it was not.
- **Strict review.** *Wrong: courts either defer or take over.* Right: the more serious the interference, the less discretion the legislature has, and the harder the Court looks ✓. This is what turns the test from a formula into a constraint.
- **Invalid, without temporal limit.** *Wrong: repealed from now on.* Right: invalid from the date it entered into force; the Court declined to limit the effect ✓.
- **A reference from a national court.** *Wrong: an appeal.* Right: a national court asks Luxembourg a question of Union law, Luxembourg answers it, and the case goes home. This is how an Austrian crowd ends up in Luxembourg.

**The cast.**
- the challengers;
- the courts in Ireland and Austria that asked the question;
- the Union legislature, which wrote the directive;
- the **providers**, compelled to keep the data;
- the **national authorities**, who may access it;
- the Court of Justice.

**The system, described operationally, before any rule applies.**
- Every call, message and connection already generates a record on the provider's side: the parties, the means, the time, the location, the frequency.
- The directive turned that by-product into a duty: keep it for six to twenty-four months, for possible access by national authorities fighting serious crime.
- **No content is kept.** The definition of "serious crime" was left to each Member State.
- **Field definition, to check the description against:** docket 02's list of what traffic and location data reveal ✓.
- **Does the system exist?** Yes. It is a legal obligation on records providers already hold. There is no technical doubt.

**The operative text.**

> **Charter Art. 52(1) ✓** — "Any limitation on the exercise of the rights and freedoms recognised by this Charter must be provided for by law and respect the essence of those rights and freedoms."

Then the second sentence, of which only the working words are given: limitations are "subject to the principle of proportionality", must be "necessary", and must "genuinely meet objectives of general interest recognised by the Union". Say the rest as a claim.

> **Charter Art. 7 ✓** — "Everyone has the right to respect for his or her private and family life, home and communications."
>
> **Charter Art. 8(1) ✓** — "Everyone has the right to the protection of personal data concerning him or her."

**What the rule is called afterwards:** **the limitation clause**. Its questions are **the aim, the fit, the need and the weighing**. Two gates come first: the limitation must be **in law**, and it must leave **the essence** intact. Both gates are named here in a clause each and taught later. The essence gets **one clause only**: the Court checked first whether the core was touched, and said no, because no content was kept.

**The four defects — the spoken path.** Never a list of four. Two pairs:
1. **Two defects about who is caught.** It covered "all individuals, all means of electronic communication and all traffic data without any differentiation, limitation or exception" ✓. And nothing confined access to serious crime or required a prior check by a court or an independent body.
2. **Two defects about how long and how safely.** The retention period had no criteria, being anywhere from six to twenty-four months with no reason given for either. And the safeguards were weak: security could be set with regard to **cost**, there was no irreversible destruction at the end, and the data could leave the Union.

**Claim that must land:** these four defects are a **design brief** — the Court told the legislature what a lawful scheme would look like. Keep that sentence's *substance*. The verdict depends on it.

**Why this rule and not another.**
- **The road not taken.** The Convention lists, right by right, the only aims for which each right may be restricted ✓ (its Art. 8(2)). The Charter instead uses **one general limitation clause for every right**.
- **Where the clause came from.** Per the Explanations, its wording was taken from the Court's earlier case law ✓.
- **What was traded.** The Charter gained flexibility and gave up predictability. Everything therefore depends on how strictly the Court runs the clause — which is why the scrutiny dial is the most important sentence in the judgment.

**The variants — change one fact.**
1. **Retention limited to people or places with some link to serious crime.** The first defect goes, and the case turns on the remaining three.
2. **Access only after review by a court or an independent body.** The second defect goes.
3. **The directive requires keeping content.** The Court would not have needed to weigh anything — the core of the right is touched. *Say this, and stop. It is the next episode's apparatus. Do not number it.*

**The levels — two that interact.** *Economic*: the directive let security be set with regard to cost. *Rights-based*: the Court treated that cost choice as a rights defect. **The claim:** a budget line inside a security scheme is a constitutional question.

**The contested joints.**
- **Retroactive invalidity.** *Settled.*
- **Whether metadata is less than content.** *Settled as law here* — the essence was untouched because no content was kept. *Contested as reasoning*: the Court's own description of what metadata reveals sits uneasily with that finding. **This framing is the series', and the script must say so.**
- **Whether any general retention could ever be strictly necessary.** *Still moving.* The Court will be asked again. **Do not name when.**

**What the rule assumes about the power — and the machine.**
- **The power.** The test assumes power arrives as a **text** that can be read for its limits: a directive with a purpose, a period and a class of data. It reads the law's design, not how the access is actually used.
- **The machine.** The essence line assumes **content** is the core of private life. The Court's own account of metadata says it maps a life: habits, movements, relationships.
- **Claim that must land:** the Court found the core of the right untouched by a measure it described as capable of mapping a person's whole life, because the line it drew is about the **kind** of data, not about what the data reveals.

**As written against as enforced.**
- **The directive fell.** The national laws that implemented it stayed on the books, which is why the same question came back. *Say that it came back; do not say when.*
- **Who audits.** No regulator does. Only litigation.

**What it deliberately compresses, said aloud.**
- the national laws that implemented the directive;
- the other European rights court — *not mentioned at all here*;
- the essence, beyond its one clause.

**Spoken deferral #1, said once:** this series is about rights and the courts that test limits on them. It is not about the AI Act's machinery, the data-protection rulebook as an instrument, or how organisations and the rest of the world govern AI — other series in this production take those.

**Say that the verdict is held, and why.** Near the close, the series names its question: is proportionality a real constraint on state power over data, or a form that ratifies whatever was going to happen? This case looks like the first answer. The listener is asked to hold that judgment, because one strike-down shows what the test *can* do, not what it does when states push back. **Episode 9** lands the answer. *This is numbered pointer one of two.*

**The handover.** The Court said the core was untouched because nobody read content. What happens when someone does — and when that someone is another country's intelligence service?

**Transfer questions.**
1. What is kept about everyone, and what has to happen before it is opened?
2. Is this restriction necessary, or only useful?
3. Who is swept in who has no connection to the stated purpose?
4. Who checks before access, and are they independent of the people asking?
5. Where does the data sit, and who could reach it there?

## Episode 2 — The Floor

**Length:** 36 min.

**Prerequisites:** Ep 1 — the limitation clause, strict necessity, the content/metadata line, and the essence named as a gate.

**Opening instance** — *type: a refused complaint.* ✓ (docket 02 §4)
- **The complainant.** **Maximillian Schrems**, "an Austrian citizen", a Facebook user since 2008. His data went from **Facebook's Irish subsidiary** to servers in the **United States**.
- **The complaint.** After **Edward Snowden's 2013 revelations** about the **NSA**, he complained to the **Irish Data Protection Commissioner**.
- **The refusal.** The Commissioner rejected the complaint: the Commission's **Safe Harbour Decision** of 26 July 2000 had already found US protection adequate.

*The move: a regulator turns a complaint away because Brussels answered the question years earlier, and the Court rules that nobody but the Court can give that answer.*

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **The essence.** *Wrong: the most important part of the right, weighed heavily; or simply "the gist".* Right: **a floor**. A measure that touches it fails, and nothing is weighed. The wrong reading has Safe Harbour falling because the balance tipped. It did not.
- **Content, against traffic data.** *Wrong: the line is about how much data.* Right: it is about **kind**. Generalised access to **content** compromises the essence ✓. The metadata in the last case did not.
- **Adequacy decision.** *Wrong: a treaty with the United States, or a certificate that US law is good.* Right: a Commission decision finding that a third country's protection is adequate. It permits transfers, and it binds until the Court invalidates it.
- **Essentially equivalent** ✓. *Wrong: identical; or merely "good enough".* Right: equivalent in substance to Union protection read with the Charter — which includes the necessity test.
- **Effective judicial protection** (Charter Art. 47 ▲ — text not held; the Explanation ✓ ties 47(2) and (3) to the Convention's fair-trial article). *Wrong: a complaints channel exists.* Right: an independent body that can **bind**. Having no legal remedy to access, rectify or erase one's data "compromises the essence of the fundamental right to effective judicial protection" ✓.
- **The regulator's power to examine.** *Wrong: a national regulator must apply the Commission's finding.* Right: it must examine the complaint. Only the Court can invalidate the Commission's decision, so the regulator and the complainant must be able to reach the Court ✓. The hook in the text is **Art. 8(3) ✓**: compliance "shall be subject to control by an independent authority".
- **Standard contractual clauses.** *Wrong: boilerplate that makes any transfer lawful.* Right: they are valid **only because** they oblige the exporter and the recipient to check the destination's protection **before** any transfer, and to **suspend** if it fails ✓. The burden lands on a company.

**The cast.**
- Schrems;
- Facebook Ireland, the exporter;
- the US servers and the US public authorities;
- the Irish Commissioner;
- the Irish High Court, which referred the second case;
- the Commission, author of three adequacy decisions;
- the **Ombudsperson**;
- **Philippe Latombe**, a French member of parliament;
- the General Court.

**The system, described operationally.**
- European users' data is copied continuously from the Irish subsidiary to servers in the United States.
- Under US law, public authorities can reach data held by companies for national security.
- Safe Harbour bound **companies** and not **US public authorities**, and it yielded to US national security "without limitation" ✓.
- **Does it exist?** Yes. The transfers are the subsidiary's ordinary operation.

**The operative text.**
- **Charter Art. 52(1) ✓, recalled.** Only the phrase "respect the essence" — now taught as the first gate.
- **Art. 8(3) ✓.**
- **The Schrems I holding, from the Court's summary ✓.** Legislation permitting authorities "to have access on a generalised basis to the content of electronic communications must be regarded as compromising the essence of the fundamental right to respect for private life".
- **Art. 47 is not quoted** — its text is not held.

**What the rules are called afterwards:** **the floor** (the essence) and **the equivalence test**.

**Then the sequence, with no dates read as a list.**
1. **2015: Safe Harbour falls.**
2. **The complaint returns.** Schrems reformulates it against the standard contractual clauses (2010/87 ✓). Meanwhile the Commission adopts **Privacy Shield** (2016/1250 ✓).
3. **2020: Privacy Shield falls ✓.** The clauses survive. Privacy Shield gave US national security "primacy". Its limits were "not limited to what is strictly necessary" ✓. Its **Ombudsperson** was not independent and could not bind the intelligence services.
4. **A third framework follows a new American executive order.** The Commission's decision of **July 2023** ✓ was challenged by Latombe. **The General Court dismissed the action on 3 September 2025 ✓.** An appeal is pending `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: appeal lodged 31 October 2025 as Case C-703/25 P, seeking annulment of Implementing Decision (EU) 2023/1795 of 10 July 2023 — OJ notice C/2025/6610; no ruling found as at 9 October 2026]`. **The programmes Privacy Shield covered are not named**: docket 02 says to take their names from the judgment. The executive order is described, not dated.

**Why this rule and not another.**
- **The essence clause.** It gives the Charter an **absolute core inside a weighing test**. The road not taken was pure balancing, in which anything can be outweighed. The trade: predictability at the core, at the risk that a court keeps the core narrow so that it stays rare.
- **Adequacy.** It is a **unilateral Commission finding**, not a treaty. That is why the Court can review it — and why it has been fragile twice.

**The variants — change one fact.**
1. **US law reaches traffic data only, not content.** There is no essence finding. The case goes the first episode's way, to necessity — and the same judgment already found that storing everything "without any differentiation, limitation or exception" is not strictly necessary ✓. **Claim:** the floor and the weighing are separate routes, and this case failed on both.
2. **The redress body is independent and can bind the intelligence services.** The remedy defect is cured. That is precisely what the 2023 framework claims to do.
3. **An exporter using the clauses finds the destination's law falls short.** It must suspend. **The clauses survive; the transfer does not.**

**The levels — two that interact.** *Legal*: the test is applied to a foreign legal order. *Economic*: transatlantic data flows. **The claim:** the test can reach a power the Union cannot command only by **stopping the flow** — the remedy the economy cannot accept. That is why there have been three frameworks.

**The contested joints.**
- **Whether the essence is a real separate step, or a name for "very disproportionate".** *Contested.* Do not resolve it here.
- **Whether the third framework meets the standard.** The General Court said yes. *Still moving*, pending appeal.
- **Whether the clauses hand a state's job to companies.** *Contested.*

**What the rule assumes about the power — and the machine.**
- **The power.** The test assumes it can judge a legal order by **reading its paperwork**. The Commission read the scheme, which bound companies, while the power that mattered sat with authorities the scheme did not bind.
- **The machine.** The contractual clauses assume a private exporter can audit a foreign intelligence system before each transfer, and pause. **The machine is a continuous copy, not a series of sendings.**
- **Claim that must land:** twice, the Court found that the document examined was not where the power was.

**As written against as enforced.**
- Regulators must examine complaints. Since 2020, every exporter must assess the destination itself.
- **The docket holds no figure for suspended transfers, and the script must say none is available rather than guess.**

**Spoken deferral #2, said once:** these transfers now sit under the data-protection rulebook's own chapter on transfers — another series' instrument. *Permission to forget.*

**What it deliberately compresses.**
- the names of the US programmes;
- the executive order's content, beyond "a new redress mechanism";
- how the Irish regulator came to be the one deciding.

**The handover.** Luxembourg has now said no to general data powers three times. Next, governments come back with an argument about **who decides what national security needs**.

**Transfer questions.**
1. Does this reach what I said, or only the traces of my saying it?
2. If a foreign authority can reach this data, where can I go that can bind it?
3. Is the check done before the data moves — and by whom?
4. Is the protection in the document someone examined, or in the place where the power actually sits?

## Episode 3 — The Ladder

**Length:** 38 min. The ladder has to be built on air — three rungs, two tools that are always available, and one narrow door — and the automated-analysis conditions need room.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 1, 2 — the four defects; strict necessity; the floor; review by an independent body.

**Opening instance** — *type: a decided case.* ✓ (docket 02 §3)
- **The cases.** *La Quadrature du Net*, *French Data Network* and others, before France's **Conseil d'État**. The association of French- and German-speaking **Belgian bar associations**, before the **Belgian Constitutional Court**.
- **Decided together.** Court of Justice (Grand Chamber), **6 October 2020**. *Privacy International*, referred by the UK's **Investigatory Powers Tribunal**, was delivered the same day.
- **Why it came back.** In the Court's summary, "certain States" feared they had been "deprived of an instrument which they consider necessary to safeguard national security and to combat crime" ✓.

*The move: governments that have lost twice come back arguing the question isn't the Court's to answer — and the Court answers not with a third ban but with a ladder.*

**The bridge, in one sentence only:** in 2016, in *Tele2 Sverige and Watson*, the Court said to the Member States what it had said to the Union legislature — **national** laws requiring general and indiscriminate retention are precluded ✓. *Name no individual party.*

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **Implementing Union law** (Art. 51(1) ✓). *Wrong: the Charter governs everything a European state does.* Right: it binds Member States "only when they are implementing Union law" ✓.
  - The states argued that national security is outside Union law ✓ (Art. 4(2) TEU). The Court disagreed: laws obliging **providers** to retain or forward data fall within the e-Privacy Directive ✓, so the Charter applies.
  - **The wrong reading makes the jurisdiction fight unintelligible.**
- **National security, against serious crime, against public security.** *Wrong: synonyms.* Right: different rungs, each carrying a different ceiling.
- **General and indiscriminate retention.** *Wrong: keeping everything forever.* Right: keeping **everyone's** traffic and location data with no link to a threat — **even if time-limited**.
- **Targeted retention.** *Wrong: keeping suspects' data.* Right: retention limited by "objective and non-discriminatory factors" ✓ — categories of persons, or **a geographical criterion**. It can cover everyone in an area.
- **A threat that is "genuine and present or foreseeable"** ✓. *Wrong: any risk a government asserts.* Right: a serious threat to national security, with retention "limited in time to what is strictly necessary" ✓, renewable while the threat persists.
- **Binding review.** *Wrong: an oversight body that advises.* Right: a court, or an independent administrative body, **whose decision is binding** ✓.
- **Quick freeze** (expedited retention) ✓. *Wrong: freezing someone's assets.* Right: an order to **preserve data already held**, once an offence is established or reasonably suspected.
- **Automated analysis.** *Wrong: a machine deciding, or an analyst using software.* Right: running models and criteria fixed in advance across **all users'** traffic and location data, to flag people. The conditions are in the next paragraph.
- **Real-time collection.** *Wrong: live surveillance of everyone.* Right: limited, for terrorism, to persons with "a valid reason to suspect" ✓ involvement, with prior review.

**The ladder — the spoken path.** Three rungs, then two tools, then one door. No table.
1. **First, any purpose:** general and indiscriminate retention as a preventive measure is **not** allowed. That is confirmed.
2. **The top rung — a serious threat to national security:** general and indiscriminate retention **is** allowed. It must be time-limited, renewable, and subject to binding review.
3. **The middle rung — serious crime and serious threats to public security:** **targeted** retention only.
4. **Two tools available on any rung:** general retention of **IP addresses** for a limited time and of **civil-identity data** without one; and **quick freeze**.
5. **One narrow door:** real-time collection, for named suspects only.

**The machine, in the judgment.**
- Under the national-security condition, the directive also does not preclude "the automated analysis of the data, inter alia traffic and location data, of all users of means of electronic communication" ✓.
- This is France's intelligence-law algorithmic analysis — the *boîtes noires*, the black boxes ✓. **Do not speak the French law's provision or year**: docket 02 says both must come from the judgment's recital (R2).
- **The conditions** `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: located by the planner in a mirror of the judgment text (data.guardint.org) as paragraphs 180–182; confirm against the CURIA/CELLAR text of C-511/18 — R2]`:
  - the models and criteria must be **specific and reliable**, and **non-discriminatory**;
  - models built on the premise that origin, opinions or religion are in themselves relevant would breach the private-life and data articles read with the Charter's non-discrimination article ▲;

**Evidence, in one sentence.** Criminal courts must disregard evidence from unlawful general retention where the accused "are not in a position to comment effectively" ✓ on it.

**The cast.**

- Privacy International, *in one clause*;
- "certain States";

**The system, described operationally.**

## Episode 4 — The Quality of the Law

**Length:** 38 min. Ten load-bearing terms, a second court introduced from nothing, and the bridge between the two rights instruments.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 1, 3 — strict necessity; traffic data; the ladder's acceptance of general measures under national security.

**Opening instance** — *type: a leak, then litigation.* ✓ (docket 02 §6)
- **The case.** *Big Brother Watch and Others v the United Kingdom*, European Court of Human Rights (Grand Chamber), **25 May 2021**: three joined applications by civil-liberties organisations, journalists and human-rights groups, among them **Big Brother Watch** and the **Bureau of Investigative Journalism**. *Name no individual applicant* (R4).
- **The measure.** The UK's bulk interception regime under section 8(4) of the **Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000**, revealed by the Snowden disclosures. Also the acquisition of communications data from providers, and the receipt of intelligence from foreign services.

*The move: the people who exposed the programme win in the Grand Chamber — and the judgment confirms that the programme may continue.*

**Introduce the second court here, from nothing.** The Council of Europe's Convention, and its court in Strasbourg, are a different system with a different membership.
- **The bridge is Charter Art. 52(3) ✓.** Where a Charter right corresponds to a Convention right, "the meaning and scope of those rights shall be the same as those laid down by the said Convention".
- The Explanations ✓ list Charter Art. 7 as corresponding to Convention Art. 8, and Charter Art. 11 to Convention Art. 10.
- The Charter may go further ✓, and the Explanation adds: "In any event, the level of protection afforded by the Charter may never be lower than that guaranteed by the ECHR." ✓
- The Explanations themselves carry weight, because courts must give them "due regard" ✓ (52(7)).

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **In accordance with the law** (Art. 8(2) ✓). *Wrong: there is a statute.* Right: the law's **quality** — accessible, foreseeable, with safeguards against arbitrariness. For secret surveillance, most of the case is decided at this step ✓.
- **Necessary in a democratic society** ✓. *Wrong: needed, in the ordinary sense.* Right: Strasbourg's version of the weighing, run inside a margin of deference.
- **Margin of appreciation.** *Wrong: states may do as they like; or a fixed amount of discretion.* Right: a zone of deference that **varies**, and is wide for national security. Protocol No. 15 wrote it into the Convention's preamble ▲ (text not held — R1). *Name the recital; do not quote it.*
- **Bulk interception.** *Wrong: reading everyone's messages.* Right: acquiring communications in volume, filtering, selecting by **selectors and search terms** ✓, and examining a fraction.
- **Selectors and search terms** ✓. *Wrong: keywords someone types now and then.* Right: the rules that decide what is pulled out for a human to see. **This is where choices about people are actually made.**
- **End-to-end safeguards** ✓. *Wrong: end-to-end encryption.* Right: safeguards at **every stage** — necessity and proportionality assessed throughout; independent authorisation **at the outset**; supervision; independent review afterwards ✓.
- **Independent authorisation.** *Wrong: a minister's signature.* Right: a body independent of the executive. The Secretary of State was not one ✓.
- **Confidential journalistic material** ✓. *Wrong: journalists' own privacy.* Right: the Convention's expression article (Art. 10 ✓), protecting sources and newsgathering. **A separate violation.**
- **Corresponding right** (52(3) ✓). *Wrong: a similar right.* Right: the **same** meaning and scope, including the permitted limitations — with the Charter free to give more, and never less.
- **Communications data.** *Wrong: something new.* Right: the same family as the first episode's traffic data. *One clause, to tie the two.*

**The cast.**
- the applicants;
- the UK government;
- the **Secretary of State** as authoriser;
- the intelligence services;
- foreign partner services;
- the Grand Chamber;
- on the same day, **Centrum för rättvisa**, a Swedish public-interest law firm, against Sweden.

**The system, described operationally** — the planner's description; R4 supplies the Court's own staging.
- Communications are acquired in bulk.
- Automated filtering discards most of them.
- Selectors (identifiers) and search terms pick out the remainder.
- Analysts examine what is selected.
- Some is retained and shared.
- **Field definition:** docket 02's "selectors and search terms" ✓. The Grand Chamber's own description of the stages is R4.
- **Does it exist?** Yes — the regime as revealed and litigated.

**The operative text.**

> **Convention Art. 8(2) ✓** — the phrases "in accordance with the law and is necessary in a democratic society", followed by the list of aims in its own words. **The list is not read**; say that national security heads it.
>
> **Art. 10(1) ✓** — "to receive and impart information and ideas without interference by public authority and regardless of frontiers".

**What the test is called afterwards:** **the quality test** and **the margin**.

**What the Court held** — three findings, each one sentence:
1. **Bulk interception is not in itself incompatible with the Convention.**
2. **The regime lacked end-to-end safeguards.**
   - It was authorised by the Secretary of State, not an independent body.
   - Oversight of the **categories** of selectors and search terms was insufficient.
   - There was no prior internal authorisation for selectors linked to an identifiable individual.
   - There was a further violation for acquiring communications data from providers, and an **Art. 10** violation for journalistic material.
3. **No violation for receiving intelligence from foreign partners** ✓.

**Sweden, in two sentences.** Three gaps:
- no clear rule on destroying intercepted material that contains no personal data;
- no requirement to weigh privacy before sharing with foreign partners;
- no effective review afterwards ✓.

**The two courts, side by side** ✓ (docket 02, "How the test runs"). Luxembourg tends to decide on **necessity**. Strasbourg tends to decide on the **quality of the law**. **Claim that must land:** a listener who knows which court is asking can predict how a case will come out.

**Why this rule and not another.**
- **Why the quality-of-law approach.** A person cannot know whether they are being watched, so Strasbourg reviews the **law in the abstract**. The trade: it can approve a system whose operation it never sees.
- **The road not taken.** Declaring bulk interception disproportionate as such — which is what its critics wanted.

**The variants — change one fact.**
1. **Authorisation by a body independent of the executive.** That violation goes.
2. **Selectors tied to an identifiable person go through prior internal authorisation.** That defect goes.
3. **The state only receives material that foreign partners intercepted.** No violation — as held ✓.

**The levels — two that interact.** *Doctrinal*: two courts asking two different first questions. *Institutional*: a court supervising many states with different systems, which leaves the margin wide where security is claimed. **The claim:** the margin is how a court of many states stays a court of all of them.

**The contested joints.**
- **Victory or normalisation?** *Contested.*
  - The critique holds that the pair of judgments normalised mass surveillance: *The Grand Normalization of Mass Surveillance*, EJIL: Talk! ✓ (title in docket 02), by Marko Milanović `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: author — located via Völkerrechtsblog and Verfassungsblog, June 2021; R4]`. ## Episode 5 — [LENS] What Weighing Hides

**Length:** 26 min. Short, because the argument is small and sharp, and padding it is the failure mode.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 1, 2, 4 — the questions; the floor; the margin and the quality test.

**Why this is a lens and not an apparatus episode.**
- **It judges rather than applies.** No court in this series asks whether weighing rights is legitimate. Weighing is what the courts *do*. Whether it protects rights is the criterion a listener uses to judge every case so far, and every one to come.
- **The single controversy considered and rejected.** ***Schrems I***. It dramatises the floor against the scale, and it is the obvious candidate. It fails because it is already Episode 2's apparatus, and because it shows only one answer: the floor working. It cannot show weighing failing or succeeding.
- **A second candidate, also rejected.** The reception of ***Big Brother Watch*** — the "Grand Normalization" critique. That is a critique of an *outcome*, not of the *method*, and it is Episode 4's contested joint.
- **Applied to, by number.** **Episode 6** — is "pressing social need" in a protest a weighing or a floor? **Episode 7** — a technique excluded outright: a floor by another name? **Episode 9** — a clause that suspends the weighing. **Episode 8** invokes it as well.

**Opening anchor** — *type: a borrowed sentence.* ✓ (docket 01, Explanation to Art. 52)
- The Charter's limitation clause, per its own Explanation, was drafted from the Court's case law. The sentence quoted is from a 2000 judgment about restrictions "in particular in the context of a common organisation of the market".
- That judgment speaks of restrictions that must not constitute "disproportionate and unreasonable interference undermining the very substance of those rights".

*The move: the sentence that decides whether a state may keep everyone's records was drafted from a judgment about running a market. Announce plainly that this episode teaches no provision; it is about how to judge the one this series has been applying.*

**The positions and their ablest defenders.** Every position has a named holder.
1. **Weighing is rational, and rights are things to be optimised.**
   - **Holder:** **Robert Alexy** `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: A Theory of Constitutional Rights, trans. Julian Rivers, Oxford University Press 2002 — the "law of balancing"; R5]`.
   - **The view:** rights are principles to be realised as far as possible. The more one is set back, the weightier the reason on the other side must be.
   - **A response to:** the charge that weighing is arbitrary.
   - **Consequence:** any right can in principle be outweighed; the court's duty is to show its weights.
2. **The questions are the shared grammar of rights, and they put the burden on the state at each step.**
   - **Holder:** **Aharon Barak** `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: Proportionality: Constitutional Rights and their Limitations, Cambridge University Press 2012; R5]`.
   - **Consequence:** the necessity step does most of the work — **which matches what this series has shown in Episode 1.**
3. **Weighing is an assault on rights.**
   - **Holder:** **Stavros Tsakyrakis** `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: "Proportionality: An assault on human rights?", International Journal of Constitutional Law 7 (2009) — issue and page unconfirmed; reply by Madhav Khosla, 8 I·CON 298 (2010); rejoinder, 8 I·CON 307 (2010); R5]`.
   - **The view:** a misguided quest for precision and objectivity. Moral questions are hidden behind something that sounds like measurement, and rights become interests with weights.
   - **Khosla's reply:** some balancing is objectionable, but the test does not require it.
   - **Consequence, if adopted:** courts should reason about what a right protects, not weigh it.
4. **There is a floor where nothing is weighed.**
   - **Holder:** the **Court of Justice**, in *Schrems I* ✓, and the Charter's own text ✓ — the limitation must "respect the essence".
   - **Consequence:** a bright line, kept narrow — content, remedy.
5. **As practised on surveillance, the test turns prohibition into procedure.**
   - **Holder:** the "Grand Normalization" critique ✓ (EJIL: Talk!; author as named in Episode 4).
   - **Consequence:** every refusal becomes a design brief.

**The contested joint, with the awkward part said out loud. This is the series' framing, labelled as such on air.** In every case so far, the final weighing — Tsakyrakis's target — **decided nothing**.
- *Digital Rights Ireland* failed at necessity and never reached the weighing ✓.
- *Schrems I* stopped at the floor ✓.
- *Big Brother Watch* turned on the quality of the law ✓.

**Where the weight leans:** the critique of weighing aims at the step courts rarely use. The live question is whether **necessity and the safeguards** are run strictly — and that is the question the verdict will answer. *Do not resolve it here.*

**The switch, announced once in each direction.** At the open: this episode judges rather than applies. At the close: back to cases.

**The handover.** Back to a case — a camera network that finds one man.

**Transfer questions.**
1. What is being weighed against what — and who chose the scale?
2. Is anything here not for trading at any price?
3. Which step actually decided it: the aim, the need, or the weighing?
4. When a court says "proportionate", did it weigh — or did it check the procedure?

**Sources.**
- Explanations relating to the Charter, Art. 52, OJ C 303, 14.12.2007, citing Case C-292/97, judgment of 13 April 2000.
- *Schrems*, C-362/14.
- Alexy, *A Theory of Constitutional Rights* (2002).
- Barak, *Proportionality* (2012).
- Tsakyrakis, *I·CON* 7 (2009); Khosla, *I·CON* 8 (2010).

## Episode 6 — One Man in a Carriage

**Length:** 32 min. Sized thin on purpose: one Chamber judgment and one applicant. Its weight is technical vocabulary, not provisions.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 1, 4, 5 — interference; the quality test; necessity in a democratic society; the weighing vocabulary.

**Opening instance** — *type: a deployment.* *Glukhin v Russia*, European Court of Human Rights `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: application no. 11519/20, judgment of 4 July 2023, final 4 October 2023, press release ECHR 206 (2023) — facts and holdings below located by the planner; R6]`. The docket names the case, its year and its subject ✓.
- **The protest.** On 23 August 2019, **Glukhin** rode the Moscow underground holding a life-size cardboard figure of **Konstantin Kotov**, a jailed protester, with a placard.
- **The identification.** Police found images of it online and used facial recognition to identify him. Days later they located him on the underground and arrested him.
- **The conviction.** An administrative offence: failing to give notice of a solo demonstration.
- **The judgment.** Strasbourg found **violations of Arts. 8 and 10**. It examined the protest under **expression**, not assembly.

*The move: a man stands alone in a carriage with a cardboard figure, and days later the cameras find him. Announce the return from the lens in the first minute: back to a case.*

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **Identification, against verification.** *Wrong: face recognition is unlocking your phone.* Right: identification searches a face **against a database** to find out who it is (one-to-many). Verification checks a claimed identity (one-to-one). Field definition: R6.
- **Live, against retrospective.** *Wrong: the same thing.* Right: live matching runs on camera feeds against a watchlist as people pass. Retrospective matching runs on images afterwards. **This case involved both** (R6).
- **Match threshold.** *Wrong: the system says yes or no.* Right: it returns a **similarity score**, and an operator-set threshold turns the score into a "match". Lower thresholds produce more false matches. Field definition: R6.
- **Peaceful assembly** (Art. 11(1) ✓). *Wrong: any protest, including one person's.* Right: the Court examined this protest under the expression article. **Teach Art. 11 here**, from its text ✓: "freedom of peaceful assembly", restricted only on the grounds in 11(2). Say plainly that it is *not* what decided this case.
- **Pressing social need.** *Wrong: any useful purpose.* Right: Strasbourg's necessity — the state must show the interference answered a pressing need with relevant and sufficient reasons. The Court found it did not (R6).
- **Chilling effect.** *Wrong: a vague mood.* Right: lawful exercise of a right, deterred by fear of the consequences. Luxembourg's version, recalled in one clause: the "feeling that their private lives are the subject of constant surveillance" ✓.
- **Identification as interference.** *Wrong: only the arrest interfered with his rights.* Right: processing his image to **identify** him was itself the interference (R6).

**The cast.**
- Glukhin;
- Kotov, depicted;
- the Moscow police;
- the camera network;
- the Russian courts;
- the Strasbourg Chamber.

**The system, described operationally.**
- A camera captures a face.
- The system detects the face and extracts a template.
- The template is compared against a database or watchlist.
- A similarity score comes back, and the threshold decides whether it counts.
- A candidate is passed to an officer.
- **Field definition:** a standards-body or evaluation source (R6).
- **Does it exist?** Yes. The Court accepted that it was used even though Russian law required no record of its use (R6).

**The operative text.**
- **Convention Art. 10(2) ✓** — only the working words: "prescribed by law and are necessary in a democratic society". Restate them plainly.
- **Art. 11(1) ✓.**
- **Art. 8(2) ✓**, recalled in one clause.

**What the rule is called afterwards:** **the protest test**.

**Why this rule and not another.** Strasbourg has no rule specific to facial recognition. It applies the general articles and demands **detailed rules and strong safeguards**, and more of them for live matching (R6). The road not taken is a ban as such. The trade: case-by-case review leaves deployment lawful wherever the rules are detailed enough.

**The variants — change one fact.**
1. **A detailed statute with safeguards, and a target suspected of serious crime rather than a peaceful protester.** The outcome could differ. The Court leaned on the peacefulness of the protest (R6).
2. **A democracy with a framework.** In England, the Court of Appeal found a police force's live facial recognition unlawful. The framework left too much to officers' discretion over who went on watchlists and where cameras went, and the force had not checked whether the software was biased `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: R (Bridges) v Chief Constable of South Wales Police [2020] EWCA Civ 1058, 11 August 2020 — confirm the grounds; R6]`. **The case is named in the docket's list ✓; its grounds are not.**
3. **The images are used only to identify him, never to locate him live.** It is still an interference. Identification alone was enough (R6).

**The levels — two that interact.** *Technical*: the threshold setting. *Rights-based*: the deterrence of protest. **The claim:** a parameter chosen by an operator decides how many innocent people are flagged — and the law rarely says who sets it.

**The contested joints.**
- **Whether facial recognition can ever be compatible with peaceful protest.** *Contested.* The Court's language is strong but tied to these facts.
- **Whether live recognition should be banned as such.** *Contested.* Legislators are split.

**Spoken deferral #3, said once:** whether the AI Act bans this kind of camera is another series' question. *Permission to forget. No detail, no article.*

**What the rule assumes about the power — and the machine.**
- **The power.** The test assumes an interference is **an act someone records** and can be asked to justify.
- **The machine.** Here the law required no record that the system was used, and the Court had to infer it.
- **Claim that must land:** the test assumes power leaves a paper trail, and this power was built not to.

**As written against as enforced.** Russia had ceased to be a party to the Convention before judgment `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: 16 September 2022 — Council of Europe; R6]`. The Court could rule because the facts came first. **The test's answer reached a state that had already left.**

**What it deliberately compresses.**
- *Bridges*, to one variant;
- the AI Act, to its one deferral;
- data-protection enforcement against facial-recognition vendors — not used.

**The handover.** A face is matched against a list somebody wrote. The next system writes its own list.

**Transfer questions.**
1. Does this identify me, or only confirm I am who I say I am?
2. Who sets the threshold, and how many false matches does it accept?
3. Is there any record that the system was used on me?
4. Would I still go to the protest knowing this exists?
5. Who decides whose face is on the watchlist?

## Episode 7 — The Criteria

**Length:** 36 min.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 1, 2, 3, 5 — the Charter test; effective remedy; automated analysis and human re-examination; the floor-against-scale vocabulary.

**Opening instance** — *type: a screening programme.* *Ligue des droits humains*, Court of Justice (Grand Chamber) `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: C-817/19, judgment of 21 June 2022, ECLI:EU:C:2022:491 — holdings below located via the Essex introductory note and the Court's press release; R7]`. The docket names the case, its year and its subject ✓.
- **The challenge.** A Belgian human-rights association challenged Belgium's law on air passengers' booking data before the Belgian Constitutional Court, which referred it.
- **The system.** Every passenger's booking data is compared against databases and **pre-determined criteria**.
- **The holding.** The Court let the scheme stand on a strict reading:
  - criteria must be specific and non-discriminatory;
  - a positive match must be reviewed individually, by non-automated means;
  - **self-learning systems** — those that can change their criteria without human control — are **excluded**.

*The move: every passenger on every flight is checked against a list before departure, and the Court lets the checking continue on one condition — the list cannot rewrite itself.*

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **Pre-determined criteria.** *Wrong: a watchlist of names.* Right: **patterns** in booking data used to flag people nobody has yet suspected (R7).
- **Self-learning system.** *Wrong: any AI, or any computer.* Right: a system that changes its own assessment criteria without human intervention or control (R7). **This definition is where the episode's variant bites.**
- **Positive match.** *Wrong: a match means suspicion.* Right: a flag that a person must review individually before anything follows. *Recall, in one clause, the same rule from the surveillance ladder.*
- **Non-discrimination** (Charter Art. 21 ▲ — text not held, R1). *Wrong: only deliberate use of a protected characteristic.* Right: the **criteria themselves** must be non-discriminatory.
- **The Convention's discrimination article is not free-standing** (Art. 14 ✓). *Wrong: a general ban on discrimination.* Right: it secures "the enjoyment of the rights and freedoms set forth in this Convention" ✓ without discrimination. A discriminatory score must be attached to another right, such as private life. **Claim:** the two instruments draw the line in different places.
- **Effective remedy** (Art. 47 ▲, recalled). *Wrong: a right to complain.* Right: a person can contest only what they can understand. That is the link between opacity and the exclusion (R7).
- **Reading down.** *Wrong: the Court approved the law as written.* Right: the Court saved the instrument **by reading it narrowly**. The conditions are the price of its validity (R7).
- **Booking data.** *Wrong: passport data.* Right: what airlines hold about a booking (R7).

**The cast.**
- the Belgian association;
- the Belgian Constitutional Court;
- the Belgian state;
- the national **passenger information unit**;
- the airlines;
- the Court of Justice.

**The system, described operationally.**
- Airlines send booking data to a national unit.
- The unit runs automated comparison against databases and against criteria.
- Flags go to an official for review.
- Reviewed flags are passed to the competent authorities.
- **Field definition:** R7.
- **Does it exist?** Yes — Union-wide, under a directive. Its name and number are R7.

**The operative text.**
- Charter Arts. 7 and 8 ✓, recalled.
- **Art. 21 ▲.** Name it; do not quote it.
- **Convention Art. 14 ✓**: "shall be secured without discrimination on any ground such as" — then say the list exists, and do not read it.

**What the rule is called afterwards:** **the fixed-list rule**.

**Why this rule and not another.** A person flagged must be able to understand the flag and contest it. A criterion that changes itself cannot be stated, and so cannot be contested (R7). Two roads not taken:
1. **Allowing learned models, subject to audit.**
2. **Grading the intrusion by the method's power.** Germany's Federal Constitutional Court struck down police data-analysis powers in **Hesse** and **Hamburg** as too broadly framed, demanding thresholds rather than banning the method `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: 1 BvR 1547/19 and 1 BvR 2634/20, 16 February 2023, press release 18/2023; Hesse's platform runs Palantir's software as hessenDATA — confirm whether the judgment weighs self-learning methods explicitly; R7]`. The docket names the case and its month ✓.

**Claim:** Luxembourg banned a type of method; Karlsruhe graded every method by what it can do. Two courts gave two answers to the same machine.

**The variants — change one fact.**
1. **The model is retrained monthly, and staff approve each new version.** Is it "self-learning"? It turns on the meaning of "without human control" — *contested; say so.*
2. **One criterion is a close stand-in for nationality or religion.** It falls on the criteria-must-not-discriminate condition.
3. **A flag leads directly to refusal of boarding, with no individual human review.** It fails.

**The levels — two that interact.** *Technical*: rules against learned weights. *Rights-based*: contestability. **The claim:** here the right to contest decided the machine's permitted architecture.

**The contested joints.**
- **Whether excluding self-learning is workable, or only symbolic.** *Contested.*
- **Whether screening every passenger is itself strictly necessary.** *Settled* — yes, with conditions.
- **Grading against banning.** *Contested.*

**What the rule assumes about the power — and the machine.**
- **The assumption.** The rule assumes the criteria are a list that could be read out to the person flagged.
- **The machine.** A learned model's "criteria" are weights spread across features. No such list exists to read.
- **Claim that must land:** this is the only case in the series where a rights test reached inside a machine and chose its architecture. *The verdict will need that sentence's substance.*

**As written against as enforced.**
- Screening continues across the Union under the conditions. Who audits the criteria is R7.
- In the Netherlands, a welfare-fraud risk system was struck down `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: District Court of The Hague, *SyRI*, 5 February 2020, under the Convention's private-life article for opacity and discriminatory risk; whether the state stopped using it — R7]`. The docket names the case and its month ✓. *One sentence.*

## Episode 8 — The Vote

**Length:** 38 min. Eight articles of a statute, an instance, and one illustration. The compression list below is explicit.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 1, 4, 5 — the test; the expression article and the margin; weighing against the floor.

**Opening instance** — *type: an annulled election.* Romania's presidential first round of 2024 `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: Constitutional Court of Romania, 6 December 2024 — annulled the entire presidential electoral process, two days before the scheduled run-off, after the President declassified intelligence on 4 December alleging a coordinated campaign on TikTok and Telegram promoting Călin Georgescu; the decision number and the constitutional provision relied on are unconfirmed; R8]`. The docket names the annulment ✓.

*The move: a constitutional court cancels an election two days before the run-off, in the name of the election.*

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **Political advertising** (Art. 3(2) ✓). *Wrong: adverts placed by parties.* Right: a message "normally provided for remuneration or through in-house activities or as part of a political advertising campaign", with either of two links:
  - (a) it is by, for or on behalf of a political actor; or
  - (b) it is "liable and designed to influence the outcome of an election or referendum, voting behaviour or a legislative or regulatory process" ✓.
  - **Anyone's** content counts if it is paid for and designed that way.
- **Personal capacity** (Art. 1(3) ✓). *Wrong: anything an individual says.* Right: "Political opinions that are expressed in a personal capacity shall not be considered to be political advertising." ✓ **This is the exit door from the whole Regulation.**
- **Sponsor** (3(10) ✓). *Wrong: the platform.* Right: the person at whose request or on whose behalf the advertisement runs.
- **Targeting techniques** (3(11) ✓). *Wrong: aiming at an audience in general.* Right: addressing a person or group, or **excluding** them, on the basis of processing personal data.
- **Ad-delivery techniques** (3(12) ✓). *Wrong: simply showing the advert.* Right: "optimisation techniques that are used to increase the circulation, reach or visibility of a political advertisement on the basis of the automated processing of personal data and that can serve to deliver the political advertisement to a specific person or group of persons only" ✓. **The platform's own algorithm counts.**
- **Explicit consent, given separately** (18(1)(b) ✓). *Wrong: accepting the platform's terms.* Right: consent given specifically "for the purpose of political advertising" ✓.
- **Profiling with special categories** (18(1)(c) ✓). *Wrong: targeting by political leaning is fine if consented to.* Right: it is **banned outright**, consent or not, wherever it uses the special categories that GDPR Art. 9(1) ✓ (as cited) protects. *The categories themselves are named from the data-protection rulebook — see the marker below — and never taught.*
- **Annulment.** *Wrong: a recount.* Right: the electoral process was cancelled entirely (R8).

**The cast.**
- the Romanian Constitutional Court;
- the candidate and his rival;
- the outgoing President, who declassified the intelligence;
- the platforms;
- **sponsors** and **political actors**, in the Regulation's vocabulary;
- data-protection authorities;
- for the illustration, a Slovak party leader and a journalist (R8).

**The system, described operationally — two machines, kept apart.**
1. **Paid delivery.** A sponsor uploads an advert and may choose targeting parameters. The platform's optimisation then decides who actually sees it.
2. **Organic amplification.** Accounts post. Ranking systems promote what draws engagement. Coordinated accounts, or paid influencers, seed it.

**Field definitions:** the Regulation's own, ✓. **The Regulation reaches the first machine, and only the paid part of the second.**

**The operative text** — said in its own words, in short pieces.

> **Art. 18(1) ✓ — the spoken path, three conditions.** The controller collected the data from the person themselves; the person gave explicit consent, separately; and there is no profiling with special categories.

> **Art. 18(2) ✓** — no targeting of anyone the controller knows "with reasonable certainty" to be at least one year under the voting age.

> **Art. 19(1)(c)(iv) ✓** — the advert must carry "meaningful information on the use of artificial intelligence systems in the targeting or ad delivery of the political advertising".

> **Art. 30(2) ✓** — "It shall apply from 10 October 2025."

**What the rules are called afterwards:** **the paid-message test**, **the consent wall** and **the special-category ban**.

**Rights.**
- **Expression.** Charter Art. 11 ▲ corresponds to the Convention's Art. 10 ✓, per the Explanation.
- **Free elections.** The right the annulment engaged `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: two texts this episode needs and this docket lacks — Article 3 of Protocol No. 1 (free elections), and the list in GDPR Art. 9(1) of the special categories (held in Series B's docket, projects/collisions/research/01); copy both across under R8]`.

**Why this rule and not another.**
- **The objective the Regulation states.** Its rights objective is to protect the Charter's rights, "in particular the right to privacy and the protection of personal data" ✓ (Art. 1(4)(b)).
- **Claim:** the Union regulated political microtargeting as a **data-protection** problem, not an election-integrity one.
- **The road not taken:** a total ban on political targeting. The trade: targeting is permitted behind a consent wall, with a special-category ban and disclosure.

**The variants — change one fact.**
1. **The influencers were paid to post.** Then their posts are political advertising: they carry labels and sponsor disclosure, and in the last three months before an election only Union-based sponsors may buy such services `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: Art. 5(2), Art. 22(1) and Art. 25(1)–(2) read by the planner in the cached CELLAR text the docket was built from (experiments/eurlex/cache/32024R0900.html); add verbatim to docket 01 under R8]`. Whether any influencers were in fact paid is R8.
2. **The same posts, unpaid, by real supporters.** They are expressed in a personal capacity, and **outside the Regulation altogether**.
3. **The sponsor chooses no audience, and the platform's optimisation delivers the advert to a narrow group anyway.** That is an ad-delivery technique, and the Art. 18 conditions apply regardless.

**The illustration, in two sentences.** In the 2023 Slovak election, synthetic audio of a party leader circulated just before polling ✓ (named in the docket's list). Unpaid synthetic speech is **content, not delivery**, and nothing in this Regulation reaches it. *Who, when, and what platforms did are R8. Say no more.*

**The levels — two that interact.** *Economic*: paid speech against organic speech. *Institutional*: who enforces — data-protection authorities, election authorities, or a constitutional court after the fact. **The claim:** the law follows the money. Influence that is not bought is left to other instruments, or to a court that can only cancel the result.

**The contested joints.**
- **Was the annulment proportionate?** *Contested.* The rival candidate called it illegal; how Strasbourg dealt with the candidate's complaint is R8.
- **Does the Regulation protect elections, or only data?** *Contested.* This is the series' framing.
- **If platforms stopped selling political adverts rather than comply, is that less political speech?** *Contested*, and it turns on facts that are R8.

**What the rule assumes about the power — and the machine.**
- **The machine.** The Regulation assumes influence travels as a **paid message aimed by data**. The documented threat — as alleged — travelled as unpaid or disguised posts lifted by a ranking system, and as synthetic audio.
- **The power.** The test is turned around. Here the **state restricts the vote to protect it**, and the court itself exercises the extraordinary power. What the test assumes — that the state is the threat — no longer holds.
- **Claim that must land:** the rule regulates the delivery of bought speech, while the threat it was cited against arrived as grown speech.

**As written against as enforced.**

- The targeting rules are enforced by **data-protection authorities**, and the Regulation's own sanctions article does not cover them (the R8 marker above).

**The handover.** Here a court used an extraordinary power to protect a democracy. The last case is a government declaring the extraordinary — and what happens to its powers when the emergency ends.

## Episode 9 — [VERDICT] The Exception

**Length:** 44 min — about 30 for the apparatus and about 14 for the verdict. Dense apparatus episodes have run long every time. This one also has to land the series.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 1–8.

**Opening instance** — *type: a declared emergency.*
- **The emergency.** Turkey's state of emergency after the 2016 coup attempt, and its notice of derogation under the Convention. The docket names the 2016 derogation ✓.
- **The case.** **Yüksel Yalçınkaya**, a former teacher, was convicted of membership of an armed terrorist organisation, decisively on the basis that the encrypted messaging app **ByLock** was on his phone.
- **The judgment.** Strasbourg's Grand Chamber found violations of the fair-trial article, of the association article and — by eleven votes to six — of **no punishment without law**. **The Court held that the derogation could not reach that last article.** `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: Yüksel Yalçınkaya v Türkiye, Grand Chamber, application no. 15669/20, 26 September 2023, press release ECHR 267 (2023); the derogation point from secondary sources — R9]`

*The move: a teacher convicted because of an app on his phone, under an emergency that suspended parts of the Convention, wins on the one article the emergency could not suspend.*

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **Derogation.** *Wrong: breaking the treaty.* Right: a lawful, notified suspension of some obligations, within limits.
- **"In time of war or other public emergency threatening the life of the nation"** ✓. *Wrong: any crisis a government declares.* Right: Strasbourg reviews the claim — though in this case it relied on earlier rulings about the same emergency (R9).
- **"To the extent strictly required by the exigencies of the situation"** ✓. *Wrong: whatever the emergency calls for.* Right: **proportionality survives the derogation.** The measures must still be strictly required.
- **Non-derogable** (Art. 15(2) ✓). *Wrong: the important rights.* Right: four rights only, and **not privacy, not expression, not assembly**.
- **No punishment without law** (Art. 7 ▲ — named in 15(2) ✓; text not held, R1). *Wrong: no punishment without a trial.* Right: no conviction for an act that was not an offence, under a foreseeable law, when it was done. The Court found the courts' uniform treatment of the app departed from that (R9).
- **Notification** (15(3) ✓). *Wrong: asking permission.* Right: keeping the Secretary General "fully informed" — **information, not approval**.
- **Improper purpose** (Art. 18 ✓). *Wrong: a bad motive in general.* Right: restrictions "shall not be applied for any purpose other than those for which they have been prescribed" ✓ — a violation of its own. ⚠ Docket 01's Art. 18 text carries a stray heading from the next section of the Convention. **Quote only the first sentence.**
- **A temporary measure.** *Wrong: it ends on its date.* Right: it ends, is renewed, or is written into ordinary law. **This is where the erosion lives.**

**The non-derogable list — the spoken path.**
1. **Two rights about the body:** life (except lawful acts of war) and the ban on torture.
2. **One about servitude:** the ban on slavery.
3. **One about the criminal law:** no punishment without law — *the one this case turns on*.
4. **Then the absences.** Privacy, expression and assembly are not on the list.

**Claim that must land:** every right this series has taught sits on the suspendable side of the line. *This is the episode's `dramatic` moment.*

**The cast.**
- Yalçınkaya;
- the Turkish courts;
- the Turkish government;
- the Secretary General, who receives notices;
- the Grand Chamber;
- the President of the Council of Europe's Parliamentary Assembly (R9);
- France's legislature and Constitutional Council (R9).

**The system, described operationally.**
- An encrypted messaging app.
- A list of its users, compiled by the state from data whose provenance is R9.
- The list used as decisive evidence of membership.
- **Field definition:** R9.
- **Does it exist?** Yes — as evidence, in the judgment.

**The operative text.**

> **Convention Art. 15(1) ✓** — "In time of war or other public emergency threatening the life of the nation" and "to the extent strictly required by the exigencies of the situation".

> **Art. 15(2) ✓** — the list, by the spoken path above.

> **Art. 15(3) ✓** — notice. **Art. 18 ✓** — first sentence only.

> **The Charter's Explanation ✓** — the Charter "does not affect the possibilities of Member States to avail themselves of Article 15 ECHR".

**What the rules are called afterwards:** **the emergency clause** and **the survivors' list**.

**Why this rule and not another.** The derogation clause was written into the Convention so that states in crisis would suspend openly and under supervision, rather than ignore the treaty. The trade: legitimacy, in exchange for supervision. The Explanations leave Member States' Convention derogations untouched ✓.

**The variants — change one fact.**
1. **The conviction rests only on a derogable right, such as association.** The derogation would then be examined for strict necessity, not set aside (R9).
2. **No derogation is notified.** There is no emergency cover at all, and the ordinary limitation test applies.
3. **The emergency ends, but its powers continue in an ordinary law.** No derogation is in force, and the full test applies to the new law. *That is where the erosion argument lives.*

**The erosion — the episode's second beat. One sentence each, with dates.**
- **Turkey.** The emergency was declared on 20 July 2016 and notified the next day. The derogation was withdrawn with effect from 19 July 2018. A law of 25 July 2018 then carried emergency-style powers into ordinary law, and the Assembly's President flagged it as a concern `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: Turkey's letters of 21 July 2016 and 8 August 2018 to the Secretary General; PACE President's statement, August 2018; R9]`.
- **France.** An experiment in algorithmic video surveillance for the 2024 Olympics. In April 2025 the Constitutional Council struck down an extension as unrelated to the bill that carried it. The extension returned in a law for the 2030 Games and was adopted, running into 2027 `[OUTSIDE DOCKET: decision of 24 April 2025; the 2026 law and the Council's decision on it — the end date is reported as both 30 September and 31 December 2027; R9]`. The docket names the experiment and asks what became of its end date ✓.

**Claim that must land:** the emergency ends and the system does not.

**The levels — two that interact.** *Legal*: derogation and its supervision. *Political*: who declares an emergency, who ends it, and who renews the experiment. **The claim:** the clause times the law, not the machine.

**The contested joints.**
- **Was Turkey's emergency genuine?** *Settled for the Court*, which relied on earlier rulings.
- **Was the app evidence reliable?** R9.
- **Do experiments ever end?** *Still moving.* Date-stamp it.

**What the rule assumes about the power — and the machine.**
- **The assumption.** The clause assumes an emergency is **temporary and visible, and ends in a notice**.
- **The machine.** Data systems built during an emergency persist. A list of app users outlived the emergency as evidence, and the camera experiment outlived the Games.
- **Claim that must land:** derogation suspends the law for a period; nothing suspends the machine.

**As written against as enforced.** The Court treated the app convictions as a **systemic** problem and called on Turkey to address it beyond this applicant. Its execution is R9.

**Then the verdict — see the series section that follows, which is part of this episode's brief.** The writer of this episode has read only Episodes 6, 7 and 8. **Every fact below from Episodes 1–4 is restated with its qualifier, and must be spoken with it:**
- ***Digital Rights Ireland* (2014).** The directive was declared invalid from the date it entered into force. It **failed strict necessity**. The essence was **not** touched, because no content was kept.
- ***Schrems I* (2015).** Generalised access to **content**, and the lack of any legal remedy, compromised the essence. Safe Harbour fell with **no weighing needed**.
- ***Schrems II* (2020).** The contractual clauses survived, **because** they require checking before a transfer and suspension when protection fails. Privacy Shield fell: its limits were not confined to what is strictly necessary, and its Ombudsperson was neither independent nor able to bind.
- ***Latombe* (3 September 2025).** The General Court upheld the third framework. The appeal's status is Episode 2's check, and must be re-stated as at the week of writing.
- ***La Quadrature du Net* (2020).** General and indiscriminate retention is precluded as a preventive measure, **except** for a serious national-security threat that is genuine and present or foreseeable — time-limited, renewable, and under binding review. Automated analysis of **all users'** traffic and location data is not precluded **under the same condition**, subject to individual human re-examination of every positive result.
- ***Big Brother Watch* (2021).** Bulk interception is **not in itself** incompatible with the Convention. The violations were the missing safeguards: authorisation by a minister rather than an independent body, oversight of selector categories, and journalistic material. There was **no** violation for receiving foreign intelligence.

**Say that the verdict was held, and why.** It was held since **Episode 1** — *numbered pointer two of two* — so that the listener would have seen the test both refuse and permit before being asked to judge it.

4. When a court says no, does it say no to the capability — or only to this design of it?
5. What single checkable event would tell me, in a year, which way this has gone?

## The verdict episode

**Episode 9, "The Exception."** It lands the series' only normative verdict.
- **Where the question is raised.** It is named in Episode 1 and explicitly held there.
- **Where the evidence is planted:** Episodes 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 7, and Episode 9's own apparatus.
- **What Episodes 5 and 8 contribute:** Episode 5 supplies the vocabulary — the scale against the floor. Episode 8 supplies the case where the test is turned around.

**The question, as held:** is proportionality a real constraint on state power over data, or a form that ratifies whatever was going to happen?

**The competing answers, and who holds them.**
1. **A real constraint.** *Holders:* Barak and Alexy, as defenders of the method (Ep 5). *Evidence planted:*
   - a directive voided retroactively (Ep 1);
   - two transatlantic frameworks struck down (Ep 2);
   - a self-learning technique excluded (Ep 7);
   - facial recognition against a peaceful protester held incompatible (Ep 6);
   - an article that held through a declared emergency (Ep 9).
2. **A form that ratifies.** *Holders:* the "Grand Normalization" critique (Milanović), the "Opens the Gates" critique (Watt), and Tsakyrakis on the method (Eps 4 and 5). *Evidence planted:*
   - conditions written for general retention and population-wide automated analysis (Ep 3);
   - bulk interception accepted in principle (Ep 4);
   - the third framework upheld at first instance (Ep 2);
   - emergency powers moved into ordinary law, and an experiment extended (Ep 9).
3. **A constraint on design, and a ratification of existence.** *Holder:* docket 02's compiler — the line bends "toward procedure — independent authorisation, review, time limits — rather than toward prohibition" ✓. **Adopted as the series' own position, and labelled as such on air.**
4. **It depends on which court is asking.** Luxembourg constrains, because it asks about necessity; Strasbourg ratifies, because it asks about the quality of the law. *Holder:* the series' own framing, built from docket 02's two-courts note ✓. **Refuted by Episode 3**, where Luxembourg bent too.

**THE VERDICT — named.** **The series comes down on answer 3.** On the record taught here, proportionality as these courts use it is a real constraint on the **design** of state power over data, and a ratification of its **existence**.
- **Its outright refusals are real, but rare.** They sit at **floors** — content, remedy, retroactive punishment — or on a **technique**, such as self-learning criteria.
- **Every capability a state came back insisting on has returned permitted with conditions.**

**The three facts that decide it.**
1. **In 2020 the Court of Justice wrote the terms of what it had refused.** In 2014 and 2016 it refused general retention. Once states pushed back, it wrote the conditions under which general retention, and automated analysis of every user's data, are lawful (Ep 3).
2. **In 2021 Strasbourg accepted bulk interception in principle.** Every violation it found was a missing safeguard (Ep 4).
3. **The refusals that did not bend all sit at a floor or on a technique.** They are the essence (Ep 2), a non-derogable article (Ep 9), and the exclusion of self-learning criteria (Ep 7). Meanwhile every right this series taught is derogable (Ep 9).

**Concede, without hedging,** the strongest counter-evidence:
- the Episode 7 exclusion is a test reaching inside a machine and forbidding an architecture;
- the Episode 6 language calls a use of the technology incompatible with democratic values.

Both are real refusals. Neither stopped the underlying capability. Passenger screening continues, and the state in Episode 6 had already left the system.

**What would overturn it — said aloud, so the listener can check.**
- **The Court of Justice allowing the Latombe appeal and striking down the third framework.** That would be a refusal **after** the design was changed to meet the test.
- **Or a court refusing population-wide automated analysis, or live facial recognition, as such** — rather than conditioning it.

If either happens, the verdict should be revisited.

**The writer may disagree, and is told how.** If the material in front of the writer leads to a different answer — most plausibly answer 1, on the strength of Episodes 6 and 7 — the writer should:
1. **say so in the narrator's voice;**
2. **name this plan's verdict as the one being departed from;**
3. **and give the reasons.**

What the writer must not do is leave the question open. Eight episodes and a promise in Episode 1 were aimed at an answer.

**The order of the close is a sequence, not a choice.**
1. **The verdict**, named and owned.
2. **What would overturn it.**
3. **Last, the date**: an appeal pending in Luxembourg, stated as at the week of recording. That is the reminder that the listener can check.

**Not the verdict.** These judgments belong where they arise, and are not the series verdict:
- that the content line is poorly drawn (Ep 1);
- that the targeting rule follows money rather than influence (Ep 8).
