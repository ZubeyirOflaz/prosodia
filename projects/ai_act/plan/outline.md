# THE INSTRUMENT — Series Outline

## The through-line

Europe decided to regulate artificial intelligence the way it regulates lifts, toys and medical devices: as a **product placed on a market**. This series builds that instrument from the definitional gate upwards — and asks, at every stage, what the product frame can see, what it structurally cannot, and whether anyone can enforce it.

The series' own verdict on that choice is **held until Episode 12**, and the listener is told so, out loud, in Episode 1.

## Episode 1 — The Gate

**Length:** 27 min.

**Prerequisites:** none. This is the entry point; every term after this is earned inside the series.

**Opening instance** — *type: a national deployment, ended.* The **Criminaliteits Anticipatie Systeem (CAS)**, run by the **Nederlandse Politie**. Piloted 2015, national from 2017, **used until mid-December 2025**; discontinuation reported February 2026. The Netherlands was the first country to deploy predictive policing nationally. The police stopped it because its operational value was unclear: there were **no clear goals and no measurable success criteria**, and in Amsterdam **roughly one incident in fifty was correctly predicted**. The public artefact is the Dutch government **Algorithm Register entry 81228922**.

*Open on the ending, not the beginning: a decade-old national system quietly switched off, and only then ask what the law would have called it.*

**The cast:** the Nederlandse Politie (would be the **deployer**, and, because it built the thing, also the **provider**); the Dutch Ministry of Justice and Security; the Algorithm Register as a voluntary national transparency instrument that exists *outside* the Act; the residents of the scored grid squares, who under Art. 2(1)(g) are "affected persons" and under the rest of the Act are almost nobody.

**The system, operationally, before any rule applies:**
- Weekly cycle. The map of a city is divided into a grid of small cells; each cell gets a risk score for the coming period. Outputs are expressed as **"hotspots"** and **"hot times."**
- Three data sources, depersonalised and anonymised: **BVI** (the central police crime database), **GBA** (municipal population administration), **CBS** (Statistics Netherlands demographics).
- Crime types covered: burglary, car theft, bicycle theft, nuisance.
- **Closed system: an officer could not see which data drove any particular prediction.**
- ⚠ **Grid size is contested in the sources — "125 square metres" and "125 by 125 metres" both appear. Check the Algorithm Register entry before any figure is spoken.**
- **Field's own definition to check the description against:** place-based predictive policing as defined in the criminology literature — the application of statistical or machine-learning techniques to identify likely targets for police intervention. *[Verify a citable definition — the RAND framework (Perry et al., 2013) is the usual reference and needs confirming before it is named.]*

**The operative text:**
> **Art. 3(1):** "a machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy and that **may exhibit adaptiveness** after deployment, and that, for explicit or implicit objectives, **infers**, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments."

Plus **Art. 2(1)(a)–(g)** — in particular **(c)**, third-country providers and deployers "where the **output** produced by the AI system is used in the Union."

Called thereafter: **the gate** (Art. 3(1)) and **the reach** (Art. 2).

⚠ Both must be taken from the consolidated text on EUR-Lex before being spoken. The docket is one remove from source.

**Why this rule and not another:** the alternative was a list — enumerate the techniques (machine learning, logic- and knowledge-based approaches, statistical methods), which is what the 2021 draft's Annex I did. A list ages. A functional definition built on **inference** does not name any technique, and so cannot be evaded by renaming one. What was traded: legal certainty. Nobody can read "infers" off the page and know where it stops, which is why the Commission had to publish **guidelines in February 2025** — and why the guidelines' most quoted line is a *negative* one: because the text says "**may** exhibit adaptiveness", adaptiveness is **not necessary**. A system that never learns another thing after it ships is still an AI system.

**The variants — change one fact:**
1. CAS is replaced by a fixed rule written by a policy officer: *"send a car to any square with three burglaries in the last month."* No model, no inference from data about how to generate the output — arguably out of the gate entirely. Same cars, same streets, same effect on the same people. **The Act does not apply.**
2. The output is not a hotspot map but a full ranking of named individuals. Still inside the gate; now also potentially inside Art. 5(1)(d). *Ep 5 takes that up.*
3. CAS is built and run in the Netherlands but by a US vendor with no EU establishment, scoring Dutch grid squares. **Art. 2(1)(c)** pulls the vendor in anyway, because the output is used in the Union.

**The levels:** *technical* — a weekly regression over three databases; *organisational* — a closed box in a command room, with no channel to ask why; *institutional* — a police force running a decade-long programme it never defined success for; *economic* — a procurement decision nobody had to justify against an outcome; *rights-based* — geographic scoring is not a decision about a person until an officer acts on it, which is precisely why it slipped so many nets. The point to land: **the Act's gate is a technical test, and every other level is invisible to it.**

**The contested joints:**
- Does a deterministic system "infer"? *Contested; the guidelines lean toward excluding simple rule engines, and nobody thinks the boundary is clear.*
- Does the Feb 2025 guidance bind? *Settled: it does not. It is non-binding, and the interpretation that binds will come from courts years from now.*
- Is a definitional gate the right architecture at all, versus regulating uses regardless of technique? *Contested; genuinely open.*

**What the rule assumes about the machine:** Art. 3(1) presumes a system with an identifiable *objective*, from which its outputs can be read as inferences toward that objective. CAS had outputs and no measurable objective — the police said so themselves when they stopped it. **The gate asks what a system is for. Here, after ten years, the honest answer turned out to be: nothing that could be measured.** A law that classifies by intended purpose has no test for a system whose purpose was never operationalised.

**As written against as enforced:** nobody assessed CAS under the AI Act, because the high-risk regime did not apply and now applies from **2 December 2027** for Annex III systems. The instrument that actually produced public knowledge about CAS was a **voluntary national algorithm register**, not this Regulation.

**Deliberately compressed, and to be said aloud:** the Art. 2 exclusions are named and not explored — national security, defence, pure scientific R&D, personal non-professional use, free and open-source (with its exceptions). Each is a hole shaped like an argument, and the episode says that it is leaving them.

**Handover:** we now know what the Act *counts*. We do not know why anyone thought a law could be written in time for a technology that moves this fast. **Next episode we step away from the instrument for one episode only — Episode 2 — and we are back inside the text in Episode 3.**

**Transfer questions:**
1. Does this system infer, or does it apply rules someone wrote down?
2. What is its stated objective, and is there a measurement that would tell you if it met it?
3. Whose output is used where — and does that put it inside a law nobody in the building has read?
4. If this system were switched off tomorrow, would anyone be able to say what was lost?

**Sources:** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Arts. 2 and 3(1), consolidated text, EUR-Lex. Commission Guidelines on the definition of an AI system, February 2025. Dutch Algorithm Register, entry 81228922 (algoritmes.overheid.nl). Reporting on the discontinuation of CAS, February 2026.

## Episode 2 — [LENS] The Pacing Problem

**Length:** 24 min. Short because the argument is small and sharp; padding it would be the failure mode.

**Prerequisites:** Ep 1 — the gate, inference, intended purpose, the idea that duties attach at the point a thing is placed on a market.

**Opening anchor** — *type: two documents, dated three weeks apart.* The Commission's proposal for an AI Regulation, **21 April 2021**, drafted against a world of classifiers and scoring systems — and **ChatGPT, 30 November 2022**, which was not in it. The general-purpose chapter that now runs to Arts. 51–56 did not exist in the text Europe started with. *Announce plainly: this episode has no statute in it.*

**The positions, and their ablest defenders:**
1. **The Collingridge dilemma** (David Collingridge, *The Social Control of Technology*, 1980). Early in a technology's life you can still steer it but you do not know what to steer it away from; by the time you know, it is entrenched and you cannot. Regulation is always either premature or too late. **Consequence of adopting it:** you build in staged application, review clauses and delegated powers to amend annexes — which is exactly what Arts. 7, 97 and 112 are.
2. **The pacing problem** (Larry Downes' "law of disruption"; Gary Marchant and colleagues, *The Growing Gap Between Emerging Technologies and Legal-Ethical Oversight*). Technology moves exponentially, law moves institutionally, and the gap widens. **Consequence:** prefer principles and regulators with discretion over rules with thresholds. *[Verify the Marchant volume's exact title and date before citing aloud.]*
3. **Regulation by architecture** (Lawrence Lessig, *Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace*, 1999, and *Code v2*, 2006 — "code is law"; Joel Reidenberg's *lex informatica*). What actually constrains behaviour is the built environment, not the rule about it; so a regulator who wants effect should specify the architecture. **Consequence:** you get logging requirements, machine-readable marking of synthetic content, and technical documentation obligations — the Act is full of Lessig even where it never says so. *[Verify edition and date.]*
4. **The refusal.** Regulate outcomes and harms in the ordinary law you already have, and do not name the technology at all. **Consequence:** no gate, no tiers, no CE mark — and no ex-ante duty on anything before it hurts someone.

**What each is a response to:** (1) and (2) respond to the failure of post-hoc regulation of chemicals, nuclear power and asbestos; (3) responds to the 1990s belief that the internet was unregulable; (4) responds to the observation that technology-specific statutes date badly and entrench incumbents.

**Applied to, by number:** **Ep 4** (the design-time bet is a Collingridge bet); **Ep 8** (the standards machinery is regulation by architecture, delegated); **Ep 9** (a compute threshold is a pacing-problem artefact — a bright line drawn where the technology was in 2023); **Ep 12** (the whole verdict).

**Contested joints:** whether the pacing problem is real or a rhetorical move used to argue against regulating anything — *contested, and the critique is serious: "it moves too fast to regulate" is also the industry's preferred position.* Whether staged application solves Collingridge or merely postpones it — *still moving; the December 2027 deferral is the live test.*

**Handover:** if you are going to regulate anyway, you must first say **who is responsible**. That question has an answer in the Act, and an airline once tried to argue there was a third one. **Back inside the text next episode.**

**Transfer questions:**
1. Is this rule drawn where the technology *is*, or where it *was* when drafting started?
2. What in this instrument can be changed without reopening the statute — and who holds that power?
3. Is the constraint here a rule about the system, or a requirement built into the system?
4. Would ordinary law already have reached this harm?

**Sources:** Collingridge (1980). Lessig, *Code v2* (2006). Marchant et al. on the pacing problem. Commission proposal COM(2021) 206 final, 21 April 2021.

## Episode 3 — Under Whose Name

**Length:** 27 min.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 1–2 — the gate; that duties attach at placing on the market; that the Act is an instrument with staged application.

**Opening instance** — *type: a decided case.* ***Moffatt v. Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149**, file SC-2023-005609, Civil Resolution Tribunal of British Columbia, Tribunal Member **Christopher C. Rivers**, issued **14 February 2024**. In November 2022, after their grandmother died, **Jake Moffatt** used the chatbot on Air Canada's website while booking. The chatbot said bereavement fares could be applied for retroactively. They could not.

Open on paragraph 27, read exactly:
> "…Air Canada suggests the chatbot is a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions. **This is a remarkable submission.** While a chatbot has an interactive component, it is still just a part of Air Canada's website. It should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website. It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot."

And para. 28: *"I find Air Canada did not take reasonable care to ensure its chatbot was accurate."* The order, para. 44: **$812.02 total — $650.88 damages, $36.14 pre-judgment interest under the Court Order Interest Act, $125 tribunal fees**, payable within 14 days.

**Disclosure the writer must make in the episode:** Canada, small-claims tribunal, contract and negligent misrepresentation. The AI Act did not apply and does not apply retrospectively. *"It is worth asking what it would have demanded"* — said out loud, at the point the counterfactual starts. And then the harder point: under **Art. 2(1)(a)**, the same chatbot offered to EU users today would be squarely in scope. The counterfactual is about the facts, not about the law.

**The cast, and what the Act calls each:** Air Canada — **deployer** (Art. 3(4): "using an AI system **under its authority**"), and possibly **provider** if it put the system into service under its own name (Art. 3(3)). Whoever built the chatbot — provider, or nothing at all if Air Canada rebadged it. Moffatt — under this Act, an "affected person" under Art. 2(1)(g), with a complaint right under Art. 85 and no money. The chatbot — **no role. The Act contains no such category, and that absence is the episode.**

**The system, operationally:** ⚠ **The decision does not describe the architecture, and the writer must not assume one.** November 2022 predates ChatGPT's release by weeks; a customer-service bot of that vintage was most likely **intent classification plus retrieval over a help-content corpus** — an utterance is embedded or keyword-matched, mapped to one of a fixed set of intents, and a templated or retrieved answer returned. A generative model would fail differently: it would compose a plausible sentence rather than mis-select a stored one. **The episode says which it does not know, and reasons about both** — because under Art. 3(1) both infer, and both are through the gate.
*Field's own definitions to check against: "task-oriented dialogue system" and "retrieval-augmented generation" as the NLP literature uses them. [Verify a citable source for each.]*

**The operative text:**
> **Art. 3(3) 'provider'** — one who develops an AI system, or has one developed, "and places it on the market or puts the AI system into service **under its own name or trademark**, whether for payment or free of charge."
> **Art. 3(4) 'deployer'** — one "**using an AI system under its authority** except where the AI system is used in the course of a personal non-professional activity."
> **Art. 3(11) 'putting into service'** — "the supply of an AI system for first use directly to the deployer or for own use in the Union for its intended purpose."

Plus **Art. 25**, responsibilities along the value chain — the rule that turns a deployer into a provider.

Called thereafter: **the name rule** (under whose name it goes out) and **the flip** (Art. 25).

**Why this rule and not another:** the Act could have attached duties to *the system* — a registry of certified models, obligations following the artefact. It attached them to **roles**, because the roles already existed in forty years of EU product law: manufacturer, importer, distributor, and the chain of responsibility between them. What was traded: the mapping is awkward for software that is configured, fine-tuned, rebadged and resold. One organisation can occupy several roles at once, and can change role by changing the system — which is why **most real disputes are role disputes**, and why it is under-taught everywhere else.

**The variants:**
1. Air Canada licenses the bot and leaves it as the vendor shipped it. Air Canada is a **deployer**; the vendor is the **provider**; the documentation duties sit with the vendor.
2. Air Canada puts its own name on it — **provider**, and the entire provider stack lands on the airline.
3. Air Canada fine-tunes it on its own fare rules, or repurposes it for something the vendor never intended. **Art. 25 flips it into provider** for that system, with the conformity obligations that follow.
4. The bot is bought by a Canadian airline and used only in Canada, but EU passengers use the website from Munich and act on the output. **Art. 2(1)(c)** becomes live and the answer is genuinely arguable.

**The levels:** *technical* — a retrieval system returning a stale document; *organisational* — nobody owned the help content; *institutional* — a tribunal that would not accept a novel legal person; *economic* — $650.88 against the cost of maintaining the corpus; *rights-based* — a consumer with no information about what he was talking to.

**The contested joints:**
- Whether "under its own name or trademark" is workable for foundation models re-served through a dozen intermediaries — *contested, and the value-chain rules in Art. 25 are the least-tested part of the Act.*
- Whether a deployer who writes a system prompt has "substantially modified" anything — *still moving; no authority.*
- Whether any legal system should ever recognise an artificial agent as a responsible party — *settled in practice, contested in the literature; the tribunal called the submission "remarkable," which is a judge's word for absurd.*

**What the rule assumes about the machine:** the provider/deployer split assumes a **stable artefact with a determinate author** — someone made it, someone else uses it, and you can tell which is which. A system that is continuously updated server-side, retrained weekly, and reconfigured by the customer with a paragraph of English strains every joint in that assumption. The Act's answer is to make modification a role-changing act. Whether that is administrable is unknown.

**As written against as enforced:** the Act is a **market-surveillance regime, not a private right of action**. Under it, Moffatt could have complained to a market-surveillance authority (Art. 85) and would still not have had his C$650.88. **His remedy came from ordinary contract and tort law.** The gap between regulatory compliance and individual redress is one of the most useful things this series teaches, and it shows here in miniature. **Liability proper is Series 3, "Who Pays."**

**Deliberately compressed:** Arts. 22–24 (authorised representatives, importers, distributors) are named and their duties summarised in one pass, not walked through. The episode says so.

**Handover:** roles tell you *who* owes something. They do not tell you *how much*. For that, the Act sorts systems into tiers — and Air Canada's chatbot lands on a rung we have not built yet.

**Transfer questions:**
1. Whose name is on this system, and did they write it, buy it, or rebadge it?
2. If I changed the configuration, would I become its provider?
3. When this goes wrong, which of these parties can the affected person actually sue — and is that the same list as the one the regulator can fine?
4. Is anyone in this chain claiming the system is responsible for itself?

**Sources:** *Moffatt v. Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149, paras. 27, 28, 44 (CanLII). Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Arts. 3(3), 3(4), 3(11), 25, 2(1). ⚠ CanLII blocks scripted retrieval — the decision has been read manually and the passages above are verbatim.

## Episode 4 — The Ladder

**Length:** 30 min — three more than default, because it builds the four rungs and the classification article that decides which one you are on.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 1 and 3 — the gate; provider and deployer; that duties attach at placing on the market.

**Opening instance** — *type: an administrative catastrophe and the government that fell over it.* The **toeslagenaffaire**. Between **2005 and 2019** the **Belastingdienst** (Dutch Tax and Customs Administration) wrongly accused roughly **26,000 families** (some estimates 35,000) of childcare-benefit fraud and demanded full repayment — commonly **€20,000 to €60,000** each. **More than 1,100 children were placed in foster care.** The **third Rutte cabinet resigned in January 2021.** On **25 May 2022** the Dutch government admitted for the first time that **institutional racism** in part of the Tax Administration was a root cause. Amnesty International's report is titled ***Xenophobic machines*** (EUR 35/4686/2021).

Open on the number of families, then the number of children.

> ⚠ **TRAP, and it is disqualifying.** The benefits scandal involved the Tax Administration's **risk-classification model**. **SyRI** (*Systeem Risico Indicatie*) was a **separate** welfare-fraud system, struck down by **the District Court of The Hague in February 2020**. Secondary sources blur them constantly. The episode may mention SyRI, and must mention it *as a different system*. **Verify which system is being described before a word is written.**

**The cast:** the Belastingdienst — provider and deployer at once, a public authority building its own tool; the civil servants who read a score as grounds for suspension; the families, disproportionately of Moroccan, Turkish, Surinamese and Caribbean descent; a parliamentary inquiry; a cabinet.

**The system, operationally:** from **2013**, a **risk-classification model** in the childcare-benefits division. Supervised learning on examples of applications previously judged correct and incorrect; output a risk score; used as a **first filter**, with officials scrutinising the highest-scoring claims. Inputs included **dual nationality**, **low income**, and **whether the applicant's name read as non-Dutch**.
Two properties the episode must land before any law is mentioned: (i) **the labels came from prior investigations**, so the model learned which applications had previously been *found* wrong, not which were wrong — a feedback loop; (ii) **it was a triage tool that produced a queue, and nothing in the model suspended anyone's benefits.** What turned a flawed model into a catastrophe was the organisation around it: a score treated as grounds for **full suspension and clawback of years of past payments, often with no explanation, no appeal pathway, and no evidence beyond the score.**
*Field's own definition to check against: supervised binary classification and risk triage as used in the fraud-analytics literature.*

**The operative text:**
> **Art. 6(1)–(2)** — the two routes: an AI system is high-risk where it is a safety component of, or is itself, a product covered by **Annex I** Union harmonisation legislation and requires third-party conformity assessment; or where it falls within **Annex III**.
> **Art. 6(3)** — the derogation: an Annex III system is not high-risk where it does not pose a significant risk of harm, including by not materially influencing the outcome of decision-making — with the carve-out that **profiling of natural persons is always high-risk**.
> **Art. 3(12) 'intended purpose'** and **Art. 3(13) 'reasonably foreseeable misuse'.**
> **Annex III point 5(a)** — systems used by public authorities to evaluate eligibility for essential public assistance benefits and services.

Called thereafter: **the ladder** (four rungs) and **the design-time bet**.

The four rungs, built in order: **prohibited** (Art. 5, live since 2 February 2025, penalties from 2 August 2025) → **high-risk** (Annex III from **2 December 2027**, Annex I from **2 August 2028**, both deferred by the 2026 Omnibus) → **limited risk**, transparency duties under Art. 50, **live since 2 August 2026** → **minimal risk**, nothing at all. *All four dates spoken with their dates attached.*

**Why this rule and not another:** the alternative shapes were (a) a **rights-based** instrument keyed to the severity of interference with a protected interest, whoever caused it (this is the EDPB–EDPS position — **Ep 6**); (b) a **sectoral** approach — rules for medicine, rules for policing, rules for credit, written by the regulators who already know those sectors. The tier model was chosen because it is the shape EU product law already had, and because it gives industry an ex-ante answer: read Annex III, know your obligations. What was traded: **the tier is fixed by intended purpose at design time, while harm is contextual and shows up in deployment.** That is the Act's central design bet, and everything in Ep 6 is an argument about it.

**The variants:**
1. The same model, same inputs, used by a **bank** to triage loan applications. Annex III point 5(b) — creditworthiness — also high-risk. Change one fact: it triages *internal audit* workload and touches no individual outcome. Art. 6(3) may drop it off the ladder entirely.
2. The model outputs no score but a **ranked queue** with no numbers attached. Does it "materially influence the outcome of decision-making"? The officials' behaviour says yes; the design document says it is only triage.
3. Nationality is removed as a variable and postcode retained. The classification under Art. 6 does not move a millimetre. **The tier is blind to the change that matters most.**

**The levels:** *technical* — a classifier with contaminated labels; *organisational* — a score used as evidence by people with no training to read it; *institutional* — a tax authority with a fraud mandate and no correction mechanism; *economic* — recovery targets; *rights-based* — discrimination on nationality, and a decade before anyone said "institutional racism" out loud. **The Act's ladder reaches the first level, gestures at the second through Art. 26, and does not reach the rest. Say so plainly and hold the judgement.**

**The contested joints:**
- Is a tiered risk model the right shape at all? — **contested, and the docket takes no position, because the informed opinion genuinely has not converged. Do not manufacture a consensus here.**
- Does Art. 6(3) open an escape hatch providers will self-certify through? — *contested; the profiling carve-out is the answer's limit and its test.*
- Is Annex III a list that can be kept current by delegated act (Art. 7), or a list that will always be one scandal behind? — *still moving.*

**What the rule assumes about the machine:** high-risk classification presumes a system with a **stable intended purpose declared by its provider** — that you can read a system's risk off what it was built for. The benefits model was built to sort a queue. It was *used* as proof of fraud. **The tier tracks the design document; the harm tracked the organisation.** Nothing in Art. 6 can see the gap.

**As written against as enforced:** none of this regime applied to the Belastingdienst, and the parts that would apply to its successor systems do not apply until **2 December 2027**. What actually ended the affair was a parliamentary inquiry, journalism, and a cabinet resignation.

**Deliberately compressed, and said aloud:** Annex I is named, its two sections described, and its 20-odd underlying product regimes left as a list — with **Arts. 102–110**, which thread AI into the existing vehicle, aviation, rail and machinery statutes, named as plumbing and skipped. The episode states that a whole parallel route into the high-risk regime is being summarised in ninety seconds, and that **Ep 8** is where that machinery gets taken apart.

**Handover:** we have built a ladder with four rungs. Three of them impose duties. One of them forbids. Next: the top rung, and why a legal order that generally regulates rather than bans decided that eight things may not be built at all.

**Transfer questions:**
1. Which rung is this on — and was that decided by what it is for, or by what it does?
2. Who declared the intended purpose, and would the people using it recognise that description?
3. If the deployment drifted from the design document, does anything in the rule notice?
4. Does this system decide, or does it produce a queue that someone else treats as a decision?
5. Is the risk here in the model, or in what the organisation is permitted to do with its output?

**Sources:** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Arts. 6, 7, 3(12), 3(13), Annexes I and III. Amnesty International, *Xenophobic machines*, EUR 35/4686/2021. Dutch parliamentary inquiry reporting; the government's statement of 25 May 2022. *(Distinguished on air: District Court of The Hague, SyRI judgment, February 2020.)*

## Episode 5 — The Red Lines

**Length:** 30 min — the prohibition list is eight limbs plus two 2026 insertions plus a heavily-qualified biometric exception.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 1, 3, 4 — the gate; provider and deployer; the ladder and which rung is which.

**Opening instance** — *type: a regulator's fine on a bank, for doing the thing before it was banned.* **Case NAIH-85-3/2022** (antecedent NAIH-7350/2021), Hungary. From **May 2018**, **Budapest Bank** ran AI speech-signal processing over **customer-service call recordings**, analysing keywords **and the emotional state of the speaker**. Results were stored with the call, replayable for **45 days**, and used to monitor call quality, pre-empt complaints, **rate the quality of the call-handling staff's work**, and rank calls so the software could recommend which customers to call back first. The Hungarian DPA (**NAIH**) opened its investigation in September 2021 and fined the bank **HUF 250 million — about €665,000**, then the largest data-protection fine in Hungary. It found automated decision-making and profiling with **no valid legal basis**, no proper balancing of interests, inadequate safeguards; held that **only freely given informed consent** could ground emotion-based voice analysis; and **ordered the bank to stop analysing emotions.**

**Disclosure:** this was decided under the **GDPR**, in 2022, three years before Art. 5 applied. The GDPR machinery itself — legal basis, legitimate interest balancing, Art. 22 — is **Series 2, "The Data Subject."** Here it is the occasion, not the subject.

**The cast:** Budapest Bank (deployer, and the Act's prohibition binds it as both a putter-into-service and a user); the vendor of the speech-analytics software; the call-centre staff being rated; the **customers**, whose emotions were the input; NAIH.

**The system, operationally:** speech emotion recognition over recorded audio. Acoustic features — fundamental frequency, energy, speaking rate, spectral shape — extracted per utterance and mapped by a trained classifier onto a small label set (typically anger, frustration, neutrality, satisfaction), often with a per-call aggregate. Keyword spotting runs alongside.
**The "does this system work" beat, which this episode must not skip:** the scientific standing of inferring internal emotional states from vocal or facial signals is **contested at the construct level** — the review literature argues that expressive configurations do not map reliably onto emotion categories across people and contexts. *[Verify and cite: Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez & Pollak, "Emotional Expressions Reconsidered," Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2019 — check pagination before it is read aloud.]* **The episode asks whether the banned thing is a thing, and reports honestly that the evidence says the measurement is weak — which makes the ban's rationale *power*, not accuracy.**

**The operative text:**
> **Art. 5(1)(f)** — inferring emotions of a natural person "**in the areas of workplace and education institutions**", "except where the use of the AI system is intended to be put in place or into the market for **medical or safety reasons**."

and the rest of the list, each read in its own words: **(a)** subliminal, purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques "with the objective, or the effect of materially distorting the behaviour"; **(b)** exploiting vulnerabilities "due to their age, disability or a specific social or economic situation"; **(c)** social scoring based on "social behaviour" leading to detrimental treatment; **(d)** criminal risk assessment "**based solely on the profiling**" of a person, with the human-assessment exception; **(e)** creating or expanding facial recognition databases "through the **untargeted** scraping of facial images from the internet or CCTV footage"; **(h)** real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement, "unless and in so far as **strictly necessary**" for three enumerated purposes, with Annex II supplying the offence list; and the **2026 insertions, in force 2 December 2026**: **(ba)** non-consensual intimate imagery of an identifiable person, and **(bb)** material within Art. 2(c) and (e) of Directive 2011/93/EU.

Called thereafter: **the red lines**.

**Why this rule and not another:** a legal order whose whole instinct is to regulate rather than forbid drew eight absolute lines. The Commission's stated rationale for (f) is worth quoting: workplaces and schools are **asymmetric environments with a fundamental power imbalance** — a worker or a student cannot meaningfully decline. That is a *consent* rationale, not an accuracy one. The road not taken: the **EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 5/2021** attacked the "positive list" method itself and wanted a **general ban on biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces**. It did not get one; it got Art. 5(1)(h) with three exceptions and a judicial-authorisation regime. **Ep 6 is that argument.**

**The variants — the load-bearing words are the qualifiers:**
1. **Budapest Bank, changed by one fact.** The people whose emotions were inferred were **customers**; the purpose included **rating employees**. Art. 5(1)(f) prohibits inference "in the areas of workplace and education institutions." **Whose workplace — and does the prohibition follow the data subject or the setting?** This case sits exactly on that seam, and the honest answer is that nobody knows yet. *(The docket previously said "employees and customers" — imprecise. It was customers' emotions, employees' evaluation.)*
2. Delete *solely* from (d) and every human-in-the-loop predictive policing tool in Europe changes category.
3. Delete *untargeted* from (e) and targeted scraping — building a database of one suspect's images — becomes prohibited too.
4. The same emotion system, sold for detecting driver drowsiness. **"Safety reasons"** — out of the prohibition, and straight into Annex III.

**The levels:** *technical* — a classifier over acoustic features with contested validity; *organisational* — a performance-management tool aimed at staff via customers; *institutional* — a data-protection regulator reaching a use case before any AI law existed; *economic* — call-centre productivity; *rights-based* — the power asymmetry the Commission names.

**The contested joints:**
- Does (f) follow the setting or the person? — **contested; genuinely unresolved, and this case is the cleanest illustration in Europe.**
- Do the Feb 2025 prohibited-practices guidelines settle anything? — *settled that they do not bind. Worked examples, non-binding, and the binding interpretation will come from courts.*
- Does "reasonably foreseeable and reproducible outcome" in the 2026 insertions make general-purpose image and video providers responsible for what users do? — **still moving, and it is the sharpest live question in the Act. ⚠ The docket's characterisation is secondary; check the operative wording on EUR-Lex before the episode states the standard.**
- Is (a)'s "materially distorting behaviour" ever provable? — *contested; no enforcement to point at.*

**What the rule assumes about the machine:** Art. 5(1)(f) presumes that emotion inference **works well enough to be dangerous**. The research literature suggests the inference is unreliable. Both can be true — an unreliable system deployed with authority over a worker's evaluation is *more* harmful, not less. But note precisely what the Act has done: it has **banned a capability claim**. If the capability does not exist, the prohibition is doing something other than what it says — it is regulating the **use of a vendor's claim as grounds for treating a person a certain way**. That is worth saying plainly, and it is the series' signature move.

**As written against as enforced:** prohibitions applied from **2 February 2025**; penalties for them only from **2 August 2025** — a six-month window with a ban and no sanction. National market-surveillance powers to investigate, demand documents, fine and withdraw from the market went live **2 August 2026**. The two new limbs bite **2 December 2026**. **No confirmed penalty under Art. 5 has been established as of this compilation (13 September 2026).** The only emotion-recognition enforcement anyone can actually point to is the Hungarian one, under a different statute, in 2022.

**Deliberately compressed:** Art. 5(2)–(7) — the authorisation, notification and impact-assessment machinery around real-time biometric identification — is described in structure and not walked through article by article. Annex II is named. The episode says both.

**Handover:** the Act bans eight things and regulates the rest. There was a serious argument that it should have been built the other way round — from the rights at stake rather than from the risk of the product. **One episode away from the instrument, then Episode 7 opens the heaviest chapter in the Regulation.**

**Transfer questions:**
1. Which single word in this prohibition is carrying the weight — and what happens to the analysis if I move it?
2. Is this ban premised on the system working, or on somebody acting as if it does?
3. Is the prohibited thing defined by the setting, the person, or the purpose?
4. When did this prohibition become enforceable, as opposed to applicable?
5. Would a slight change of stated purpose move this out of the ban and into a tier?

**Sources:** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 5(1) in full, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744; Annex II. Commission Guidelines on prohibited AI practices, 4 February 2025. NAIH decision **NAIH-85-3/2022** (GDPRhub). Barrett et al. (2019) on emotion inference *[pending verification]*.

## Episode 6 — [LENS] Risk Against Rights

**Length:** 26 min.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 1, 3, 4, 5 — the gate, roles, the ladder, the red lines. This episode is unintelligible without the design-time bet from Ep 4.

**Opening anchor** — *type: an objection filed before the law existed.* The **EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 5/2021**, **18 June 2021** — two European data-protection institutions reading the Commission's two-month-old proposal and saying, in effect: you have built the wrong kind of instrument.

**Boundary, stated in the episode:** this is an argument *about* rights-based regulation. What a fundamental right is, how the Charter works, and how a court tests a limitation on one is **Series 4, "The Charter."**

**The positions and their ablest defenders:**

1. **Risk-based product safety — the Act as built.** Defended by the Commission and the co-legislators, and on the merits by the argument that it is the only machinery that operates **ex ante and at scale**: it reaches every unit placed on the market, it imposes duties before anyone is harmed, it has an existing enforcement apparatus (market surveillance, withdrawal, CE marking), and it gives firms a determinate answer. **Consequence of adopting it:** obligations are about *process* — documentation, testing, oversight procedures — and compliance is demonstrated by paperwork against a standard.

2. **Rights-based regulation — the road not taken.** **EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 5/2021** is the ablest short statement: it criticised the **"positive list" approach to prohibitions** (you can only ban what you thought of), argued that "risk to fundamental rights" should be aligned with the GDPR's meaning, wanted **societal risks to *groups*** assessed and not only risks to individuals, and called for a **general ban on biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces**. **Consequence:** the trigger is severity of interference with a protected interest, not the category of the product; the assessment is contextual; the remedy runs to the affected person.

3. **The structural critique.** **Michael Veale and Frederik Zuiderveen Borgesius**, *"Demystifying the Draft EU Artificial Intelligence Act,"* **Computer Law Review International 22(4) (2021) 97–112** (preprint arXiv:2107.03721). On the 2021 draft: the Act takes its structure from **four decades of EU product-safety regimes** rather than from fundamental-rights law and inherits that machinery's assumptions — conformity assessment, CE marking, harmonised standards, presumption of conformity. Two consequences they name: reliance on **"1980s product safety regulation"**, and delegation of what are effectively fundamental-rights questions to **"standardisation bodies with no fundamental rights experience."** **Consequence of taking them seriously:** you would not have written Art. 40 the way it is written. **Status: contested but foundational — and dated on specifics, since it addresses the 2021 draft, not the adopted Act or the 2026 amendments. Say that.**

4. **The sectoral alternative.** Let the medicines regulator regulate medical AI, the financial supervisor regulate credit models, the police inspectorate regulate policing tools. **Consequence:** deep competence, no horizontal coherence, twenty-seven times the fragmentation.

**Which way the weight leans:** **on the narrow question of standardisation, the critics have been empirically vindicated** — Ep 8 shows how. **On the broader question of instrument shape, the weight does not lean, and the episode must say so rather than invent a consensus.** The strongest thing that can honestly be said in 2026 is that the product route bought administrability and paid for it in contextual sensitivity and in individual remedy, and the bill for that has not fully arrived.

**Applied to, by number:** **Ep 7** (process obligations versus outcome duties, and what Art. 27's FRIA is doing in a product statute at all); **Ep 8** (standards bodies deciding rights questions); **Ep 11** (a regime with a complaint right and no compensation); **Ep 12** (the verdict).

**Contested joints:** whether Art. 27's fundamental-rights impact assessment is a genuine graft of rights-based method onto product law, or a form nobody will read — *still moving, and it does not apply until 2 December 2027*. Whether the prohibitions in Art. 5 are themselves rights-based regulation smuggled into a product statute — *contested, and a good case can be made that they are.*

**Handover:** the argument is unresolvable in the abstract. So we do the concrete thing: open the heavy chapter and see, requirement by requirement, what a high-risk system's provider actually has to do — and what those duties assume about the human being who is supposed to supervise a machine. **This is the last time we step back before the middle of the series; the next lens episode is Episode 10.**

**Transfer questions:**
1. Does this rule trigger on what the system *is*, or on what it *does to someone*?
2. Whose harm counts here — an individual's, a group's, or the market's?
3. If the developer complies fully, does the affected person get anything?
4. Was the alternative instrument actually proposed by someone, or is it only a debating position?

**Sources:** EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 5/2021, 18 June 2021 (edps.europa.eu). Veale & Zuiderveen Borgesius, *Computer Law Review International* 22(4) (2021) 97–112. Commission proposal COM(2021) 206 final.

## Episode 7 — The Weight

**Length:** 35 min — flagged. Chapter III sections 2 and 3 are the densest obligations in the Act; the alternative is to gut them.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 1, 3, 4, 6 — the gate; provider and deployer; the ladder and Art. 6; the risk/rights argument.

**Opening instance** — *type: an audit report.* **DOT Office of Inspector General, report AV2021020, 23 February 2021: "Weaknesses in FAA's Certification and Delegation Processes Hindered Its Oversight of the 737 MAX 8."** The findings: no risk-based approach to oversight of Boeing's Organization Designation Authorization; **ODA personnel independence from company influence not ensured**; FAA engineers stationed at the Boeing office balancing certification work against oversight duties; oversight insufficient to catch emerging high-risk concerns; and **the FAA did not have a complete understanding of Boeing's safety assessments of MCAS until after the first accident.** An internal FAA survey found **56% of aircraft-certification staff felt there was too much external influence on the agency, affecting safety.** Fourteen recommendations followed; the FAA restored limited certification delegation to Boeing in September 2025.

Open on a single finding read from the report, flat, with its page. Not on the crashes.

**Disclosure:** United States, aviation certification, no AI Act, no AI system in the Act's sense. **This episode uses it for one thing only — what a *human oversight requirement* assumes — and says so at the point it starts.** The delegation half of this report is Episode 8's.

**The cast (in the Act's vocabulary, applied by analogy and flagged as analogy):** Boeing as provider; the airlines as deployers; the flight crew as the **human oversight**; the FAA as a market-surveillance authority that had outsourced its assessment.

**The system, operationally:** **MCAS** — the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System — a flight-control law that commanded nose-down trim of the horizontal stabiliser under certain flight conditions, triggered from angle-of-attack data, and capable of repeated activation. It existed to make the aircraft's handling resemble its predecessor's so that pilots would need minimal retraining. The mitigation assumed in the safety assessment was **existing crew procedure** — recognise runaway stabiliser trim, and cut it out.
*Field's own terms to check the description against: "control augmentation system," "failure hazard classification," "assumed crew response time." **⚠ Verify the mechanism against the OIG report, the Joint Authorities Technical Review and the accident reports before writing. The docket supplies the OIG findings only.***

**The operative text** (this is the chapter, and it is read in pieces across the episode):
> **Art. 14 — human oversight.** High-risk systems "shall be designed and developed in such a way, including with appropriate human-machine interface tools, that they **can be effectively overseen by natural persons** during the period in which they are in use" — including enabling the overseer to **understand the capacities and limitations** of the system, remain aware of **automation bias**, correctly interpret the output, **disregard, override or reverse** it, and intervene or halt.
> **Art. 9** risk management as an iterative process across the lifecycle · **Art. 10** data governance: training, validation and testing data sets "relevant, sufficiently representative, and to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete" · **Art. 11 and Annex IV** technical documentation · **Art. 12** automatic recording of events · **Art. 13** transparency and instructions for use · **Art. 15** accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity · **Art. 16** the provider's consolidated duties · **Art. 17** quality management system · **Arts. 18–21** documentation retention, logs, corrective action, cooperation · **Art. 26** deployer duties, including assigning oversight to competent, trained persons with the necessary authority and support · **Art. 27** the fundamental-rights impact assessment, for public bodies and certain private deployers · **Art. 4** AI literacy.
> And the relief valves: **Arts. 57–63** — regulatory sandboxes, testing in real-world conditions with informed consent, and measures for SMEs.

⚠ Every quotation above is paraphrased from secondary material in the docket. **Verify each against the consolidated text before it is spoken as the Act's words.**

Called thereafter: **the requirements** (8–15), **the file** (Annex IV), and **the overseer** (Art. 14).

**Why this rule and not another:** the alternative to process duties is an **outcome duty** — "your system shall not discriminate", "your system shall be accurate to within X." Outcome duties are unenforceable ex ante against a system whose behaviour depends on deployment context, and they are impossible to certify before anything has happened. So the Act requires a **process that a documentation file can evidence**. What was traded: a provider can satisfy every article in this chapter and still ship a system that harms people, as long as the file is in order. That is not a cynical reading; it is what ex-ante process regulation *is*, and the episode should say it in the Act's own logic rather than as an accusation.

**The variants:**
1. The overseer is given the authority to override but not the information to know when — Art. 14's substance versus Art. 14's form. The Boeing finding is precisely this: the procedure existed; the knowledge that it applied did not.
2. The deployer assigns oversight to a person with the training but without the standing to stop production. **Art. 26 requires "the necessary competence, training and authority."** Change "authority" to "responsibility" and the article becomes decorative.
3. A high-risk system is deployed by a private company rather than a public body. **Art. 27's FRIA duty may not attach at all** — the same system, the same people affected, and one of the Act's two rights-facing instruments disappears.

**The levels:** *technical* — a control law with a single-sensor trigger; *organisational* — a training decision made for commercial reasons; *institutional* — a regulator that did not hold the safety assessment; *economic* — the value of "no simulator training required"; *rights-based* — not reachable by any of this chapter's articles, which is itself the finding.

**The contested joints:**
- Does human oversight work? — **The weight of evidence leans against it in the strong form.** The human-factors literature on automation bias and on vigilance in monitoring tasks is consistent and old, and Art. 14 acknowledges automation bias in its own text while still making a human the mitigation. *[Verify and cite: Parasuraman & Riley on use, misuse and disuse of automation (1997); Skitka et al. on automation bias. Check before naming.]*
- Is Art. 10's "free of errors and complete" achievable for any real dataset? — *contested; widely argued to be a drafting overreach, softened by "to the best extent possible."*
- Is the FRIA a genuine obligation or a template exercise? — *still moving; nothing applies until 2 December 2027.*

**What the rule assumes about the machine — the heart of this episode:** Art. 14 assumes a system whose outputs a trained person can **understand in the time available**, and whose failure modes that person has been told about. It assumes the human is a **control**. Boeing's safety assessment made the same assumption in aviation's own vocabulary: the crew is the mitigation. What the requirement cannot reach is the case where the system's behaviour is unexpected *to the organisation that built it*, and where the overseer's information comes from the provider's own instructions for use. **The Act requires the provider to tell the deployer what the system's limitations are. It has no mechanism for limitations the provider does not know about — which is the category that produces the accidents.**

**As written against as enforced:** none of Chapter III applies before **2 December 2027** for Annex III systems and **2 August 2028** for Annex I. Whether the requirements will be assessed against harmonised standards, and by whom, is **Episode 8** — and the answer, as of June 2026, is that no such standard exists.

**Deliberately compressed, and said aloud:** Annex IV is characterised, not enumerated. Arts. 18–21 are grouped. Chapter VI (sandboxes, real-world testing, SME measures) gets three minutes and is named as a whole chapter compressed — with the honest note that sandboxes are the part of the Act industry likes most and the part with the least evidence behind it. **Post-market monitoring (Art. 72) and serious-incident reporting (Art. 73) are deliberately not taught here: they are the enforcement loop, and Episode 11 has them.**

**Handover:** every requirement in this chapter is demonstrated by conformity assessment. Conformity assessment runs against harmonised standards. So the next question is the one that decides whether any of this is real: **who writes those standards, and do they exist?**

**Transfer questions:**
1. What exactly is the human here supposed to be able to *do*, and in how long?
2. Does the overseer have the information, the training, *and* the authority — or only one of the three?
3. Is this obligation about process or about outcome, and could it be fully met by a system that harms people?
4. Who wrote the instructions for use, and what do they *not* say?
5. If the deployer is a private firm rather than a public body, which of these duties quietly vanishes?

**Sources:** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Arts. 4, 8–21, 26, 27, 57–63, Annex IV. DOT OIG, AV2021020, 23 February 2021. Human-factors literature on automation bias *[pending verification]*.

## Episode 8 — The Presumption

**Length:** 33 min — flagged. Notified bodies, standards, conformity assessment, CE marking and the deferral decision are one causal chain and splitting them destroys the argument.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 4, 6, 7 — the ladder; the risk/rights argument, including Veale and Zuiderveen Borgesius on standardisation; the Chapter III requirements that this episode's machinery is supposed to certify.

**Opening instance** — *type: a standards committee suspending its own procedure.* **October 2025: CEN and CENELEC adopted exceptional measures to accelerate AI standards work** — reducing publication stages and using smaller drafting groups — because the harmonised standards the high-risk regime depends on were not going to exist for the **2 August 2026** deadline. **Technical committee members publicly warned that this could undermine consensus and produce "serious unintended consequences."** ⚠ *The docket does not name the members who said this. Find a named, quotable source before the episode attributes it, or attribute it to the reported record and say so.*

Open in the committee, on the vote to change the rules.

**The cast:** **CEN** and **CENELEC**, private standards associations; **JTC 21**, their joint technical committee for AI; the **Commission**, which issues the standardisation request and decides whether to cite a standard in the Official Journal; **notified bodies**; the **providers** who will self-assess; and **Marta Cantero Gamito**, whose REALaw post of **28 November 2025**, *"From Consensus to Exceptionality — What the EU's AI Standards Crisis Reveals About Delegated Technical Governance,"* is the sharpest short account.

**The system, operationally — the machinery is the system in this episode:**
1. The Commission issues a **standardisation request** to CEN-CENELEC under Regulation (EU) 1025/2012. *[Verify the AI Act request's reference and date — commonly cited as M/593, May 2023. Confirm before speaking it.]*
2. A technical committee drafts; national standards bodies vote; an enquiry stage and a formal vote follow; the text is published as an EN.
3. **The Commission assesses it and cites it in the Official Journal.** Only then does it become a *harmonised* standard.
4. A provider that conforms to a cited harmonised standard enjoys a **presumption of conformity** with the corresponding requirement of the Act (**Art. 40**). If standards are missing or inadequate, the Commission may adopt **common specifications** by implementing act (**Art. 41**), through the comitology procedure of **Arts. 97–98**.
5. **Art. 43** decides who checks: for **Annex III points 2–8**, "providers shall follow the conformity assessment procedure based on internal control as referred to in Annex VI, **which does not provide for the involvement of a notified body**." For **Annex III point 1 (biometrics)** the provider chooses internal control or a notified-body route under Annex VII — and where such systems are for **law enforcement, immigration, asylum or EU institutions**, "**the market surveillance authority shall act as a notified body.**"
6. **Art. 47** declaration of conformity; **Art. 48** CE marking; **Arts. 28–39** the accreditation and supervision of notified bodies.

**The operative text:** Art. 40 (presumption), Art. 43 (allocation), Art. 41 (common specifications), Art. 48 (CE marking). Called thereafter: **the presumption**, and **self-assessment**.

**Why this rule and not another:** delegating technical detail to standards bodies is the **New Approach** — the mechanism that made the single market work from 1985 onwards. The legislature sets essential requirements; private experts write the specifications; conformity to the specification buys a presumption. It scales, it updates faster than a statute, and it puts the drafting where the expertise is. What was traded: **the substance.** Veale and Zuiderveen Borgesius predicted in 2021 that this would hand fundamental-rights questions to "standardisation bodies with no fundamental rights experience." Note what the Act itself does in **Art. 67**: the Advisory Forum's **permanent members are the Fundamental Rights Agency, ENISA, CEN, CENELEC and ETSI** — the standards bodies sit permanently beside the rights agency. **The Act saw the objection and answered it with a seating plan.**

**The historical instance, mid-episode — the machinery under load.** ***Dieselgate*, EU, 2015 onwards. Not counterfactual: this happened inside EU product-safety law.** Volkswagen fitted software that recognised the conditions of the **type-approval test** and ran full emissions control only during it — roughly **8.5 million affected cars in Europe**. The CJEU held such defeat devices unlawful under Art. 5(2)(a) of Regulation 715/2007 and rejected the engine-protection defence: **C-128/20, C-134/20 and C-145/20, judgments of 14 July 2022** (referrals from the Austrian Supreme Court and the regional courts of **Eisenstadt** and **Klagenfurt**), on a "thermal window" that ran full exhaust-gas recirculation only between **15°C and 33°C and below 1,000 metres**; and earlier, **C-693/18**. The European Parliament ran an inquiry (**EMIS**, report A8-0049/2017). Afterwards, **Member States applied neither financial nor legal penalties to manufacturers**: no mandatory recalls or retrofits, no withdrawal of type approvals, and VW maintained it had never breached EU rules. The framework was later revised to require Member States to run a minimum number of independent tests a year.
**The transfer, stated exactly: the product was built to pass the assessment rather than to meet the standard. An AI system tuned to perform on the benchmark used for its conformity assessment, and differently in deployment, is the identical failure with different physics.**

**Where it stands — date-stamped, and this is the series' verdict question in one fact:** **as of June 2026, not one JTC 21 deliverable grants presumption of conformity, because none has been cited in the Official Journal.** **EN 18286** — a quality-management-system standard for AI Act purposes — is the most advanced, at **Approval** stage, and the likeliest to be cited first. The original **April 2025** deadline for the standards was missed; the **October 2025** acceleration was the response; in **November 2025** the Commission tabled the Digital Omnibus; and the high-risk deadline moved to **2 December 2027** (Annex III) and **2 August 2028** (Annex I), by **Regulation (EU) 2026/1744** (OJ 24 July 2026, in force 27 July 2026), which also amended the EASA Regulation 2018/1139 and the Machinery Regulation 2023/1230. **Status: still moving. Say so, and date it.**

**The variants:**
1. A high-risk system in Annex III point 5 (essential services) — **self-assessed against a standard that does not exist.** What, concretely, does the provider assess against? Art. 43's answer in the absence of standards is that the provider must demonstrate conformity with the requirements directly. Nobody knows what that looks like.
2. The same system, moved into Annex III point 1 and deployed for immigration. **The market surveillance authority becomes the notified body** — regulator and certifier in one office.
3. EN 18286 is cited in the OJ in early 2027. Every provider that implements it gets a presumption covering its quality-management obligations — **and none of its accuracy, robustness or fundamental-rights obligations.** A presumption is only as wide as the standard it rests on.

**The levels:** *technical* — a specification document; *organisational* — small drafting groups replacing consensus; *institutional* — private associations holding the substance of a public regulation, with the Commission holding a veto it exercises by citation; *economic* — standards participation costs money, which selects for who is in the room; *rights-based* — the Veale/Zuiderveen Borgesius objection, now empirically live.

**The contested joints:**
- Can conformity assessment against a technical standard discharge a fundamental-rights duty? — **contested, and no longer only theoretically.**
- Was the acceleration of the standards process a pragmatic rescue or a legitimacy failure? — *contested; Cantero Gamito's framing is that exceptionality became the method.*
- Should the Commission use **Art. 41 common specifications** if standards slip again? — *still moving; it is the most consequential unexercised power in the Act, because it would move the substance from a private body into an implementing act.*
- Is self-assessment for most of Annex III defensible? — *contested, and the Boeing ODA record from Episode 7 is the strongest available evidence against.*

**What the rule assumes about the machine:** the presumption of conformity assumes that **a system's compliance can be established by testing it against a written specification**, and that the specification is a good proxy for the requirement. That holds well for a lift. It holds badly for a system whose performance depends on the distribution of the data it meets after deployment — which is why *"Lost in Vagueness: Towards Context-Sensitive Standards for Robustness Assessment under the EU AI Act"* (arXiv 2511.15620) exists. **Dieselgate proved the proxy can be gamed when it is a fixed test; the AI case is worse, because the deployment distribution moves on its own.**

**As written against as enforced:** the regime this machinery serves does not apply until December 2027. The machinery itself has, at the point of this compilation, **produced nothing that confers the presumption it exists to confer.**

**Deliberately compressed:** Arts. 28–39 (notifying authorities, notified body accreditation, subsidiaries, challenges to competence) are taught as one institution rather than twelve articles. Arts. 44–46 (certificates, derogations) are named. The episode says so.

**Handover:** everything so far assumed a system built for a purpose. The next chapter concerns models built for **no particular purpose at all** — and the machinery in this episode has no idea what to do with them.

**Transfer questions:**
1. Who checks this, and are they the people who built it?
2. What document does the checker check against — and has anyone published it?
3. Could this system be built to pass the test rather than to meet the requirement?
4. What exactly does the certificate cover, and what does it leave uncovered?
5. If the specification is late, does the obligation move, or does the deadline?

**Sources:** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Arts. 40, 41, 43, 47, 48, Annexes VI and VII. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744. CEN-CENELEC announcements on exceptional measures, October 2025. Cantero Gamito, REALaw, 28 November 2025. *"Standards and the EU AI Act: legitimacy, state of play, and future challenges,"* Information & Communications Technology Law (2025). CJEU C-128/20, C-134/20, C-145/20, 14 July 2022; C-693/18. European Parliament EMIS report A8-0049/2017.

## Episode 9 — The General Case

**Length:** 32 min — carries Chapter V, Chapter X, Art. 50 and the two codes of practice.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 1, 3, 4, 8 — the gate (and that a *model* is not a *system*); provider and deployer; the ladder, including the limited-risk rung this episode now fills in; the standards machinery, because GPAI is governed by a **code of practice** instead, and the contrast is the point.

**Opening instance** — *type: a country switching a service off.* **30 March 2023: the Italian Garante per la protezione dei dati personali orders ChatGPT blocked in Italy.** Access was restored about a month later. Italy was first. The Garante then fined **OpenAI €15 million** — *provvedimento* **n. 755**, decision dated **2 November 2024**, widely reported in December 2024 — for processing personal data to train ChatGPT **without an adequate legal basis**, inadequate transparency to users, inadequate age verification, and failure to notify the March 2023 breach within 72 hours. It also used, for the first time, its power under Art. 166(7) of the Italian Data Protection Code to order a **six-month public awareness campaign** across radio, television, print and online media.

**Disclosure:** a GDPR action. There was no AI-specific regime to use. **The GDPR itself is Series 2.**

**The twist, held for the "as enforced" beat, not spent at the top:** on **18 March 2026** the **Court of Rome**, Judge **Damiana Colla**, annulled *provvedimento* n. 755 — **on jurisdiction, not on the merits.** Once OpenAI established **OpenAI Ireland Limited**, the GDPR's one-stop-shop made the **Irish Data Protection Commission** lead supervisory authority — reportedly **from 15 February 2024**, months before the Garante's final decision — so the Garante had no competence to issue a stand-alone national sanction. **The court never reached the substantive allegations at all.** The decision did not find OpenAI innocent; it found that Italy had no right to judge OpenAI. *(European Law Blog: "Establish, Then Escape? How the Court of Rome, the One-Stop-Shop and a Single Word Opened an AI Enforcement Gap." ⚠ Now multi-sourced — Wilson Sonsini, European Law Blog, trade press — but the judgment itself has not been read. Say that the reasoning is reported, not read.)*

**The cast:** the provider of a **general-purpose AI model**; **downstream providers** who build systems on it; the **AI Office**, which supervises GPAI centrally; the **Scientific Panel of Independent Experts** (Art. 68), which may alert the AI Office to systemic risks; **notified bodies — absent, because this regime has none.**

**The system, operationally, before any rule applies:** a transformer language model, pretrained by next-token prediction over a large corpus, then post-trained by supervised fine-tuning and preference optimisation. The quantity the Act reaches for is **training compute**, measured in floating-point operations — a number that can be estimated from parameter count and token count without anyone seeing the model. **Why that number was chosen:** empirical scaling relationships between compute, data, parameters and loss made compute the best available public proxy for capability around 2022–2023. *[Verify a citable scaling-laws reference — Kaplan et al. 2020; Hoffmann et al. 2022 — before naming one.]*
**What the proxy misses, as of 2026:** post-training and distillation can produce strong models below the line; **inference-time compute** — models that spend more computation reasoning at the point of use — buys capability that training-compute thresholds cannot see at all. **This is the episode's live technical beat and it should be stated as contested, not as settled.**

**The operative text:**
> **Art. 51** — classification of a general-purpose AI model as having **systemic risk**, including the presumption where cumulative training compute exceeds **10²⁵ floating-point operations**; **Annex XIII** supplies the criteria. **Art. 52** — notification to the Commission. **Art. 53** — obligations of all GPAI providers: technical documentation (**Annex XI**), information for downstream providers (**Annex XII**), a **copyright policy**, and a **sufficiently detailed summary of the content used for training**. **Art. 55** — systemic-risk obligations: model evaluation, systemic-risk assessment and mitigation, serious-incident reporting, cybersecurity. **Art. 56** — codes of practice.
> **Art. 50** — transparency: interactive systems must tell people they are dealing with **AI and not a human**; deepfakes must be **labelled**; AI-generated or manipulated content must carry **machine-readable marks** so it can be detected.
> **Arts. 88–94** — the Commission's enforcement arm for GPAI: monitoring, alerts from the Scientific Panel, **requests for information**, evaluations, measures, procedural rights. **Art. 101** — the **Commission** may fine GPAI providers up to **3% of worldwide annual turnover or €15 million**.

Called thereafter: **the model regime** (as against the system regime), and **the threshold**.

**Date-stamped facts, spoken with their dates:** GPAI obligations apply from **2 August 2025**. The **GPAI Code of Practice** was published **10 July 2025** and declared adequate **1 August 2025**; three chapters — **Transparency, Copyright, Safety and Security** — the last binding only providers of systemic-risk models; **more than 20 providers had signed by August 2026**. The AI Office's enforcement powers over GPAI went live **2 August 2026**. On **31 July 2026** the Commission published a first list of **more than 180 organisations** signing a **second and distinct** code — the **Code of Practice on transparency of AI-generated content**. The 10²⁵ FLOP threshold is reported as capturing roughly **5–15 companies worldwide**.

**Why this rule and not another:** the alternatives were (a) do nothing about models and regulate only systems built from them — the shape of the 2021 proposal, which ChatGPT destroyed; (b) put GPAI into the high-risk regime, which would have required conformity assessment against standards for a thing with no intended purpose; (c) **a capability-based threshold** — regulate models that can do specified dangerous things — which is what everyone wanted and nobody could operationalise. The Act chose a **compute proxy plus a code of practice**, because a compute threshold is administrable and a code of practice could be written in months rather than years. What was traded: **the threshold does not measure what anyone cares about**, and the code is voluntary in form even where the obligations behind it are not.

**The variants:**
1. A model trained just under 10²⁵ FLOP and then heavily post-trained into frontier capability. **Below the line by the presumption; the Annex XIII criteria and a Commission designation decision are the only route back in.**
2. A model released under a free and open-source licence. Parts of the GPAI regime soften — **but not for systemic-risk models.** Change one fact — the model crosses the threshold — and the open-source relief largely disappears.
3. The Air Canada chatbot from Episode 3, rebuilt on a general-purpose model today. **Art. 50(1) now requires it to say it is not a person** — and says nothing at all about whether what it says is true. *That gap is worth naming twice.*

**The levels:** *technical* — FLOP as a measurable proxy for an unmeasurable property; *organisational* — a code of practice signed by the parties it regulates; *institutional* — supervision centralised in the Commission rather than spread over twenty-seven authorities; *economic* — a threshold that names 5–15 firms is also a definition of an industry structure; *rights-based* — nothing here runs to an individual.

**The contested joints:**
- Does 10²⁵ FLOP track anything real about risk? — **contested, and the argument is getting stronger against as inference-time compute grows.**
- Is a code of practice co-written with industry an adequate instrument for systemic risk? — *contested; its defenders point out it exists, which the standards do not.*
- Does the Act's copyright chapter settle anything about training data? — **No. The training-data summary duty is taught here; the copyright substance is Series 3, "Who Pays."**
- Is centralised supervision the right answer? — *the Garante story is the argument for it, made by events; still contested on competence and resourcing grounds.*

**What the rule assumes about the machine:** the GPAI chapter assumes that **a model's risk can be estimated from how it was made rather than from what it does** — because at the point of regulation, nobody, including its builder, knows what it does. That is an honest response to a real epistemic problem, and it is also a confession: the Act's entire system-side architecture runs on *intended purpose*, and for these models there isn't one. **Chapter V is the Act admitting that its own organising concept does not apply to the most consequential technology it governs.**

**As written against as enforced:** AI Office enforcement powers live from **2 August 2026**. The AI Office reportedly sent its first **requests for information** to frontier GPAI providers around **29 August 2026** — *secondary and unconfirmed*. **No confirmed penalty under the Act has been established as of 13 September 2026.** And Europe's most famous enforcement action against a general-purpose model — the Garante's €15 million — **was annulled by a court, on the ground that the regulator was not allowed to decide.**

**Deliberately compressed:** Art. 54 (authorised representatives for non-EU model providers) is named only. Annexes XI, XII and XIII are characterised rather than enumerated. The Code of Practice's Copyright chapter is described and its substance handed to Series 3, aloud.

**Handover:** a regime that reaches 5–15 companies, most of them outside the Union, raises a question the Act cannot answer from inside itself: does any of this travel? **We step back for one episode — Episode 10 — and then Episode 11 asks who is actually watching.**

**Transfer questions:**
1. What is the threshold measuring, and what is it a proxy *for*?
2. Could a system get the capability the rule worries about without crossing the number the rule uses?
3. Is this obligation on the model or on the system built from it — and who is it that owes it?
4. If this instrument is a code of practice, what happens to a provider who declines to sign?
5. When a regulator loses a case on jurisdiction, what has actually been decided?

**Sources:** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Arts. 50, 51–56, 88–94, 101, Annexes XI–XIII. GPAI Code of Practice, 10 July 2025. Code of Practice on transparency of AI-generated content, first signatory list 31 July 2026. Commission press release IP/26/1714, 31 July 2026. Garante *provvedimento* n. 755, 2 November 2024. Court of Rome, 18 March 2026 (reported; judgment not yet read). European Law Blog, *"Establish, Then Escape?"*

## Episode 10 — [LENS] Does It Travel

**Length:** 24 min.

**Prerequisites:** Eps 4, 8, 9 — the ladder; the standards machinery; the GPAI regime and its 5–15 firms.

**Opening anchor** — *type: two research teams counting the same thing and disagreeing.* One paper says the AI Act will reshape the global market by default; another counts the copycat statutes and finds almost none.

**Boundary, said in the episode:** this is a lens on **a mechanism of influence**, not a tour of other countries' laws. **The comparative survey — the United States, China, the Council of Europe Framework Convention, Brazil, Canada — is Series 5, "Everyone Else's Instrument."**

**The positions and their ablest defenders:**
1. **The Brussels effect** (Anu Bradford's framework). Two mechanisms: the **de facto** effect, where a firm finds it cheaper to apply the strictest rule globally than to maintain two products; and the **de jure** effect, where other legislatures copy the rule. **Consequence of adopting it:** you stop asking whether other countries will regulate and start asking whether the product is divisible.
2. **For, applied to AI:** *"The Brussels Effect and Artificial Intelligence: How EU regulation will impact the global AI market"* (GovAI, arXiv:2208.12645) — de facto and de jure effects likely for **parts** of the Act. **Consequence:** documentation and evaluation practices spread even where the statute does not.
3. **Against:** CEPA, *"Burying the Brussels Effect? AI Act Inspires Few Copycats"* — limited diffusion; **Canada's AIDA and Brazil's risk-tiered bill are the main echoes, and Brazil's final shape is uncertain**. **Consequence:** the Act is a regional rule with compliance spillovers, not a global standard.
4. **Inverted:** *"The Brussels Side-Effect: How the AI Act Can Reduce the Global Reach of EU Policy"* (German Law Journal). Strictness can cause **withdrawal** — features shipped everywhere but Europe — which reduces EU influence rather than extending it.
5. **Reframed:** *"Brussels effect or experimentalism? The EU AI Act and global standard-setting"* (Internet Policy Review) — the mechanism is not export but iterative co-development through standards bodies, which are international.
6. **The critique of the frame itself:** that "Brussels effect" confuses **compliance with legitimate authority**, and assumes the universality of European liberal values.

**Which way the weight leans: it does not.** This is genuinely unsettled, and the episode's job is to make the listener able to tell which mechanism they are looking at, not to pick a winner. **Do not manufacture balance and do not manufacture a conclusion.**

**Applied to, by number:** **Ep 8** (if standards are the real substance, and standards bodies are international, then influence travels through ISO/IEC rather than through the statute — position 5's strongest case); **Ep 9** (5–15 firms, mostly non-EU, and a code of practice they chose to sign: a test case for the de facto mechanism); **Ep 11** (Clearview: a foreign firm that simply declined, which is what the de facto mechanism looks like when it fails); **Ep 12**.

**Contested joints:** whether **Art. 2(1)(c)** — output used in the Union — is a jurisdictional reach that works or one that cannot be enforced, *still moving, and Episode 11 supplies the evidence*.

**Handover:** every version of this argument depends on something being enforceable. So: who is actually watching, what can they do, and what have they done? **This is the last lens episode. Episode 11 is machinery, and Episode 12 is the verdict.**

**Transfer questions:**
1. Is the product divisible — can the firm ship a different version here?
2. Is the influence running through the statute, or through a standard nobody voted on?
3. Who is copying this, and are they copying the structure or only the vocabulary?
4. Does compliance here signal that the rule is authoritative, or only that it is cheaper to obey than to litigate?

**Sources:** GovAI, arXiv:2208.12645. CEPA, *"Burying the Brussels Effect?"*. *German Law Journal*, "The Brussels Side-Effect." *Internet Policy Review*, "Brussels effect or experimentalism?". Bradford's Brussels-effect framework *[verify edition before citing]*.

## Episode 11 — Who Is Actually Watching

**Length:** 34 min. **⚠ Overloaded — flagged in the coverage map. My recommendation is to split it (11a governance, 11b enforcement) and run a 13-episode series. The plan below is the single-episode version, with the compressions named.**

**Prerequisites:** Eps 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9 — roles; the tiers and their dates; the prohibitions; the Chapter III duties; conformity assessment; the GPAI regime's separate enforcement arm.

**Opening instance** — *type: a sum of money that was never collected.* **Clearview AI.** A US company that scraped the open web for faces — **it claims more than 60 billion images** — and sold facial recognition to law enforcement. **Five European authorities have fined it roughly €105 million in total**: the Dutch **Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens** **€30.5m** (2024); the Italian **Garante** **€20m** (February 2022); the French **CNIL** **€20m** (October 2022, plus **€5.2m** in overdue-penalty payments); the Greek **HDPA** **€20m** (July 2022); and the UK **ICO** **£7.5m** (2022). The Dutch authority found processing without a legal basis under Arts. 5(1) and 6(1) GDPR and unlawful processing of biometric special-category data under Art. 9(1).

**Clearview disputes EU jurisdiction and has not paid. Collection of every one of those fines remains contested.** Open on the total, then the amount collected.

> **Method beat, stated in the episode:** "more than 60 billion images" is **Clearview's own claim**. It is evidence about Clearview, not about the database. The same discipline applies to every vendor number in this series.

**Disclosure:** GDPR enforcement, not AI Act enforcement. Under the Act, untargeted scraping to build a facial-recognition database is **Art. 5(1)(e)** — prohibited since 2 February 2025, penalisable since 2 August 2025. The counterfactual is announced where it starts.

**The cast, and this is the episode's spine — three enforcers, not one.** From Commission press release **IP/26/1714, 31 July 2026**, responsibility for enforcing the transparency rules and the prohibited practices is **split three ways**:
- **The AI Office** — for AI systems offered by the **same provider as the underlying GPAI model**, and for systems integrated into **very large online platforms or search engines** designated under the Digital Services Act. *(The DSA itself is Series 3.)*
- **National competent authorities** — for **all other AI systems**.
- **The European Data Protection Supervisor** — for AI systems used by **EU institutions, bodies and agencies**.

Plus: the **AI Office** (Art. 64), inside the Commission; the **European AI Board** (Arts. 65–66), Member State representatives plus the Commission, coordinating consistent application and issuing opinions, recommendations and guidance; **national competent authorities** (Art. 70), each Member State designating at least one **notifying authority** and one **market surveillance authority**; and **notified bodies** from Episode 8.

**The operative text:**
> **Art. 74** — market surveillance: the post-market work. Investigate complaints, demand documentation, require corrective measures, fine, **withdraw from the market**. **Live from 2 August 2026.**
> **Arts. 72–73** — the provider's post-market monitoring plan, and reporting of **serious incidents**.
> **Art. 71** — the **EU database** of registered high-risk systems (**Annex VIII**), with **Annex X** governing the law-enforcement and migration entries.
> **Art. 85** — the right of any natural or legal person to **lodge a complaint** with a market surveillance authority. **Art. 86** — a right to an explanation of individual decision-making for certain Annex III decisions. **Art. 87** — whistleblower protection.
> **Art. 99** — penalties, **laid down and applied by Member States**:

| Infringement | Ceiling |
|---|---|
| Prohibited practices (Art. 5) | **€35m or 7%** of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher |
| Other obligations — Arts. 16, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 31, 33, 34, 50 | **€15m or 3%** |
| Incorrect, incomplete or misleading information to authorities | **€7.5m or 1%** |
| SMEs and start-ups (Art. 99(6)) | the **lower** of the percentage or the amount |

> **Art. 100** — fines on Union institutions, imposed by the EDPS. **Art. 111** — legacy systems already on the market. **Art. 113** — the staged application dates.

Called thereafter: **market surveillance**, and **the three-way split**.

**The timeline, taught as an instrument in its own right** — each date spoken with the thing it switches on: **2 February 2025** definitions, AI literacy, prohibitions · **2 August 2025** penalties for prohibited practices, and GPAI obligations · **2 August 2026** Art. 50 transparency, AI Office powers over GPAI, and national market-surveillance powers · **2 December 2026** the two new Art. 5 limbs · **2 December 2027** Annex III high-risk · **2 August 2028** Annex I high-risk. **The six-month gap in 2025 between a live prohibition and any penalty for breaching it is a teaching point, not a footnote.** *(Why the last two dates moved is Episode 8's; this episode teaches what the staging does, not why it slipped.)*

**Why this rule and not another:** enforcement could have been centralised in one European agency, as it effectively is for GPAI. It was decentralised because **market surveillance is a Member State competence** in the product-safety tradition this Act borrows from, and because centralised enforcement of every hiring tool in twenty-seven countries is not feasible. What was traded: **twenty-seven levels of capacity, expertise and appetite.** The Commission's own caveat, worth quoting exactly: *"Effective enforcement will also depend on Member States ensuring that national competent authorities are properly designated and adequately resourced."*

**The variants:**
1. Clearview, today, offering the same service to an EU police force. **Art. 5(1)(e)** prohibition; **Art. 99** penalties up to 7% of worldwide turnover — imposed by a **national** authority, and **collectible against a US company with no EU establishment by exactly the mechanisms that have so far collected nothing.**
2. The same company with an EU subsidiary. Collection becomes possible — and, as Episode 9 showed with OpenAI, **so does a jurisdictional argument about which authority may decide.** Establishing in Europe makes you reachable and makes you harder to reach.
3. A hiring platform breaching Annex III documentation duties. **Not the AI Office's to fine. Art. 99, a national authority, from 2 December 2027.**

**The method beat this episode is built to carry — the fines that were not.** Two or more aggregator sites report that the EU AI Office issued the Act's first fines shortly after 2 August 2026: commonly **€47 million** across a hiring platform (€18m), a credit-scoring provider (€14m) and a retail chain (€15m). **Three reasons it is not true, and the third is the transferable one:**
1. **No authoritative source.** A search restricted to `europa.eu` returns the enforcement framework pages and the 2 August announcement and **no press release announcing any fine.**
2. **The Commission's own release says otherwise.** **IP/26/1714, 31 July 2026** describes the machinery being switched on — the Scientific Panel, complaints tools, whistleblower channels — and **names no company and no penalty.**
3. **It does not fit the architecture.** Under **Art. 101** the Commission may fine providers of **general-purpose AI models**. Penalties under **Art. 99** are laid down and applied by **Member States**. A hiring platform, a credit scorer and a retail chain fall to **national competent authorities** — not to the AI Office the reports credit.
**And, checked again on 13 September 2026: the story now exists in at least four mutually incompatible versions — €47m in August 2026, €85m in March 2026, a €45m/€28m/€12m trio, a separate €4.2m retail fine — none traceable to an official source.** The episode reports this as what it is: **a fabrication propagating through content farms, detectable without any special knowledge by anyone who has learned which body may fine whom.** That is the single most useful thing this series can hand a listener, and it is why the architecture was taught before the enforcement.

**The honest state of enforcement, dated 13 September 2026:** powers went live **2 August 2026**; the AI Office **reportedly** sent first **requests for information** to frontier GPAI providers around **29 August 2026** *(secondary, unconfirmed)*; **no confirmed penalty, formal proceeding or fine under the AI Act has been established.**

**The levels:** *technical* — a scraped embedding index; *organisational* — five regulators acting separately; *institutional* — a three-way split of competence and no European bailiff; *economic* — €105m assessed, nothing collected, and the arithmetic of simply not paying; *rights-based* — a complaint right under Art. 85 that produces an investigation and not a remedy.

**The contested joints:**
- Are national market surveillance authorities resourced for this? — **The weight leans clearly to no, and the Commission has said so in its own words.**
- Does Art. 86's right to an explanation add anything? — *contested. ⚠ The comparison to GDPR Art. 22 is Series 2's; this episode teaches Art. 86 and says where the comparison goes.*
- Can fines be collected from non-EU firms without EU establishment? — **still moving, and Clearview is the evidence: five authorities, €105 million, no money.**

**What the rule assumes about the machine — and here, about the institution:** the market-surveillance model assumes a **product you can withdraw**. Withdrawal is the sanction that makes product safety bite: a defective lift is removed. A scraped face database is not withdrawable; a model served from a foreign data centre is not recallable; an embedding, once computed, does not come back. **The Act inherited an enforcement tool whose central move does not exist for its subject matter**, and what it has instead is money it cannot always collect.

**Deliberately compressed — and this list is what goes if the episode cannot flex:** Arts. 75–78 (mutual assistance, supervision of real-world testing, the powers of fundamental-rights authorities, confidentiality) and Arts. 79–83 (the procedures for systems presenting a risk, compliant systems presenting a risk, and formal non-compliance) are described as a single escalation ladder, not walked through. Art. 84 is named. The episode says aloud that a whole procedural chapter has been summarised, and that Art. 77 — which lets national fundamental-rights bodies demand documentation — is the most under-noticed provision in the Act.

**Handover:** we have now built the whole instrument, from the gate to the fine. One question has been held since Episode 1, and it is the only one left: was a **product** the right thing to regulate?

**Transfer questions:**
1. If this rule were broken, who exactly would find out, and by what mechanism?
2. Which body may fine this actor — and does the claim I just read match that architecture?
3. Can the sanction actually be collected, and from whom?
4. Is the remedy here the *removal of a product*, and does removal mean anything for this system?
5. When did this obligation become applicable, and when did it become enforceable?

## Episode 12 — [VERDICT] A Product

**Length:** 38 min — the verdict episode, and the only one permitted to be long because it is the argument rather than the machinery.

**Prerequisites:** all eleven. This episode assumes the whole instrument.

**Opening instance** — *type: a document that does not exist yet.* **EN 18286**, a quality-management-system standard for AI Act purposes, at **Approval** stage, the most advanced of the CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 deliverables and the likeliest to be the first cited in the Official Journal. **As of June 2026 it had not been cited, and neither had anything else — so not one deliverable grants presumption of conformity.** The high-risk regime it serves applies from **2 December 2027**.

Open on the empty Official Journal citation list, and the date on the calendar.

**The question, stated as it was held:** since Episode 1 this series has said it would not answer this until now. **Was regulating artificial intelligence as a product the right choice?**

**The competing answers, and who holds them:**

1. **Yes — and there was no serious alternative that scales.** Held by the Commission and the co-legislators, and defensible on its own terms: product-safety machinery is the only European apparatus that imposes duties **before** harm, reaches **every unit** placed on the market, has an **existing enforcement network**, and yields determinate obligations firms can plan against. Rights-based regulation is contextual, slow, and post-hoc; sectoral regulation fragments. **The evidence planted for this answer:** Ep 4 (the ladder gives an answerable question), Ep 7 (Chapter III is a genuinely demanding engineering programme, whatever else it is), Ep 9 (the GPAI chapter was written and in force within two years of the technology arriving).

2. **No — it delegated rights questions to people with no competence in them.** **Veale and Zuiderveen Borgesius (2021)**: "1980s product safety regulation," and fundamental-rights questions handed to "standardisation bodies with no fundamental rights experience." **The evidence planted:** Ep 6 (the objection, in its own words), Ep 8 (the objection empirically vindicated — the standards were late, the process suspended its own consensus norms, and the deadline moved to fit the standards rather than the standards moving to fit the law), Ep 3 (a regime that produces no remedy for the person harmed), Ep 11 (a complaint right that yields an investigation, and €105 million in adjacent fines that yielded no money).

3. **No — it should have started from the rights.** **EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 5/2021**: the positive-list method cannot anticipate; societal risks to groups are invisible to it; biometric identification in public should have been banned outright. **The evidence planted:** Ep 4 (the design-time bet, and a classification that does not move when nationality is removed from the model), Ep 5 (the prohibitions are rights-shaped and sit awkwardly in a product statute), Ep 7 (Art. 27's FRIA as a graft).

4. **The frame is misposed — what was regulated was neither a product nor a right, but a market.** The Act's operative concept is **placing on the market** and **putting into service**; its sanctions are market sanctions; its actors are supply-chain roles. On this reading the useful question is not "product or right" but *"is a market instrument capable of governing a system whose harm is contextual?"* **The evidence planted:** Ep 3 (roles, not technologies), Ep 9 (a model regime with no market concept that fits it), Ep 11 (withdrawal as a sanction that does not exist for this subject matter).

5. **The pragmatic answer: it was right and it was unfinished.** The frame is defensible; the infrastructure was not built. **The evidence planted:** Ep 8, in full.

**Where the weight actually leans, said honestly and separately for the two questions:**
- **On the narrow standardisation question, the critics have been vindicated by events, and this should be stated without hedging.** The prediction was made in 2021; by June 2026 no deliverable conferred the presumption; the law moved to fit the standards. That is not a matter of opinion.
- **On the broad question of instrument shape, the record is not in, and the episode must say so rather than close the series with a manufactured conclusion.** What it should do instead is name the fact that would settle it: **whether EN 18286 and the deliverables behind it are cited in the Official Journal in time for 2 December 2027, and whether the deadline holds or moves a second time.** If the substance arrives, the product frame will have been slow and workable. If the deadline moves again, the critics' structural claim — that this machinery cannot carry this subject matter — stops being a prediction.

**The Act's own answer to the question:** **Art. 112**, the evaluation and review clause. The instrument contains a scheduled moment for asking whether it worked. The episode should end on what that clause requires and when, because the honest close to a series about an unfinished instrument is a date, not a verdict about the future.

**What the writer must say out loud:** that the series held this question deliberately, since Episode 1; that the holding was so the listener would have the machinery before the argument; and which of the five answers the narrator finds strongest, with the reason — and what would change their mind.

**Deliberately compressed:** no new apparatus is taught except Art. 112.

**Transfer questions (the series' closing set):**
1. When a rule is late because its technical substance is late, has the law been delayed or has it been rewritten?
2. Who wrote the part of this rule that actually decides cases?
3. Does this instrument give the person harmed anything at all?
4. What single, checkable fact would tell me in twelve months whether this regime works?
5. What is the strongest argument against my own view here, and who makes it best?

**Sources:** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 112. Veale & Zuiderveen Borgesius (2021). EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 5/2021. Cantero Gamito (2025). CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 status, June 2026, including EN 18286 at Approval. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744.
