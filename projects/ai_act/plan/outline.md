## Episode 1 — The Gate

**Length:** 34 min. Long for a first episode because it carries eight load-bearing terms and the concept the whole series rests on.

**Prerequisites:** none. This is the entry point; every term afterwards is earned inside the series.

**Opening instance** — *type: a deployment.* The **Criminaliteits Anticipatie Systeem (CAS)**, run by the **Nederlandse Politie**. Piloted 2015, national from 2017, **used until mid-December 2025**; discontinuation reported February 2026. The Netherlands was the first country to deploy predictive policing nationally. The police stopped it because its operational value was unclear: **no clear goals and no measurable success criteria**, and in Amsterdam **roughly one incident in fifty was correctly predicted**. The public artefact is Dutch Algorithm Register entry **81228922**.

*The move: open on the ending. A decade-old national system quietly switched off — and only then ask what the law would have called it.*

**Load-bearing terms** — a listener holding the ordinary meaning gets a wrong answer on something this episode teaches.
- **AI system** — wrong reading: "anything clever a computer does", or "machine learning". Right: a system that passes the Art. 3(1) test, whose centre is inference. Gets the scope of the entire Act wrong.
- **infers** — wrong: "guesses", "is uncertain". Right: derives from input *how to generate* outputs. This is the element that separates a model from a rule somebody wrote down, and it is what decides whether CAS is in.
- **may exhibit adaptiveness** — wrong: "it has to keep learning". Right: optional. A system frozen at shipping is still an AI system. The wrong reading puts most deployed systems outside the Act.
- **varying levels of autonomy** — wrong: "it acts without humans". Right: *varying levels*. A system where a person approves every output still qualifies.
- **placing on the market** (3(9)) — wrong: "selling it". Right: the first making available on the Union market, **including free of charge**. The wrong reading exempts every free and internal tool.
- **putting into service** (3(11)) — wrong: "switching it on". Right: supply for first use directly to the deployer, **or for own use**, for its intended purpose. This is the route an in-house system takes into the Act, and it is how CAS would have entered.
- **output is used in the Union** (2(1)(c)) — wrong: "the company has to be in Europe".
- **guidelines** (Art. 96) — wrong: "the rules". Right: non-binding; the interpretation that binds will come from courts.

**The cast:** the Nederlandse Politie — **deployer**, and, because it built the thing, also **provider**; the Ministry of Justice and Security; the Algorithm Register as a *voluntary national* transparency instrument that exists outside the Act; the residents of the scored grid squares, who are "affected persons" under Art. 2(1)(g) and, under the rest of the Act, almost nobody.

**The system, operationally, before any rule applies.** Weekly cycle. A city map divided into small cells; each cell scored for the coming period; outputs expressed as **"hotspots"** and **"hot times"**. Three depersonalised sources: **BVI** (central police crime database), **GBA** (municipal population administration), **CBS** (Statistics Netherlands demographics). Crime types: burglary, car theft, bicycle theft, nuisance. **Closed system — an officer could not see which data drove any particular prediction.** *The field's own description to check this against is the Algorithm Register entry itself, which is both the source and a first-rate artefact for the episode.*

**The operative text.**
> **Art. 3(1) ✓** — "a machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy and that **may exhibit adaptiveness** after deployment, and that, for explicit or implicit objectives, **infers**, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments."
> **Art. 2(1) ✓**, limbs (a)–(g), and in particular **(c)** — third-country providers and deployers "where the **output** produced by the AI system is used in the Union."
> **Art. 3(9), 3(10), 3(11) ✓** — placing on the market, making available, putting into service.

Called thereafter: **the gate** (Art. 3(1)) and **the reach** (Art. 2).

**Why this rule and not another.** The alternative was a list — enumerate the techniques, which is exactly what the 2021 proposal's Annex I did (**COM(2021) 206 final, 21 April 2021**, confirmed in docket 08). A list ages. A functional definition built on **inference** names no technique and so cannot be evaded by renaming one. What was traded: legal certainty. Nobody can read "infers" off the page and know where it stops — which is why the Commission had to publish guidelines in February 2025, and why the most quoted line in them is a negative one.

**The variants — change one fact.**
1. CAS is replaced by a fixed rule a policy officer wrote: send a car to any square with three burglaries last month. No inference from data about how to generate the output — arguably outside the gate. Same cars, same streets, same people. **The Act does not apply.**
2. The output is not a hotspot map but a ranked list of named individuals. Still inside the gate; now also possibly inside Art. 5(1)(d). *That is Episode 5's.*
3. CAS is built and run for the Dutch police by a US vendor with no EU establishment. **Art. 2(1)(c)** pulls the vendor in, because the output is used in the Union.

**The levels.** *Technical* — a weekly model over three databases. *Organisational* — a closed box in a command room with no channel to ask why. *Institutional* — a police force running a decade-long programme it never defined success for. *Economic* — a procurement nobody had to justify against an outcome. *Rights-based* — geographic scoring is not a decision about a person until an officer acts on it, which is precisely why it slipped so many nets. **The claim that must land: the Act's gate is a technical test, and every other level is invisible to it.**

**The contested joints.** Does a deterministic system "infer"? *Contested; the guidelines lean towards excluding simple rule engines and nobody thinks the boundary is clear.* Does the February 2025 guidance bind? *Settled: it does not.* Is a definitional gate the right architecture at all, against regulating uses regardless of technique? *Contested, genuinely open.*

**What the rule assumes about the machine.** Art. 3(1) presumes a system with an identifiable **objective**, from which outputs can be read as inferences towards it. CAS had outputs and no measurable objective — the police said so when they stopped it. **The gate asks what a system is for; here, after ten years, the honest answer was: nothing anyone could measure.** A law that classifies by intended purpose has no test for a purpose that was never operationalised.

**As written against as enforced.** Nobody assessed CAS under this Act; the high-risk regime would not have applied, and for Annex III systems it now applies from **2 December 2027**. What actually produced public knowledge about CAS was a **voluntary national register**, not this Regulation.

**Deliberately compressed, and said aloud.** The Art. 2 exclusions are named and not explored: national security and defence, pure scientific research and development, purely personal non-professional use, and free and open-source licences with their exceptions. Each is a hole shaped like an argument, and the episode says it is leaving them.

**The second gate — teach it here, because three later episodes assume it.** The Act attaches no duty to *building* a system. It attaches duties to **placing it on the market** and to **putting it into service**. Give both in plain words: the duty bites when the thing is handed to someone else, or first used for its own purpose. **The claim that must land: a system built and used inside one organisation reaches this Act by a different door than one that is sold.** This is the concept the through-line rests on and the one Episode 12 turns on.

**Say that the verdict is held, and why — out loud, near the close.** The series will end by asking whether regulating artificial intelligence as a **product** was the right choice; the question is not answerable yet because the listener has not seen the machinery; **Episode 12** lands it. This promise must be heard here for Episode 12 to keep it.

**Handover.** We know what the Act counts. We do not know why anyone thought a law could be written in time for a technology moving this fast. **One episode away from the text — Episode 2 — and we are back inside it after that.**

**Transfer questions.** 1. Does this system infer, or apply rules a person wrote down? 2. What is its stated objective, and is there a measurement that would tell you whether it met it? 3. Whose output is used where — and does that put it inside a law nobody in the building has read? 4. At what moment did anybody's duty begin: when it was built, when it was handed over, or when it was switched on? 5. If this were switched off tomorrow, could anyone say what was lost?

**Sources.** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Arts. 1, 2, 3(1), 3(9)–(11), 96, consolidated text, EUR-Lex. Commission Guidelines on the definition of an AI system, February 2025. Dutch Algorithm Register, entry 81228922. Reporting on the discontinuation of CAS, February 2026.

**The single fact-check:** the **grid size** in the Algorithm Register entry — sources give both "125 square metres" and "125 by 125 metres". One retrieval of entry 81228922 settles it and supplies the register's own purpose statement for the operational description.

## Episode 2 — [LENS] The Pacing Problem

**Length:** 26 min. Short because the argument is small and sharp; padding it is the failure mode.

**Prerequisites:** Episode 1 — the gate, inference, and that duties attach when a thing is placed on a market.

**Opening anchor** — *type: a published disagreement.* **Frank H. Easterbrook, "Cyberspace and the Law of the Horse", *University of Chicago Legal Forum* 1996, art. 7, p. 207**, against **Lawrence Lessig, "The Law of the Horse: What Cyberlaw Might Teach", *Harvard Law Review* 113 (1999) 501.** Two named people, a real published disagreement, three years apart, about whether you should ever write a statute named after a technology. *Announce plainly: this episode has no statute in it.*

**The episode's central fact, and its strongest one:** the Commission's proposal, **COM(2021) 206 final, 21 April 2021**, defined an AI system by reference to techniques listed in its Annex I — and **ChatGPT was released on 30 November 2022**, nineteen months later. The general-purpose chapter that now runs to Articles 51 to 56 does not exist in the text Europe started with. That is checkable directly against the proposal.
*⚠ The docket's own heading calls these "two documents three weeks apart". It is wrong. Nineteen months.*

**The positions and their ablest defenders.**
1. **The Collingridge dilemma** — **David Collingridge, *The Social Control of Technology*** (Frances Pinter / St Martin's Press, 1980). Early, while a technology is steerable, you do not know what to steer away from; by the time you know, it is entrenched. Regulation is always premature or too late. *Response to:* the post-hoc regulation of chemicals, nuclear power and asbestos. *Consequence of adopting it:* you build in the power to change the instrument after you have learned something — **Arts. 7, 97, 112**. **⚠ The canonical sentence is stable across secondary citations but its page is not established. The writer paraphrases and attributes; no page number is spoken.**
2. **The pacing problem** — **Larry Downes, *The Laws of Disruption*** (Basic Books, 2009): technology changes exponentially, institutions incrementally. And **Gary Marchant, Braden Allenby and Joseph Herkert (eds), *The Growing Gap Between Emerging Technologies and Legal-Ethical Oversight: The Pacing Problem*** (Springer, 2011). *Consequence:* prefer principles and regulators with discretion over rules with fixed thresholds. **The tension with (1) is the episode's best beat:** Collingridge says regulate early and expect to be wrong; the pacing problem says never write what a technology can outrun — and the Act does both at once, a fixed compute threshold in Art. 51(2) with a delegated power to move it in Art. 51(3).
3. **Regulation by architecture** — **Lawrence Lessig, *Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace*** (Basic Books, 1999; *Code: Version 2.0*, 2006), and **Joel Reidenberg, "Lex Informatica", *Texas Law Review* 76(3) (1998) 553–593**, whose term precedes Lessig's book by a year. What constrains behaviour is the built environment, not the rule about it. *Response to:* the 1990s belief that the internet was unregulable. *Consequence:* logging duties (Art. 12), machine-readable marking (Art. 50), technical documentation (Art. 11 and Annex IV) — the Act is full of Lessig where it never says so.
4. **The refusal** — **Easterbrook**. There is no more a coherent law of cyberspace than a law of the horse. *Consequence of taking him:* no gate, no tiers, no CE mark, and no duty on anything before it hurts somebody. **Lessig's 1999 reply is the ablest answer and it is by name.**

**Contested joint — and say the awkward part out loud.** Whether the pacing problem is a serious analytic claim or a rhetorical move. **Both sides have names.** The critique: **William Aspray and Philip Doty, "Does technology really outpace policy, and does it matter?", *JASIST* 74(8) (2023) 885–904**, which reconsiders the outpacing argument rather than assuming it. The other side in good faith: **Adam Thierer** (Mercatus Center), on the pacing problem, Collingridge and permissionless innovation. *Contested.* The same sentence does two jobs — it is a serious claim and it is the deregulatory position's favourite argument — and the episode is more honest for naming both holders. Whether staged application solves Collingridge or merely postpones it: *still moving; the December 2027 deferral is the live test.*

**Applied to, by number:** **Episode 4** (the design-time bet is a Collingridge bet); **Episode 8** (the standards machinery is regulation by architecture, delegated); **Episode 9** (a compute threshold is a pacing-problem artefact).

**Handover.** If you are going to regulate anyway, you must first say **who is responsible**. That question has an answer in the Act, and an airline once argued for a third one. **Back inside the text.**

**Transfer questions.** 1. Is this rule drawn where the technology is, or where it was when drafting started? 2. What in this instrument can change without reopening the statute, and who holds that power? 3. Is the constraint here a rule about the system, or something built into it? 4. Would ordinary law already have reached this harm? 5. Who benefits from the claim that this moves too fast to regulate?

**Sources** (all in `research/08_lens_sources.md`): Collingridge (1980). Downes (2009). Marchant, Allenby and Herkert (2011). Lessig, *Code: Version 2.0* (2006). Reidenberg, 76 *Tex. L. Rev.* 553 (1998). Easterbrook, 1996 *U. Chi. Legal F.* 207. Lessig, 113 *Harv. L. Rev.* 501 (1999). Aspray and Doty, *JASIST* 74(8) (2023) 885. COM(2021) 206 final.

**The single fact-check:** the **ChatGPT release date**, taken from OpenAI's own announcement rather than from the docket, because the episode states it as a date.

## Episode 3 — Under Whose Name

**Length:** 30 min.

**Prerequisites:** Episodes 1–2 — the gate; that duties attach at placing on the market and putting into service; that the Act is an instrument with staged application.

**Opening instance** — *type: a decided case.* ***Moffatt v. Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149**, file SC-2023-005609, Civil Resolution Tribunal of British Columbia, Tribunal Member **Christopher C. Rivers**, issued **14 February 2024**. In November 2022, after their grandmother died, **Jake Moffatt** used the chatbot on Air Canada's website while booking. It said bereavement fares could be applied for retroactively. They could not.

Open on paragraph 27, read exactly:
> "…Air Canada suggests the chatbot is a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions. **This is a remarkable submission.** While a chatbot has an interactive component, it is still just a part of Air Canada's website. It should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website. It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot."

And paragraph 28: *"I find Air Canada did not take reasonable care to ensure its chatbot was accurate."* The order at paragraph 44: **$812.02 in total — $650.88 damages, $36.14 interest under the Court Order Interest Act, $125 tribunal fees**, payable within fourteen days.

**Disclosure the writer must make, one sentence, straight after the quotation:** Canada, a small-claims tribunal, contract and negligent misrepresentation. The Act did not apply and does not apply retrospectively. The counterfactual is announced where it starts — and then the harder point: under **Art. 2(1)(a)** the same chatbot offered to EU users today would be squarely in scope. *The counterfactual is about the facts, not about the law.*

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **provider** (3(3)) — wrong: "the company that wrote the code". Right: whoever places it on the market or puts it into service **under its own name or trademark**. A rebadger is a provider; a developer who never ships may not be.
- **deployer** (3(4)) — wrong: "the user", "the person typing". Right: the body using it **under its authority**, professional use only. An individual using a tool personally is not a deployer and owes nothing.
- **operator** (3(8)) — wrong: "the person operating the machine". Right: the umbrella term covering provider, manufacturer, deployer, representative, importer and distributor. The enforcement articles say "operator", so the wrong reading mis-reads every penalty provision.
- **under its own name or trademark** — the hinge of the whole split, and the phrase that decides Air Canada's position.
- **substantial modification** (3(23)) — wrong: "any change", "an update". Right: a change not foreseen in the initial conformity assessment that affects compliance or alters the intended purpose. The wrong reading has every patch re-triggering conformity.
- **authorised representative** (3(5)) — wrong: "a lawyer" or "a sales agent". Right: a mandated holder of documentation who can be addressed by authorities instead of the provider.
- **importer** (3(6)) against **distributor** (3(7)) — wrong: interchangeable. Right: an importer places a third-country-branded system on the market; a distributor makes it available without being provider or importer. Different duties.
- **affected person** (2(1)(g)) — wrong: "a party with rights in this Act". Right: within scope, and with **no private right of action**. This is the episode's sharpest wrong answer.

**The cast, and what the Act calls each.** Air Canada — **deployer**, and possibly **provider** if it put the system into service under its own name. Whoever built the chatbot — provider, or nothing at all if Air Canada rebadged it. Moffatt — an affected person with a complaint right and no money. The chatbot — **no role at all. The Act contains no such category, and that absence is the episode.**

**The system, operationally.** ⚠ **The decision does not describe the architecture and the writer must not assume one.** November 2022 predates ChatGPT's release by weeks; a customer-service bot of that vintage was most likely **intent classification plus retrieval over a help-content corpus** — an utterance matched or embedded, mapped to one of a fixed set of intents, and a templated or retrieved answer returned. A generative model fails differently: it composes a plausible sentence rather than mis-selecting a stored one. **The episode says which it does not know, and reasons about both** — because under Art. 3(1) both infer, and both are through the gate. *This characterisation is the writer's, not a cited source's, and is marked as such in the script.*

**The operative text.**
> **Art. 3(3) ✓ provider** — develops, or has developed, and places on the market or puts into service "**under its own name or trademark**, whether for payment or free of charge."
> **Art. 3(4) ✓ deployer** — "**using an AI system under its authority** except where the AI system is used in the course of a personal non-professional activity."
> **Art. 25 ✓** — responsibilities along the AI value chain: a distributor, importer, deployer or third party **becomes** a provider by putting its name on the system, by substantially modifying it, or by changing its intended purpose so that it becomes high-risk. And **Art. 25(2) ✓**, inserted in 2026: the initial provider then **stops being the provider** of that system, and must hand over documentation and technical access.

Called thereafter: **the name rule**, and **the flip** (Art. 25).

**Why this rule and not another.** The Act could have attached duties to the *system* — a registry of certified models, obligations following the artefact. It attached them to **roles**, because those roles already existed in forty years of EU product law: manufacturer, importer, distributor, and the chain between them. What was traded: the mapping is awkward for software that is configured, fine-tuned, rebadged and resold. One organisation can hold several roles at once and can change role by changing the system. **The claim that must land: most real disputes under this Act will be arguments about who the provider is.**

**The variants.** 1. Air Canada licenses the bot and leaves it as the vendor shipped it — Air Canada is deployer, the vendor provider, and documentation duties sit with the vendor. 2. Air Canada puts its own name on it — **provider**, and the whole provider stack lands on the airline. 3. Air Canada fine-tunes it on its own fare rules, or repurposes it — **Art. 25 flips it**, and the original provider drops out with a duty to hand over what the new one needs. 4. The bot is Canadian and used only in Canada, but EU passengers use the website from Munich and act on the output — **Art. 2(1)(c)** becomes live and the answer is genuinely arguable.

**The levels.** *Technical* — a retrieval system returning a stale document. *Organisational* — nobody owned the help content. *Institutional* — a tribunal that would not accept a novel legal person. *Economic* — $650.88 against the cost of maintaining a corpus. *Rights-based* — a consumer with no information about what he was talking to.

**The contested joints.** Whether "under its own name or trademark" is workable for foundation models re-served through a dozen intermediaries — *contested; the value-chain rules are the least-tested part of the Act.* Whether a deployer who writes a system prompt has substantially modified anything — *still moving; no authority.* Whether any legal system should recognise an artificial agent as a responsible party — *settled in practice, contested in the literature; the tribunal's word was "remarkable", which is a judge's word for absurd.*

**What the rule assumes about the machine.** The provider/deployer split assumes a **stable artefact with a determinate author**: somebody made it, somebody else uses it, and you can tell which is which. A system updated server-side, retrained weekly and reconfigured by the customer with a paragraph of English strains every joint in that assumption. The Act's answer is to make modification a role-changing act. Whether that is administrable is unknown.

**As written against as enforced.** This is a **market-surveillance regime, not a private right of action**. Under it Moffatt could have complained to a market-surveillance authority and would still not have had his C$650.88. **His remedy came from ordinary contract and tort law.** *Spoken deferral, once: what you can actually recover is a later series — "Who Pays".*

**Deliberately compressed, and said aloud.** Arts. 22, 23 and 24 — authorised representatives, importers, distributors — are named and summarised in one pass rather than walked through.

**Handover.** Roles tell you *who* owes something. They do not tell you *how much*. For that the Act sorts systems into tiers, and this chatbot lands on a rung we have not built yet.

**Transfer questions.** 1. Whose name is on this system, and did they write it, buy it, or rebadge it? 2. If I changed the configuration, would I become its provider? 3. When this goes wrong, which of these parties can the affected person actually sue — and is that the same list the regulator can fine? 4. Is anyone in this chain claiming the system is responsible for itself? 5. Who holds the documentation, and are they the same people who hold the risk?

**Sources.** *Moffatt v. Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149, paras. 27, 28, 44. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Arts. 2(1), 3(3), 3(4), 3(11), 22, 25.

**The single fact-check:** none required. The decision has been read manually and the quoted passages and the order are verbatim in the docket. **The writer's one check is unspent and should stay unspent.**

## Episode 4 — The Ladder

**Length:** 36 min — it builds four rungs, two routes in, and the classification article that decides which you are on.

**Prerequisites:** Episodes 1 and 3 — the gate; provider and deployer; that duties attach at placing on the market.

**Opening instance** — *type: an incident.* **Volkswagen and the defeat device, EU, 2015 onwards.** Software that recognised the conditions of the **type-approval test** and ran full emissions control only during it — roughly **8.5 million affected cars in Europe**. *Not counterfactual: this happened inside EU product-safety law.* Open on the car recognising its examiner.

**The point of opening here, and it is the route nobody talks about:** a car is a product already covered by Union harmonisation legislation and already required to undergo third-party assessment. If that emissions strategy were an AI system today, it would be **high-risk under Art. 6(1) without anyone consulting Annex III**. Two doors into the same regime, and most listeners have only heard of one.

**Planned two-episode use, named here so it is not a repeat.** Episode 4 takes the facts and **the route in**. Episode 8 takes **the gaming, the CJEU rulings and the enforcement aftermath**, as a callback on facts the listener already holds. Episode 4 must not spend the line about building the product to pass the test.

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **risk** (3(2)) — wrong: "danger". Right: the combination of probability of harm and severity. This is the definition that makes risk something you can *rate*, which is what lets a ladder exist at all.
- **high-risk** — wrong: "dangerous". Right: a legal classification triggered by an Annex III listing or the Annex I product route, with nothing to say about how harmful this particular deployment is. The single most consequential wrong reading in the Act.
- **intended purpose** (3(12)) — wrong: "what it is used for". Right: the use **the provider intends**, as specified in the instructions for use, in promotional and sales material, and in the technical documentation. A listener holding the wrong reading gets every classification question wrong.
- **reasonably foreseeable misuse** (3(13)) — wrong: "anything a user might do". Right: use not in accordance with the intended purpose but resulting from reasonably foreseeable human behaviour.
- **safety component** (3(14)) — wrong: "a part that is safe", or "any important part". Right: a component whose failure or malfunctioning endangers health and safety — and after the 2026 amendment, components used **solely** for convenience, efficiency, optimisation or quality control **expressly do not qualify** (Art. 6(1a)), unless failure would endanger health and safety (Art. 6(1b)).
- **profiling** (3(52)) — wrong: "building a customer profile". Right: the meaning imported from the GDPR — and it is the trigger that makes an Annex III system **always** high-risk, defeating the escape hatch.
- **derogation** (Art. 6(3)) — wrong: "an exemption you apply for". Right: a self-assessed escape, documented before placing on the market, with a registration duty under Art. 49(2).
- **Annex III** — wrong: "a technical appendix", or "a list of banned things". Right: the operative list of high-risk use cases, amendable by delegated act.
- **"the four tiers"** — wrong: the Act has four named tiers. Right: **it does not.** The Act has prohibitions, high-risk, specific transparency obligations, and silence. "Limited risk" and "minimal risk" are the Commission's presentation, not the Regulation's words. A listener who goes looking for them in the text will not find them.

**The cast.** Volkswagen as the manufacturer and, in the Act's vocabulary, the **provider**; the national type-approval authorities; the European Parliament's inquiry committee; and — for the second instance — the **Belastingdienst**, a public authority that was provider and deployer at once.

**The system, operationally.** A defeat device is a piece of control software that detects the operating conditions characteristic of the test cycle and changes the emissions-control strategy accordingly. The CJEU cases concerned a **"thermal window"** that ran full exhaust-gas recirculation only between **15°C and 33°C and below 1,000 metres**, letting NOx rise past the limits outside it. *The field's own term is "defeat device" as defined in Art. 5(2) of Regulation 715/2007; the episode uses that definition and does not paraphrase it.*

**The second instance, mid-episode — the Annex III route worked.** The **toeslagenaffaire**. From **2013** the **Belastingdienst** ran a **risk-classification model** in its childcare-benefits division: supervised learning on applications previously judged correct or incorrect, producing a risk score, used as a **first filter** so officials scrutinised the highest-scoring claims. Inputs included **dual nationality**, **low income**, and **whether the applicant's name read as non-Dutch**. Between **2005 and 2019** roughly **26,000 families** (some estimates 35,000) were wrongly accused of fraud and required to repay in full — commonly **€20,000 to €60,000** each. **More than 1,100 children were placed in foster care.** The **third Rutte cabinet resigned in January 2021**; on **25 May 2022** the government admitted **institutional racism** in part of the Tax Administration was a root cause. Amnesty International's report is ***Xenophobic machines*** (EUR 35/4686/2021).

That system is **Annex III point 5(a) ✓** — systems used by public authorities to evaluate eligibility for essential public assistance benefits, and to grant, reduce, revoke or reclaim them. It is the cleanest match in the whole annex.

> ⚠ **TRAP, and it is disqualifying.** The benefits scandal involved the Tax Administration's **risk-classification model**. **SyRI** was a **separate** welfare-fraud system, struck down by **the District Court of The Hague in February 2020**. Secondary sources blur them constantly. The episode may mention SyRI, and must mention it as a different system. **Verify which system is being described before a word is written.**

**The operative text.**
> **Art. 6(1) ✓** — high-risk where the system is a safety component of, or is itself, a product covered by the Annex I legislation **and** that product must undergo third-party conformity assessment.
> **Art. 6(2) ✓** — and, in addition, the systems in **Annex III**.
> **Art. 6(3) ✓** — the derogation: an Annex III system is **not** high-risk where it does not pose a significant risk of harm, "including by **not materially influencing the outcome of decision making**", under four listed conditions — **notwithstanding which, a system that performs profiling of natural persons is always high-risk.**
> **Art. 6(4) ✓** — the provider who so concludes must document the assessment and register it anyway.

Called thereafter: **the ladder**, and **the design-time bet**.

**The rungs, built in order, each with its date attached:** **prohibited** (Art. 5, live since 2 February 2025) → **high-risk** (Annex III from **2 December 2027**, Annex I from **2 August 2028**) → **transparency obligations** under Art. 50, live since **2 August 2026** → **nothing at all.**

**Why this rule and not another.** The rival shapes were a **rights-based** instrument keyed to severity of interference with a protected interest, whoever caused it — the EDPB–EDPS position, which is **the next-but-one episode** — and a **sectoral** approach, with rules for medicine, policing and credit written by the regulators who already know those fields. The tier model won because it is the shape EU product law already had and because it gives industry an ex-ante answer: read Annex III, know your obligations. What was traded: **the tier is fixed by intended purpose at design time, while harm is contextual and turns up in deployment.**

**The variants.** 1. The same benefits model used by a **bank** to triage loan applications — Annex III point 5(b), creditworthiness, also high-risk. Change one fact: it triages *internal audit* workload and touches no individual outcome, and Art. 6(3) may take it off the ladder. 2. The model outputs no score but a **ranked queue** with no numbers. The officials' behaviour says it materially influences the outcome; the design document says it is only triage. *(Quote Art. 6(3) as it stands — the test is negative. Do not put an inverted paraphrase inside quotation marks; the listener cannot hear the difference.)* 3. Nationality is removed as a variable and postcode retained. **The classification under Art. 6 does not move a millimetre. The tier is blind to the change that matters most.**

**The levels.** *Technical* — a classifier whose labels came from prior investigations, so it learned which applications had previously been *found* wrong. *Organisational* — a score read as evidence by people untrained to read it, with no explanation and no appeal path. *Institutional* — a tax authority with a fraud mandate and no correction mechanism. *Economic* — recovery targets. *Rights-based* — discrimination on nationality, and a decade before anyone said "institutional racism" aloud. **The ladder reaches the first level, gestures at the second through Art. 26, and does not reach the rest. Say so plainly and hold the judgement.**

**The contested joints.** Is a tiered risk model the right shape at all? — **contested, and the docket takes no position, because informed opinion genuinely has not converged. Do not manufacture a consensus.** Does Art. 6(3) open an escape hatch providers will self-certify through? — *contested; the profiling carve-out is both its limit and its test.* Is Annex III a list that can be kept current by delegated act, or one that will always be a scandal behind? — *still moving.*

**What the rule assumes about the machine.** High-risk classification presumes a system with a **stable intended purpose declared by its provider** — that you can read risk off what a thing was built for. The benefits model was built to sort a queue. It was *used* as proof of fraud. **The tier tracked the design document; the harm tracked the organisation.** Nothing in Article 6 can see that gap.

**As written against as enforced.** None of this applied to the Belastingdienst, and the parts that would apply to its successors do not apply until **2 December 2027**. What ended the affair was a parliamentary inquiry, journalism, and a cabinet resignation.

**Deliberately compressed, and said aloud.** **Annex I** is named, its two sections described, and its underlying product regimes left as a list; **Arts. 102–110**, which thread AI into existing Union product legislation, are named as plumbing and skipped. The episode states that a whole parallel route into the high-risk regime has been summarised in ninety seconds, and that the machinery behind it is taken apart later.

**Handover.** We have a ladder. Three rungs impose duties; one forbids. Next: the top rung, and why a legal order whose instinct is to regulate rather than ban decided that some things may not be built at all.

**Transfer questions.** 1. Which rung is this on — and was that decided by what it is for, or by what it does? 2. Who declared the intended purpose, and would the people using it recognise that description? 3. If the deployment drifted from the design document, does anything in the rule notice? 4. Does this system decide, or produce a queue that somebody else treats as a decision? 5. Is the risk in the model, or in what the organisation is permitted to do with its output?

**Sources.** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Arts. 6, 7, 3(2), 3(12)–(14), 3(52), Annexes I and III. Amnesty International, *Xenophobic machines*, EUR 35/4686/2021. The Dutch government's statement of 25 May 2022. Regulation 715/2007, Art. 5(2). *(Distinguished on air: District Court of The Hague, SyRI, February 2020.)*

**The single fact-check:** the **contents of Annex I, Section A** — enough to name three of the product regimes aloud. The docket lists the annex but not what is in it.

## Episode 5 — The Red Lines

**Length:** 40 min. Long by design and the reason is the term list: ten load-bearing terms, and an article with ten prohibition limbs and seven paragraphs of machinery. Naming ten terms and landing none would waste the episode.

**Prerequisites:** Episodes 1, 3, 4 — the gate; provider and deployer; the ladder and which rung is which.

**Opening instance** — *type: a regulator's decision.* **Case NAIH-85-3/2022** (antecedent NAIH-7350/2021), Hungary. From **May 2018**, **Budapest Bank** ran AI speech-signal processing over **customer-service call recordings**, analysing keywords **and the emotional state of the speaker**. Results were stored with the call, replayable for **45 days**, and used to monitor call quality, pre-empt complaints, **rate the quality of the call-handling staff's work**, and rank calls so the software could recommend which customers to call back first. **NAIH** opened its investigation in September 2021 and fined the bank **HUF 250 million — about €665,000**, then the largest data-protection fine in Hungary. It found automated decision-making and profiling with **no valid legal basis**, no proper balancing of interests and inadequate safeguards; held that **only freely given informed consent** could ground emotion-based voice analysis; and **ordered the bank to stop analysing emotions.**

**Disclosure, one sentence, before the facts:** decided under the **GDPR**, in 2022, three years before Article 5 applied. *Spoken deferral, once: the data-protection law running underneath almost every case in this series is a later series — "The Data Subject".*

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **prohibited practice** — wrong: "an illegal system". Right: what is prohibited is the placing on the market, putting into service **for that purpose**, or use. The same capability can be lawful in another configuration, which is what every variant in this episode turns on.
- **social scoring** (5(1)(c)) — wrong: "any scoring of people". Right: evaluation or classification over a period of time based on **social behaviour** or personal characteristics, leading to detrimental treatment in an **unrelated context** or treatment **disproportionate** to the behaviour. A listener with the wrong reading concludes credit scoring is banned. It is not — it is Annex III point 5(b), high-risk.
- **solely** (5(1)(d)) — the word holding the whole predictive-policing prohibition up. Move it and every human-in-the-loop tool in Europe changes category.
- **untargeted** (5(1)(e)) — the word that separates Clearview from building a file on one suspect.
- **emotion recognition system** (3(39)) — wrong: "reading faces". Right: identifying or inferring emotions **or intentions** from **biometric data** — which is why voice qualifies, and why a system that infers mood from typing speed may not.
- **biometric identification** (3(35)) against **biometric verification** (3(36)) against **biometric categorisation** (3(40)) — wrong: all of it is "facial recognition". Right: three different operations with three different legal fates — identification one-to-many, verification one-to-one and expressly carved out of Annex III 1(a), categorisation an assignment to a class and the subject of its own prohibition in 5(1)(g). **The episode must teach 5(1)(g); the previous plan never did.**
- **publicly accessible space** (3(44)) — wrong: "public property". Right: any place accessible to an undetermined number of people, **privately owned included**, and regardless of entry conditions. A shopping centre is one.
- **"real-time"** (3(42)) against **post-remote** (3(43)) — wrong: "live versus recorded". Right: whether capture, comparison and identification occur without significant delay, with short delays included to stop circumvention. Only the real-time kind is in Art. 5; the post kind is high-risk with its own authorisation regime.
- **strictly necessary** (5(1)(h)) — wrong: "important". Right: the phrase that, with the three enumerated objectives and Annex II, does all the limiting work.
- **"in the areas of workplace and education institutions"** (5(1)(f)) — the seam this case sits on.

**The cast.** Budapest Bank as deployer; the vendor of the speech-analytics software; the call-centre staff being rated; the **customers**, whose emotions were the input; NAIH.

**The system, operationally.** Speech emotion recognition over recorded audio: acoustic features — fundamental frequency, energy, speaking rate, spectral shape — extracted per utterance and mapped by a trained classifier onto a small label set, usually with a per-call aggregate. Keyword spotting runs alongside.

**Does the system exist — the beat this episode must not skip.** The scientific standing of inferring internal emotional states from vocal or facial signals is **contested at the construct level**: the review literature argues expressive configurations do not map reliably onto emotion categories across people and contexts. The episode asks whether the banned thing is a thing, and reports honestly that the evidence says the measurement is weak — **which makes the ban's rationale power, not accuracy.** *[OUTSIDE DOCKET: Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez and Pollak, "Emotional Expressions Reconsidered", Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2019 — confirm authors, year and pagination before it is named aloud.]*

**The operative text.**
> **Art. 5(1)(f) ✓** — inferring emotions of a natural person "**in the areas of workplace and education institutions**, except where the use of the AI system is intended to be put in place or into the market for **medical or safety reasons**."
> And the list, each limb in its own words: **(a)** subliminal or purposefully manipulative techniques "with the objective, or the effect of materially distorting the behaviour" ✓ · **(b)** exploiting vulnerabilities "due to their age, disability or a specific social or economic situation" ✓ · **(c)** social scoring ✓ · **(d)** criminal-risk assessment "**based solely on the profiling**" of a person, with the human-assessment exception ✓ · **(e)** facial-recognition databases built "through the **untargeted** scraping of facial images from the internet or CCTV footage" ✓ · **(g)** biometric categorisation to deduce race, political opinions, trade union membership, religious or philosophical beliefs, sex life or sexual orientation, with the labelling-and-filtering carve-out ✓ · **(h)** real-time remote biometric identification for law enforcement, "unless and in so far as such use is **strictly necessary**" for three enumerated objectives ✓.
> And the **2026 insertions, in force 2 December 2026**: **(ba)** non-consensual intimate imagery of an identifiable person ✓, **(bb)** material within Art. 2(c) and (e) of Directive 2011/93/EU ✓ — with **Art. 5(1a) ✓**, which is the provision that actually decides how far they reach: for placing on the market or putting into service, they bite where that generation is the **intended purpose**, **or** where the system's design, training, architecture, capabilities or user-facing functionality make it a "**reasonably foreseeable and reproducible outcome, without requiring significant technical modification**", and the system lacks reasonable and adequate safeguards. For *use*, only where the deployer uses it for that purpose.

Called thereafter: **the red lines**.

**Why this rule and not another.** A legal order whose instinct is to regulate rather than forbid drew a small number of absolute lines. The Commission's stated rationale for (f) is a **consent** rationale, not an accuracy one: workplaces and schools are asymmetric environments with a fundamental power imbalance, and a worker or a student cannot meaningfully decline. The road not taken: the **EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 5/2021** attacked the "positive list" method itself and wanted a **general ban on biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces**. It got Art. 5(1)(h) with three exceptions, a judicial-authorisation regime and an annual reporting duty instead. **That argument is the next episode.**

**The variants — one fact changed each time, from the case as it stands.** *(The case as it stands: customers' emotions were inferred, and the output was used to evaluate employees.)*
1. **Drop the employee-evaluation purpose.** Same inference, output goes only to marketing; no worker is rated by it. The subjects are customers; the place is still a workplace for the staff standing in it. **Does (f) still bite?** If no, the prohibition is about *whose* emotions; if yes, it is about *where*.
2. **Move it to the interview room** — emotion inferred from job candidates. Squarely workplace, squarely a person whose treatment turns on the output: the easy end of the rule, which makes the Budapest seam audible as a hard case rather than a typical one.
3. **The same system sold for detecting driver drowsiness.** "Medical or safety reasons" — out of the prohibition and straight into Annex III. The capability did not change; the stated purpose did.
4. **A general-purpose image tool that was never built for it but will produce (ba) material without significant technical modification, and ships without safeguards.** Under Art. 5(1a)(a)(ii) that is enough. **This is the live edge of the whole Act.**

**A different exercise, announced before it starts so nobody thinks a rule was amended — find the load-bearing word.** Delete *solely* from (d), and every human-in-the-loop predictive tool in Europe changes category. Delete *untargeted* from (e), and building a database of one suspect's images becomes prohibited too.

**The levels.** *Technical* — a classifier over acoustic features with contested validity. *Organisational* — a performance-management tool aimed at staff through customers. *Institutional* — a data-protection regulator reaching a use case before any AI law existed. *Economic* — call-centre productivity. *Rights-based* — the power asymmetry the Commission names.

**The contested joints.** Does (f) follow the setting or the person? — **contested; genuinely unresolved, and this case is the cleanest illustration in Europe.** Do the February 2025 prohibited-practices guidelines settle anything? — *settled that they do not bind.* Does "reasonably foreseeable and reproducible outcome" make general-purpose image and video providers answerable for what users do? — **still moving, and it is the sharpest live question in the Act.** Is (a)'s "materially distorting behaviour" ever provable? — *contested; no enforcement to point at.*

**What the rule assumes about the machine.** Art. 5(1)(f) presumes emotion inference **works well enough to be dangerous**. The research suggests the inference is unreliable. Both can be true — an unreliable system with authority over a worker's evaluation is *more* harmful, not less. But note precisely what has happened: the Act has **banned a capability claim**. If the capability does not exist, the prohibition is regulating **the use of a vendor's claim as grounds for treating a person a certain way**. That is the series' signature move and this is its clearest instance.

**As written against as enforced — said in full here, and nowhere else in the series.** Prohibitions applied from **2 February 2025**; penalties for breaching them only from **2 August 2025** — six months of a ban with no sanction. National market-surveillance powers went live **2 August 2026**. The two new limbs bite **2 December 2026**. **No confirmed penalty under Article 5 has been established as of 14 September 2026.** The only emotion-recognition enforcement anyone can point to is the Hungarian one, under a different statute, in 2022.

**Deliberately compressed, and said aloud.** Art. 5(2)–(7) — the authorisation, notification, impact-assessment and annual-reporting machinery around real-time biometric identification — is described in structure and not walked through paragraph by paragraph. Annex II is named.

**Handover.** The Act forbids a short list and regulates the rest. There was a serious argument that it should have been built the other way round — from the rights at stake rather than from the risk of the product. **One episode away from the text.**

**Transfer questions.** 1. Which single word in this prohibition is carrying the weight, and what happens if I move it? 2. Is this ban premised on the system working, or on somebody acting as if it does? 3. Is the prohibited thing defined by the setting, the person, or the purpose? 4. When did this become enforceable, as opposed to applicable? 5. Would a slight change of stated purpose move this out of the ban and into a tier?

**Sources.** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 5 in full as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744; Annex II. Commission Guidelines on prohibited AI practices, 4 February 2025. NAIH decision NAIH-85-3/2022 (GDPRhub). The emotion-expression review literature *(pending the check below)*.

**The single fact-check:** the **Barrett et al. (2019) citation** — authors, title, journal, year — before it is named aloud. If it cannot be confirmed, the episode makes the claim as a characterisation of the review literature and names nobody.

## Episode 6 — [LENS] Risk Against Rights

**Length:** 28 min.

**Prerequisites:** Episodes 1, 3, 4, 5. This episode is unintelligible without the design-time bet from Episode 4.

**Opening anchor** — *type: a published objection.* The **EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 5/2021**, **18 June 2021** — two European data-protection institutions reading a proposal two months old and saying, in effect: you have built the wrong kind of instrument. *Announce that this episode has no statute in it.*

**Boundary, stated in the episode:** this is an argument *about* rights-based regulation. *Spoken deferral, once: what a fundamental right is, how the Charter works, and how a court tests a limit on one is a later series — "The Charter".*

**The positions and their ablest defenders.**
1. **Risk-based product safety — the Act as built.** Held by the Commission and the co-legislators. The defence on the merits: it is the only European machinery that operates **ex ante and at scale** — it reaches every unit placed on the market, imposes duties before anyone is harmed, has an existing enforcement apparatus, and gives firms a determinate answer. *Consequence:* obligations are about **process**, and compliance is demonstrated by documentation against a standard.
2. **Rights-based regulation — the road not taken.** **EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 5/2021** is the ablest short statement: it criticised the **"positive list" approach to prohibitions** — you can only ban what you thought of; argued that "risk to fundamental rights" should be aligned with the GDPR's meaning; wanted **societal risks to groups** assessed and not only risks to individuals; and called for a general ban on biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces. *Consequence:* the trigger is severity of interference with a protected interest, the assessment is contextual, and the remedy runs to the affected person.
3. **The structural critique.** **Michael Veale and Frederik Zuiderveen Borgesius, "Demystifying the Draft EU Artificial Intelligence Act", *Computer Law Review International* 22(4) (2021) 97–112** (preprint arXiv:2107.03721). The Act takes its structure from **four decades of EU product-safety regimes** rather than from fundamental-rights law, and inherits that machinery's assumptions. Two consequences they name: reliance on **"1980s product safety regulation"**, and delegation of what are effectively fundamental-rights questions to **"standardisation bodies with no fundamental rights experience."** *Consequence of taking them seriously:* you would not have written Article 40 the way it is written. **Status: contested but foundational — and dated on specifics, because it addresses the 2021 draft, not the adopted Act or the 2026 amendments. Say that.**
4. **The sectoral alternative.** Let the medicines regulator regulate medical AI, the financial supervisor regulate credit models, the police inspectorate regulate policing tools. *Consequence:* deep competence, no horizontal coherence, twenty-seven times the fragmentation. **⚠ No holder in the docket. This is the narrator's own framing and must be labelled as such in the episode, or cut. It is not attributed to anyone.**

**Which way the weight leans.** **On the narrow question of standardisation the critics have been empirically vindicated** — the next apparatus episode shows how. **On the broader question of instrument shape the weight does not lean, and the episode says so rather than inventing a consensus.** The strongest honest statement available in 2026: the product route bought administrability and paid for it in contextual sensitivity and in individual remedy, and the bill has not fully arrived.

**Applied to, by number:** **Episode 7** (process obligations against outcome duties, and what a fundamental-rights impact assessment is doing in a product statute at all); **Episode 8** (standards bodies deciding rights questions); **Episode 11** (a regime with a complaint right and no compensation).

**Contested joints.** Whether Art. 27's fundamental-rights impact assessment is a genuine graft of rights-based method onto product law or a form nobody will read — *still moving, and it does not apply until 2 December 2027.* Whether the Article 5 prohibitions are themselves rights-based regulation smuggled into a product statute — *contested, and a good case can be made that they are.*

**Handover.** The argument is unresolvable in the abstract, so we do the concrete thing: open the heavy chapter and see, requirement by requirement, what a provider actually has to do — and what those duties assume about the human being who is supposed to supervise a machine. **Back inside the text.**

**Transfer questions.** 1. Does this rule trigger on what the system *is*, or on what it does to someone? 2. Whose harm counts — an individual's, a group's, or the market's? 3. If the developer complies fully, does the affected person get anything? 4. Was the alternative instrument actually proposed by someone, or is it only a debating position? 5. What would each side have to show to be proved right?

**Sources.** EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 5/2021, 18 June 2021. Veale and Zuiderveen Borgesius, *Computer Law Review International* 22(4) (2021) 97–112. COM(2021) 206 final.

**The single fact-check:** none required; all three sources are in the docket with full bibliographic detail. **Unspent.**

## Episode 7 — The Weight

**Length:** 42 min — flagged. Twenty-six articles, eleven load-bearing terms, and the densest obligations in the Act. The alternative is to gut them.

**Prerequisites:** Episodes 1, 3, 4, 6 — the gate; provider and deployer; the ladder and Article 6; the risk-against-rights argument.

**Opening instance** — *type: an audit.* **DOT Office of Inspector General, report AV2021020, 23 February 2021: "Weaknesses in FAA's Certification and Delegation Processes Hindered Its Oversight of the 737 MAX 8."** Open on one finding, read flat, and not on the crashes: **the FAA did not have a complete understanding of Boeing's safety assessments of MCAS until after the first accident.** The report also found no risk-based approach to oversight of Boeing's Organization Designation Authorization, that ODA personnel independence from company influence was not ensured, and that FAA engineers stationed at Boeing were balancing certification work against oversight duties. An internal FAA survey found **56% of aircraft-certification staff felt there was too much external influence on the agency, affecting safety.** Fourteen recommendations followed; the FAA restored limited certification delegation to Boeing in September 2025.

**Disclosure, one sentence, inside the finding:** United States, aviation certification, no AI Act and no AI system in this Act's sense. **This episode uses the report for one thing — what a human-oversight requirement assumes — and says so where the analogy starts.** The delegation half of the report belongs to the next episode.

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **risk management system** (Art. 9) — wrong: "a document". Right: a continuous iterative process planned and run across the entire lifecycle, with regular review.
- **residual risk** (Art. 9(5)) — wrong: "risk left over, which must be eliminated". Right: it must be **judged acceptable**. The Act permits residual risk, and a listener who does not know that misreads the whole chapter as a safety guarantee.
- **"relevant, sufficiently representative, and to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete"** (Art. 10(3)) — wrong: "the data must be perfect". Right: the qualifier is doing the work, and representativeness is relative to the **intended purpose**.
- **human oversight** (Art. 14) — wrong: "a human approves each decision". Right: a designed-in capability to understand capacities and limitations, resist automation bias, interpret output, **disregard, override or reverse** it, and halt the system. The two-person rule is **only** for Annex III point 1(a), and it is disapplied for law enforcement, migration, border control and asylum where Union or national law considers it disproportionate.
- **automation bias** (Art. 14(4)(b)) — named in the statute itself: the tendency to rely or over-rely on an automated output.
- **instructions for use** (3(15), Art. 13) — wrong: "a manual". Right: the legal instrument by which the provider transfers knowledge, and with it responsibility, to the deployer.
- **logs** (Art. 12) — wrong: "server logs". Right: automatic recording of events over the system's lifetime, for traceability, with a deployer retention duty of at least six months under Art. 26(6).
- **serious incident** (3(49)) — wrong: "an accident". Right: death or serious harm to health, serious and irreversible disruption of critical infrastructure, **infringement of Union-law obligations intended to protect fundamental rights**, or serious harm to property or the environment. The third limb is the one nobody expects.
- **fundamental rights impact assessment** (Art. 27) — wrong: "a human-rights audit". Right: a procedural duty on **certain deployers**, largely public bodies — which is why the same system deployed privately may carry no such duty at all.
- **AI literacy** (3(56), Art. 4) — wrong: "training courses". Right: a defined competence obligation covering providers, deployers and affected persons.
- **AI regulatory sandbox** (3(55), Art. 57) — wrong: "a test environment". Right: a controlled framework established by a **competent authority** with legal consequences: an exit report that market surveillance authorities and notified bodies must take positively into account, and **no administrative fines** for infringements where the provider followed the plan and the authority's guidance in good faith.

**The cast, in the Act's vocabulary and flagged as analogy:** Boeing as provider; the airlines as deployers; the flight crew as the **human oversight**; the FAA as a market-surveillance authority that had outsourced its assessment.

**The system, operationally.** **MCAS** — the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System — a flight-control law that commanded nose-down trim of the horizontal stabiliser under certain conditions, triggered from angle-of-attack data, and capable of repeated activation. It existed so that the aircraft's handling would resemble its predecessor's and pilots would need minimal retraining. The mitigation assumed in the safety assessment was **existing crew procedure**: recognise runaway stabiliser trim, and cut it out. **⚠ The docket supplies the OIG findings only. The writer describes the mechanism from those findings and does not reach past them; no second retrieval.**

**The second instance, mid-episode — and it is a callback, not a new case.** The **Belastingdienst** risk-classification model from Episode 4, now run against Articles 10 and 12 rather than against Article 6. The listener already holds the facts, so this costs no exposition. Two properties do the work: **the labels came from prior investigations**, so the model learned which applications had previously been *found* wrong rather than which were wrong — an Art. 10(2)(f) and (g) bias-examination question with a feedback loop the Act's own Art. 15(4) names; and **officials could not see what drove any individual score** — an Art. 12 and Art. 13 question, and the reason an Art. 86 explanation would have had nothing to draw on.

**The operative text** — read in pieces across the episode, not as a run.
> **Art. 14(1) ✓** — high-risk systems "shall be designed and developed in such a way, including with appropriate human-machine interface tools, that they **can be effectively overseen by natural persons** during the period in which they are in use."
> **Art. 14(4) ✓** — the overseer must be enabled to understand capacities and limitations, remain aware of automation bias, correctly interpret the output, **decide not to use it or to disregard, override or reverse** it, and **intervene or interrupt through a 'stop' button or similar procedure that allows the system to come to a halt in a safe state.**
> **Art. 26(2) ✓** — deployers "shall assign human oversight to natural persons who have the necessary **competence, training and authority, as well as the necessary support**."
> **Art. 9 ✓** risk management · **Art. 10 ✓** data governance · **Art. 11 ✓** and Annex IV technical documentation · **Art. 12 ✓** record-keeping · **Art. 13 ✓** transparency to the deployer · **Art. 15 ✓** accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity · **Art. 21 ✓** cooperation with authorities · **Art. 16 ▲** the provider's consolidated duties · **Art. 17 ▲** quality management · **Arts. 18–20 °** documentation retention, logs, corrective action · **Art. 27 ▲** the fundamental-rights impact assessment · **Art. 4 ▲** AI literacy · **Art. 4a ▲** special-category data processed for bias detection.
> And the relief valves: **Art. 57 ✓** sandboxes, **Arts. 58–63 °** their arrangements, personal-data processing, real-world testing inside and outside them, informed consent, and measures for SMEs and microenterprises.

Called thereafter: **the requirements** (Arts. 8–15), **the file** (Annex IV), and **the overseer** (Art. 14).

**Why this rule and not another.** The alternative to process duties is an **outcome duty**. **Nothing in the Act is drafted that way**, so any example is an illustration and the script must say so before speaking it: a rule that said a system must not discriminate, or must be accurate to within some figure. Outcome duties cannot be certified before anything has happened, against a system whose behaviour depends on deployment context. So the Act requires a **process a documentation file can evidence**. What was traded: **a provider can satisfy every article in this chapter and still ship a system that harms people, as long as the file is in order.** That is not a cynical reading; it is what ex-ante process regulation *is*, and the episode states it in the Act's own logic rather than as an accusation.

**The variants.** 1. The overseer has authority to override but not the information to know when — Article 14's substance against its form, which is precisely the Boeing finding: the procedure existed, the knowledge that it applied did not. 2. The deployer assigns oversight to someone with the training but without the standing to stop production. **Art. 26(2) requires competence, training *and* authority, as well as support.** Remove authority and the article is decorative. 3. The benefits model's labels are redrawn from confirmed adjudications rather than from opened investigations. Nothing in Article 6 moves; **Article 10 is the only place in the Act where that change is visible at all.** 4. The same high-risk system deployed by a private firm rather than a public body — **Art. 27's duty may not attach, and one of the Act's two rights-facing instruments disappears.**

**The levels.** *Technical* — a control law with a single-sensor trigger; a classifier with contaminated labels. *Organisational* — a training decision made for commercial reasons; a score treated as evidence. *Institutional* — a regulator that did not hold the safety assessment. *Economic* — the value of "no simulator training required"; recovery targets. *Rights-based* — not reachable by any article in this chapter except Art. 27, which is itself procedural. That is the finding.

**The contested joints.** Does human oversight work? — **the weight of evidence leans against it in the strong form.** The human-factors literature on automation bias and on vigilance in monitoring tasks is consistent and old, and Article 14 acknowledges automation bias in its own text while still making a human the mitigation. *[OUTSIDE DOCKET: Parasuraman and Riley on use, misuse and disuse of automation (1997); Skitka and colleagues on automation bias — confirm before either is named.]* Is Art. 10(3) achievable for any real dataset? — *contested; widely argued to be a drafting overreach, softened by "to the best extent possible".* Is the FRIA a genuine obligation or a template exercise? — *still moving; nothing applies before 2 December 2027.*

**What the rule assumes about the machine — the heart of this episode.** Article 14 assumes a system whose outputs a trained person can **understand in the time available**, and whose failure modes that person has been told about. It assumes the human is a **control**. Boeing's safety assessment made the same assumption in aviation's own vocabulary: the crew is the mitigation. What the requirement cannot reach is the case where the system's behaviour is unexpected **to the organisation that built it**, and where the overseer's information comes from the provider's own instructions for use. **The Act requires the provider to tell the deployer what the limitations are. It has no mechanism for limitations the provider does not know about — and that is the category that produces the accidents.**

**As written against as enforced.** None of Chapter III applies before **2 December 2027** for Annex III systems and **2 August 2028** for Annex I. Whether these requirements will be assessed against harmonised standards, and by whom, is the next episode — and the answer, as of June 2026, is that no such standard exists.

**Deliberately compressed, and said aloud.** Annex IV is characterised, not enumerated. Arts. 18–20 are grouped as one duty to keep and hand over. Chapter VI gets four minutes and is named as a whole chapter compressed, with the honest note that sandboxes are the part of the Act industry likes most and the part with the least evidence behind it. **Post-market monitoring and serious-incident reporting are deliberately not taught here: they are the enforcement loop, and Episode 11 has them.**

**Handover.** Every requirement in this chapter is demonstrated by conformity assessment. Conformity assessment runs against harmonised standards. So the next question is the one that decides whether any of this is real: **who writes those standards, and do they exist?**

**Transfer questions.** 1. What exactly is the human here supposed to be able to *do*, and in how long? 2. Does the overseer have the information, the training **and** the authority, or only one of the three? 3. Is this obligation about process or about outcome — and could it be fully met by a system that harms people? 4. Who wrote the instructions for use, and what do they not say? 5. Where did the training labels come from, and what were they actually recording?

**Sources.** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Arts. 4, 4a, 8–21, 26, 27, 57–63, Annexes IV and IX. DOT OIG, AV2021020, 23 February 2021. Amnesty International, *Xenophobic machines*, EUR 35/4686/2021. Human-factors literature on automation bias *(pending the check below)*.

**The single fact-check:** the **automation-bias citation** — Parasuraman and Riley (1997), and whether Skitka is the right name for the automation-bias work — before either is spoken. If it fails, the claim is made as a characterisation of the human-factors literature with nobody named. **The MCAS description is not a second check: it comes from the OIG findings the docket holds, and goes no further.**

## Episode 8 — The Presumption

**Length:** 40 min — flagged. Notified bodies, standards, conformity assessment, CE marking, registration and the deferral decision are one causal chain; splitting it destroys the argument.

**Prerequisites:** Episodes 4, 6, 7 — the ladder; the risk-against-rights argument including Veale and Zuiderveen Borgesius on standardisation; the Chapter III requirements this machinery is supposed to certify.

**Opening instance** — *type: an institutional decision.* **October 2025: CEN and CENELEC adopted exceptional measures to accelerate AI standards work** — reducing publication stages and using smaller drafting groups — because the harmonised standards the high-risk regime depends on were not going to exist for the **2 August 2026** deadline. **Technical committee members publicly warned that this could undermine consensus and produce "serious unintended consequences."** Open in the committee, on the vote to change the rules.
⚠ *The docket does not name the members who said this. The warning is attributed to the reported record — "committee members warned, as reported" — and the quoted phrase is spoken as a report, not as a named person's words.*

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **harmonised standard** (3(27), Art. 40) — wrong: "an EU rule", or "an ISO standard". Right: a standard **whose reference has been published in the Official Journal**. Until that citation exists, the document confers nothing. This is the term the entire episode and the series' verdict turn on.
- **presumption of conformity** (Art. 40(1)) — wrong: "approval", "certification". Right: a legal presumption, and only **to the extent that the standard covers the requirement**. A presumption is never wider than the document underneath it.
- **common specification** (3(28), Art. 41) — wrong: "more detailed rules". Right: the Commission's fallback, adopted by implementing act under the **examination procedure of Art. 98(2)**, available precisely when standards are missing or inadequate — and it moves the substance out of a private body into Union law.
- **conformity assessment** (3(20)) — wrong: "an inspection". Right: the process of demonstrating that the requirements have been fulfilled — and for most high-risk systems, the party demonstrating is the party being assessed.
- **internal control** (Annex VI) — wrong: "internal audit". Right: the conformity route that expressly **does not provide for the involvement of a notified body**.
- **notifying authority** (3(19)) against **notified body** (3(22)) — near-homophones that a listener will merge. Right: the authority designates and monitors; the body performs the assessment. Merge them and the episode's central point — who checks the checker — becomes unhearable. **Audio-critical: the writer must find a way to keep them apart by sound, not by spelling.**
- **CE marking** (3(24), Art. 48) — wrong: "tested by the EU", "a safety certificate". Right: a marking the **provider affixes** to indicate its own conformity.
- **EU declaration of conformity** (Art. 47) — wrong: "a certificate issued to you". Right: a document you write about yourself and keep for ten years.
- **registration** (Art. 49) and the **EU database** (Art. 71) — wrong: "a public register of AI systems". Right: high-risk systems only, and the Annex III point 1, 6 and 7 entries — biometrics, law enforcement, migration — go into a **secure non-public section** open only to the Commission and the authorities in Art. 74(8).

**The cast.** **CEN** and **CENELEC**, private standards associations; **JTC 21**, their joint technical committee for AI; the **Commission**, which issues the standardisation request and decides whether to cite a standard in the Official Journal; **notified bodies**; the **providers** who will self-assess; and **Marta Cantero Gamito**, whose REALaw post of **28 November 2025**, *"From Consensus to Exceptionality — What the EU's AI Standards Crisis Reveals About Delegated Technical Governance"*, is the sharpest short account.

**The system, operationally — here, the machinery is the system.**
1. The Commission issues a **standardisation request** to CEN-CENELEC under Regulation (EU) No 1025/2012 (**Art. 40(2) ✓**), specifying that standards must be clear and consistent and asking the organisations to evidence their best efforts.
2. A technical committee drafts; national standards bodies vote; enquiry and formal vote follow; the text is published as an EN.
3. **The Commission assesses it and cites it in the Official Journal.** Only then is it *harmonised*.
4. Conformity with a cited standard gives a **presumption of conformity** (**Art. 40(1) ✓**). If standards are absent, late, non-compliant with the request, or **"insufficiently address fundamental rights concerns"** (**Art. 41(1)(a)(iii) ✓** — the Act's own anticipation of the objection), the Commission may adopt **common specifications**.
5. **Art. 43 ▲** decides who checks: for **Annex III points 2–8**, internal control under Annex VI, "which does not provide for the involvement of a notified body." For **Annex III point 1**, the provider chooses internal control or a notified-body route under Annex VII — and where such systems are for law enforcement, immigration, asylum or EU institutions, "the market surveillance authority **referred to in Article 74(8) or (9), as applicable**, shall act as a notified body." *Do not elide the middle of that sentence: Art. 74(8) makes that authority the **data protection authority**, so the body that checks the system is the body that guards the right.*
6. **Art. 44 ✓** certificates, valid up to five years for Annex I systems and four for Annex III; **Art. 47 ✓** declaration; **Art. 48 ✓** CE marking, digital where the system is supplied digitally; **Art. 49 ▲** registration; **Arts. 28–39** the designation and supervision of notified bodies.

**The operative text.** Art. 40 ✓ (presumption), Art. 41 ✓ (common specifications), Art. 43 ▲ (allocation), Art. 48 ✓ (CE marking). Called thereafter: **the presumption**, and **self-assessment**.

**Why this rule and not another.** Delegating technical detail to standards bodies is the mechanism that built the single market: the legislature sets essential requirements, private experts write the specifications, conformity to a specification buys a presumption. It scales, it updates faster than a statute, and it puts drafting where the expertise is. What was traded: **the substance.** Veale and Zuiderveen Borgesius predicted in 2021 that this would hand fundamental-rights questions to "standardisation bodies with no fundamental rights experience." Note what the Act does in **Art. 67**: the advisory forum's **permanent members are the Fundamental Rights Agency, ENISA, CEN, CENELEC and ETSI** — the standards bodies sit permanently beside the rights agency. **The claim that must land: the Act saw the objection and answered it with a seating plan.**

**The historical instance, mid-episode — the machinery under load, and a callback.** **Dieselgate**, whose facts the listener already has from Episode 4. Now the rest: the CJEU held such devices unlawful under Art. 5(2)(a) of Regulation 715/2007 and rejected the engine-protection defence — **C-128/20, C-134/20 and C-145/20, judgments of 14 July 2022**, on referrals from the Austrian Supreme Court and the regional courts of **Eisenstadt** and **Klagenfurt**; and earlier **C-693/18**. The European Parliament ran an inquiry (**EMIS**, report A8-0049/2017). Afterwards **Member States applied neither financial nor legal penalties to manufacturers**: no mandatory recalls or retrofits, no withdrawal of type approvals, and VW maintained it had never breached EU rules. The framework was later revised to require Member States to run a minimum number of independent tests each year.
**The transfer, and this is the claim that must land: the product was built to pass the assessment rather than to meet the standard. An AI system tuned to perform on the benchmark used for its conformity assessment, and differently in deployment, is the identical failure with different physics.**

**The delegation half of Boeing, one beat, no re-exposition.** The FAA's **Organization Designation Authorization** lets a manufacturer perform certification functions on the regulator's behalf, and the OIG found the FAA could not properly assess risk in that arrangement. **For most stand-alone high-risk AI systems, Art. 43's default is the provider's own internal control.** The question the episode can then put honestly is the one the OIG report answers: what does a regulator need in order to check work it has delegated, and does any AI market-surveillance authority currently have it?

**Where it stands, date-stamped — and this is the verdict question in one fact.** **As of June 2026 not one JTC 21 deliverable grants presumption of conformity, because none has been cited in the Official Journal.** **EN 18286**, an AI quality-management-system standard, is the most advanced, at **Approval** stage, and the likeliest to be cited first. The original **April 2025** deadline was missed; the **October 2025** acceleration was the response; in **November 2025** the Commission tabled the Digital Omnibus; and the high-risk dates moved to **2 December 2027** and **2 August 2028** by **Regulation (EU) 2026/1744**. **Status: still moving. Say so, and date it.**

**The variants.** 1. A high-risk system in Annex III point 5 — **self-assessed against a standard that does not exist.** Art. 43's answer in the absence of standards is that the provider demonstrates conformity with the requirements directly. Nobody knows what that looks like. 2. The same system moved into Annex III point 1 and deployed for immigration — **the market surveillance authority becomes the notified body**, and by Art. 74(8) that authority is the data protection authority. 3. EN 18286 is cited in early 2027. Every provider implementing it gets a presumption covering its **quality-management** obligations — **and none of its accuracy, robustness or fundamental-rights obligations.** A presumption is only as wide as the standard beneath it.

**The levels.** *Technical* — a specification document. *Organisational* — smaller drafting groups replacing consensus. *Institutional* — private associations holding the substance of a public regulation, with the Commission holding a veto it exercises by citation. *Economic* — standards participation costs money, which selects who is in the room. *Rights-based* — the 2021 objection, now empirically live.

**The contested joints.** Can conformity assessment against a technical standard discharge a fundamental-rights duty? — **contested, and no longer only theoretically.** Was the acceleration a pragmatic rescue or a legitimacy failure? — *contested; Cantero Gamito's framing is that exceptionality became the method.* Should the Commission use Art. 41 if standards slip again? — *still moving; it is the most consequential unexercised power in the Act.* Is self-assessment for most of Annex III defensible? — *contested, and the ODA record is the strongest available evidence against.*

**What the rule assumes about the machine.** The presumption assumes that **compliance can be established by testing against a written specification**, and that the specification is a good proxy for the requirement. That holds for a lift. It holds badly for a system whose performance depends on the distribution of data it meets after deployment — which is why *"Lost in Vagueness: Towards Context-Sensitive Standards for Robustness Assessment under the EU AI Act"* (arXiv 2511.15620) exists. **Dieselgate proved a fixed test can be gamed; the AI case is worse, because the deployment distribution moves on its own.**

**As written against as enforced.** The regime this machinery serves does not apply until December 2027, and the machinery has so far **produced nothing that confers the presumption it exists to confer.**

**Deliberately compressed, and said aloud.** Arts. 29–39 — application, notification procedure, requirements for notified bodies, their presumption of conformity, subsidiaries and subcontracting, operational obligations, identification numbers, changes to notifications, challenges to competence, coordination, and third-country bodies — are taught as **one institution rather than eleven articles**. Arts. 42, 45 and 46 are named. Annexes V, VI, VII, VIII and XIV are characterised, not read.

**Handover.** Everything so far assumed a system built for a purpose. The next chapter concerns models built for **no particular purpose at all** — and this machinery has no idea what to do with them.

**Transfer questions.** 1. Who checks this, and are they the people who built it? 2. What document does the checker check against, and has anyone published it? 3. Could this system be built to pass the test rather than to meet the requirement? 4. What exactly does the certificate cover, and what does it leave uncovered? 5. If the specification is late, does the obligation move, or does the deadline?

**Sources.** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Arts. 40, 41, 43, 44, 47, 48, 49, 67, 71, Annexes VI and VII. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744. CEN-CENELEC announcements on exceptional measures, October 2025. Cantero Gamito, REALaw, 28 November 2025. CJEU C-128/20, C-134/20 and C-145/20, 14 July 2022; C-693/18. European Parliament EMIS report A8-0049/2017. DOT OIG AV2021020.

## Episode 9 — The General Case

**Length:** 38 min — Chapter V, Chapter IX Section 5, Article 50, Article 95 and two codes of practice.

**Prerequisites:** Episodes 1, 3, 4, 8 — the gate, and that a *model* is not a *system*; provider and deployer; the ladder, including the transparency rung this episode now fills in; the standards machinery, because general-purpose AI is governed by a **code of practice** instead, and the contrast is the point.

**Opening instance** — *type: a decided case.* **18 March 2026: the Court of Rome, Judge Damiana Colla, annuls the Garante's €15 million penalty against OpenAI — on jurisdiction, not on the merits.** Once OpenAI established its Irish subsidiary, the GDPR's one-stop-shop made the Irish authority lead supervisory authority, reportedly from **15 February 2024**, months before the Garante's final decision. **The court never reached the substantive allegations at all.** The decision did not find OpenAI innocent; it found that Italy had no right to judge OpenAI. Open on the judge taking the case away from the regulator who brought it.

**Why the episode opens here and not on the block — a design decision the docket asked for.** `03_cases_and_instances.md` records general-purpose AI as the biggest hole in the docket, and `04` warns that the Garante case teaches four **GDPR** questions and none of this episode's apparatus. So the case is used as **pre-history, in ninety seconds**: what a regulator reached for when there was no AI-specific regime, and how it ended. The March 2023 block and the €15m fine are told inside that, not at the top. *(The previous plan opened Episode 1 on a system being switched off and this episode on a country switching a service off — the same move twice. Fixed here.)* **The episode's own decidable object is the threshold.**

**Disclosure, one sentence, at the pivot:** a GDPR action; there was no AI-specific regime to use. ⚠ *The Court of Rome reasoning is multi-sourced but the judgment has not been read. Say that it is reported, not read.*

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **general-purpose AI model** (3(63)) against **general-purpose AI system** (3(66)) — wrong: the same thing. Right: the model is what the obligations of Chapter V attach to; the system is what is built from it. Different duties, different owners, and the wrong reading makes the whole chapter unparseable.
- **high-impact capabilities** (3(64)) — wrong: "very capable". Right: capabilities that **match or exceed the capabilities recorded in the most advanced models**. A relative, moving definition — which means the class redefines itself as the field moves.
- **systemic risk** (3(65)) — wrong: "risk to the financial system", or "risk the AI takes over". Right: risk **specific to high-impact capabilities**, with significant impact on the Union market due to reach or to reasonably foreseeable negative effects, propagated at scale across the value chain.
- **floating-point operation** (3(67)) — wrong: "computing power", "speed". Right: a **count of operations** over the whole training run, which is why it can be estimated from parameters and tokens without anyone seeing the model.
- **presumption** (Art. 51(2)) — wrong: "10^25 FLOP is the definition of a systemic-risk model". Right: it is a **rebuttable presumption** of high-impact capability, which a provider may argue against under Art. 52(2), and which the Commission may **move by delegated act** under Art. 51(3). **An episode that puts the number in the definition has the architecture wrong, and the docket flags this as its single most-repeated error.**
- **downstream provider** (3(68)) — wrong: "a reseller". Right: a provider of a system that integrates a model, whoever supplied it.
- **code of practice** (Art. 56) against **code of conduct** (Art. 95) — wrong: the same instrument. Right: different chapters, different subjects, different bindingness. Codes of practice are how a GPAI provider demonstrates compliance until a harmonised standard exists; codes of conduct are voluntary application of high-risk-style requirements to systems that are **not** high-risk. **Audio-critical: two near-identical phrases meaning different things.**
- **deep fake** (3(60)) — wrong: "a fake video". Right: a defined term — and Art. 50's duty is **labelling**, not prohibition.
- **transparency** (Art. 50) — wrong: "explaining how it works". Right: telling people they are dealing with AI, labelling deepfakes, and machine-readable marking of synthetic content. It says nothing about whether the output is true.

**The cast.** The provider of a general-purpose AI model; **downstream providers** who build on it; the **AI Office**, which is a function of the Commission and not an agency (3(47) ✓); the **scientific panel**, which may raise a qualified alert; **notified bodies — absent, because this regime has none.**

**The system, operationally, before any rule applies.** A transformer language model pretrained by next-token prediction over a large corpus, then post-trained by supervised fine-tuning and preference optimisation. The quantity the Act reaches for is **training compute**, in floating-point operations — estimable from parameter count and token count without access to the model. **Why that number:** empirical scaling relationships between compute, data, parameters and loss made compute the best available public proxy for capability around 2022–2023. **What the proxy misses, as of 2026:** post-training and distillation can produce strong models below the line, and **inference-time compute** — models that spend more computation reasoning at the point of use — buys capability a training-compute threshold cannot see at all. **This is the live technical beat, and it is stated as contested, not settled.**

**The operative text.**
> **Art. 51(1)–(2) ✓** — a model is systemic-risk where it has high-impact capabilities on appropriate technical tools and benchmarks, or by Commission decision on the Annex XIII criteria; and it "**shall be presumed** to have high impact capabilities pursuant to paragraph 1, point (a), when the cumulative amount of computation used for its training measured in floating point operations is **greater than 10^25**." *(Quote it whole or not at all — an ellipsis inside quotation marks is inaudible, so the listener hears the shortened version as the instrument's own sentence.)* **Art. 51(3) ✓** — the Commission may amend that threshold by delegated act.
> **Art. 52 ✓** — notification within two weeks, the provider's right to argue it is not systemic-risk, and reassessment no earlier than six months.
> **Art. 53(1) ✓** — technical documentation (Annex XI), information for downstream providers (Annex XII), a **copyright policy** including respecting a reservation of rights, and "a **sufficiently detailed summary about the content used for training**". **Art. 53(2) ✓** — the free-and-open-source relief, **which does not apply to systemic-risk models**.
> **Art. 55 ✓** — model evaluation including documented adversarial testing, systemic-risk assessment and mitigation, serious-incident reporting to the AI Office, cybersecurity of the model and its physical infrastructure.
> **Art. 56 ✓** — codes of practice, ready "at the latest by 2 May 2025", with the Commission empowered to impose common rules by implementing act if none is adequate.
> **Art. 88 ✓** — "The Commission shall have **exclusive powers** to supervise and enforce Chapter V", entrusted to the AI Office.
> **Art. 50 ▲** — interactive systems must disclose they are AI; deepfakes must be labelled; generated or manipulated content must carry machine-readable marks.

Called thereafter: **the model regime** (against the system regime), and **the threshold**.

**Date-stamped facts, each spoken with its date.** GPAI obligations apply from **2 August 2025**. The **GPAI Code of Practice** was published **10 July 2025** and declared adequate **1 August 2025**; three chapters — Transparency, Copyright, Safety and Security — the last binding only systemic-risk providers; **more than twenty providers had signed by August 2026**. AI Office enforcement powers went live **2 August 2026**. On **31 July 2026** the Commission published a first list of **more than 180 organisations** signing a **second, distinct** code — on transparency of AI-generated content. The threshold is reported as capturing roughly **5–15 companies worldwide**.

**Why this rule and not another.** The alternatives: **do nothing about models** and regulate only systems built from them — the shape of the 2021 proposal, which ChatGPT destroyed; **put GPAI into the high-risk regime**, which would have required conformity assessment against standards for a thing with no intended purpose; or **a capability threshold** — regulate models that can do specified dangerous things — which is what everyone wanted and nobody could operationalise. The Act chose **a compute proxy plus a code of practice**, because a threshold is administrable and a code could be written in months rather than years. What was traded: **the threshold does not measure what anyone cares about**, and the code is voluntary in form even where the obligations behind it are not.

**The variants.** 1. A model trained just under the threshold, then heavily post-trained into frontier capability — **below the line by the presumption**, and the only routes back in are the Annex XIII criteria and a Commission designation. 2. A model released under a free and open-source licence — parts of the regime soften; change one fact so it crosses the threshold and **the relief largely disappears**. 3. The Air Canada chatbot from Episode 3, rebuilt on a general-purpose model today. **Art. 50 now requires it to say it is not a person — and says nothing about whether what it says is true.** 4. A provider argues under Art. 52(2) that its over-threshold model is not systemic-risk. The Commission rejects the argument in a reasoned decision. **There is no notified body, no standard and no certificate anywhere in this process: the regulator decides directly.**

**The levels.** *Technical* — FLOP as a measurable proxy for an unmeasurable property. *Organisational* — a code of practice signed by the parties it regulates. *Institutional* — supervision centralised in the Commission rather than spread across twenty-seven authorities. *Economic* — a threshold naming 5–15 firms is also a description of an industry structure. *Rights-based* — nothing in this chapter runs to an individual.

**The contested joints.** Does 10^25 FLOP track anything real about risk? — **contested, and the argument grows stronger against it as inference-time compute grows.** Is a code of practice co-written with industry adequate for systemic risk? — *contested; its defenders point out that it exists, which the standards do not.* Does the copyright chapter settle anything about training data? — **No. The training-data summary duty is taught here; the copyright substance is not this series'.** Is centralised supervision right? — *the Garante story is the argument for it, made by events; still contested on competence and resourcing.*

**What the rule assumes about the machine.** Chapter V assumes **a model's risk can be estimated from how it was made rather than from what it does** — because at the point of regulation nobody, including its builder, knows what it does. That is an honest response to a real epistemic problem, and it is also a confession: the Act's entire system-side architecture runs on **intended purpose**, and these models do not have one. **The claim that must land: Chapter V is the Act admitting that its own organising concept does not apply to the most consequential technology it governs.**

**As written against as enforced.** AI Office powers live from **2 August 2026**; the AI Office **reportedly** sent first **requests for information** to frontier providers around **29 August 2026** *(secondary, unconfirmed)*. And Europe's most famous enforcement action against a general-purpose model was annulled — not because the regulator was wrong, but because it was not allowed to decide.

**Deliberately compressed, and said aloud.** Art. 54 (authorised representatives for third-country model providers) is named only. Arts. 89–94 — monitoring actions, scientific-panel alerts, requests for documentation, evaluations, requests for measures, and the provider's procedural rights — are taught as **one escalation, not six articles**. Annexes XI, XII and XIII are characterised. Art. 95's codes of conduct get one beat, as the contrast that makes Art. 56 legible. The Code of Practice's copyright chapter is described and its substance handed on.

**Handover.** A regime reaching five to fifteen companies, most of them outside the Union, raises a question the Act cannot answer from inside itself: does any of this travel? **One episode away from the text.**

**Transfer questions.** 1. What is the threshold measuring, and what is it a proxy *for*? 2. Could a system get the capability the rule worries about without crossing the number the rule uses? 3. Is this obligation on the model or on the system built from it — and who owes it? 4. If the instrument is a code of practice, what happens to a provider who declines to sign? 5. When a regulator loses a case on jurisdiction, what has actually been decided?

## Episode 11 — Who Is Actually Watching

**Length:** 40 min. **⚠ The overload, flagged in the coverage map. My recommendation is to split it — 11a governance, 11b enforcement — and run thirteen episodes. What follows is the single-episode version, with the compressions named.**

**Prerequisites:** Episodes 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9 — roles; the tiers and their dates; the prohibitions; the Chapter III duties; conformity assessment; the model regime's separate enforcement arm.

**Opening instance** — *type: a live controversy.* **Clearview AI.** A US company that scraped the open web for faces — **it claims more than 60 billion images** — and sold facial recognition to law enforcement. **Five European authorities have fined it roughly €105 million in total**: the Dutch **Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens** €30.5m (2024); the Italian **Garante** €20m (February 2022); the French **CNIL** €20m (October 2022, plus €5.2m in overdue-penalty payments); the Greek **HDPA** €20m (July 2022); the UK **ICO** £7.5m (2022). Open on the total assessed, then on the amount collected. **Clearview disputes EU jurisdiction and has not paid. Collection of every one of those fines remains contested.**

> **Method beat, stated in the episode:** "more than 60 billion images" is **Clearview's own claim**. It is evidence about Clearview, not about the database. The same discipline applies to every vendor number in this series.

**Disclosure, one sentence, at the counterfactual:** GDPR enforcement, not AI Act enforcement. Under this Act, untargeted scraping to build a facial-recognition database is **Art. 5(1)(e)** — prohibited since 2 February 2025, penalisable since 2 August 2025.

**Load-bearing terms.**
- **market surveillance authority** (3(26)) — wrong: "a markets regulator", "a competition authority". Right: the post-market product-safety enforcer — and for law-enforcement systems, by Art. 74(8), **the data protection authority**.
- **national competent authority** (3(48)) — wrong: "the AI regulator". Right: **either** a notifying authority **or** a market surveillance authority; and for Union institutions, the European Data Protection Supervisor.
- **AI Office** (3(47)) — wrong: "an EU agency". Right: "the Commission's function"; the definition says references to the AI Office **are references to the Commission**. Almost every claim about "the AI Office fining someone" turns on this.
- **exclusive competence** (Art. 75(1) as amended) — wrong: "the AI Office enforces the Act". Right: it is exclusively competent for a **defined class** of systems, and everything else belongs to Member States.
- **recall** (3(16)) against **withdrawal** (3(17)) — wrong: synonyms. Right: recall brings back, takes out of service or disables systems already supplied to deployers; withdrawal prevents a system in the supply chain being made available. The distinction is the whole of this episode's closing argument.
- **penalties** (Art. 99) — wrong: "fines are up to seven per cent". Right: **three ceilings**, chosen by which rule you broke, plus an inversion for small firms.
- **complaint** (Art. 85) — wrong: "a claim". Right: a submission that **shall be taken into account for the purpose of conducting market surveillance activities**. It starts an investigation. It does not start a case.
- **right to explanation** (Art. 86) — wrong: "the right to know how the algorithm works". Right: the right of an affected person to **clear and meaningful explanations of the role of the AI system in the decision-making procedure and the main elements of the decision taken** — from the **deployer**, for Annex III systems **except point 2**, and only to the extent the right is not already provided elsewhere in Union law.
- **applicable** against **enforceable** — the distinction the 2025 gap is built on.

**The cast — three enforcers, not one.** From Commission press release **IP/26/1714, 31 July 2026**, responsibility for enforcing the transparency rules and the prohibited practices is split three ways: **the AI Office**, for systems offered by the same provider as the underlying general-purpose model, and for systems integrated into very large online platforms or search engines; **national competent authorities**, for all other AI systems; and **the European Data Protection Supervisor**, for systems used by EU institutions, bodies and agencies (**Art. 100 ✓** carries its fining power — €1.5m for prohibited practices, €750,000 otherwise). Plus the **European AI Board** (Arts. 65–66), Member State representatives coordinating consistent application; **national competent authorities** (Art. 70); the **pool of experts** (Art. 69); and the notified bodies from Episode 8.

**The operative text.**
> **Art. 74 ▲** — market surveillance: investigate complaints, demand documentation, require corrective measures, fine, withdraw from the market. **Live from 2 August 2026.**
> **Art. 75(1) as amended ▲** — the AI Office is **exclusively competent** for two classes of AI **system**: those built on a general-purpose model **where model and system come from the same provider or the same undertaking** (excluding Annex I product systems, Annex III point 2, the law-enforcement, border and financial systems under Art. 74(6), and Annex III point 8 in the administration of justice); and those that constitute or are integrated into a **VLOP or VLOSE** designated under the DSA. That competence runs to providers, and to deployers only where the deployer is the provider or part of the same undertaking.
> **Art. 75a ✓** — the AI Office gets all the powers of a market surveillance authority: requests for information by simple request or by decision, remote and on-site inspections, entry to premises, examination and copying of books and data, oral explanations recorded, and **sealing premises and records**. **Art. 75b ✓** — commitments made binding. **Art. 75c ✓** — non-compliance decisions, fines under Art. 99(3)–(7) applied *mutatis mutandis*, and periodic penalty payments **not exceeding 5% of average daily worldwide turnover per day**; a five-year limitation period; unlimited CJEU jurisdiction to cancel, reduce or increase. **Art. 75d ✓** — rights of defence, access to the file on negotiated disclosure, and publication of decisions.
> **Arts. 72–73 ▲** — the provider's post-market monitoring plan, and reporting of serious incidents.
> **Art. 85 ✓** complaints · **Art. 86 ✓** explanation · **Art. 87 ✓** whistleblowers, via Directive (EU) 2019/1937.
> **Art. 99 ▲** penalties, **laid down and applied by Member States**. **Art. 111 ▲** systems already on the market. **Art. 113 ✓** the staged dates.

Called thereafter: **market surveillance**, and **the three-way split**.

**The spoken path through the penalty tiers — three numbers, in this order, and no table.** There is no picture; a listener cannot scan four rows.
1. **The shape first.** Three ceilings, and which applies depends on **which rule you broke — not on how much harm you did.** That is the sentence the beat exists for.
2. **The top, once:** a prohibited practice — **seven per cent of worldwide turnover, or thirty-five million euro, whichever is higher.** Say "whichever is higher"; it is the half people drop.
3. **The middle, once:** an ordinary obligation — a provider's duties, a deployer's, the transparency rules — **three per cent, or fifteen million.** Do not enumerate the articles; say "the obligations this series has spent nine episodes on".
4. **Then the twist, which is the teachable part.** Give an authority incorrect information and the ceiling is **one per cent, or seven and a half million** — the smallest number in the Act attaches to misleading the regulator. And under Art. 99(6) a small or start-up firm pays the **lower** of the two figures, not the higher: the same infringement is priced by who committed it. End the beat there.

**The timeline, taught as an instrument in its own right** — each date with the thing it switches on: **2 February 2025** definitions, AI literacy, prohibitions · **2 August 2025** penalties for prohibited practices, and the general-purpose obligations · **2 August 2026** Article 50 transparency, AI Office powers over models, and national market-surveillance powers · **2 December 2026** the two new Article 5 limbs · **2 December 2027** Annex III high-risk · **2 August 2028** Annex I high-risk. **The six-month gap in 2025 between a live prohibition and any penalty for breaching it is a teaching point, not a footnote.** *(Why the last two dates moved is Episode 8's; this episode teaches what the staging does.)*

**Why this rule and not another.** Enforcement could have been centralised in one European agency, as it effectively is for models. It was decentralised because **market surveillance is a Member State competence** in the product-safety tradition this Act borrows from, and because centrally policing every hiring tool in twenty-seven countries is not feasible. What was traded: **twenty-seven levels of capacity, expertise and appetite.** The Commission's own caveat, worth quoting exactly: *"Effective enforcement will also depend on Member States ensuring that national competent authorities are properly designated and adequately resourced."*

**The variants.** 1. Clearview today, offering the same service to an EU police force — **Art. 5(1)(e)**, penalties up to seven per cent, imposed by a **national** authority, and collectible against a US company with no EU establishment **by exactly the mechanisms that have so far collected nothing.** 2. The same company with an EU subsidiary — collection becomes possible, and, as the previous episode showed, **so does a jurisdictional argument about which authority may decide.** Establishing in Europe makes you reachable and makes you harder to reach. 3. A hiring platform breaching Annex III documentation duties — **not the AI Office's to fine. Article 99, a national authority, from 2 December 2027.**

**The method beat this episode is built to carry — the fines that were not.** Aggregator sites report that the AI Office issued the Act's first fines shortly after 2 August 2026: commonly **€47 million** across a hiring platform, a credit-scoring provider and a retail chain. **Three reasons it is not true, and the third is the transferable one.**
1. **No authoritative source.** A search restricted to `europa.eu` returns the enforcement framework pages and the 2 August announcement and **no press release announcing any fine.**
2. **The Commission's own release says otherwise.** **IP/26/1714, 31 July 2026** describes the machinery being switched on — the scientific panel, complaints tools, whistleblower channels — and **names no company and no penalty.**
3. **It does not fit the architecture — and the architecture changed in 2026, which sharpens the point rather than weakening it.** The AI Office is **not** confined to general-purpose models: under Art. 75(1) as amended it is exclusively competent for the two classes above, and Art. 75c(4) lets it impose penalties under Art. 99(3)–(7). **So the AI Office *can* fine the provider of a high-risk system — when that system falls inside Art. 75(1).** Now apply it: a pan-European HR platform, a credit-scoring provider and a retail chain running emotion recognition are none of them a same-undertaking general-purpose system, and none of them a very large online platform. They fall to **national competent authorities** under Article 99. **The reports credit the AI Office with fines that, on the amended text, are still not the AI Office's to impose.**
**The claim that must land: a listener can now reach that conclusion from a competence rule they can quote, instead of from a general impression of how the thing is shaped. That is the single most useful thing this series hands over, and it is why the architecture was taught before the enforcement.**
*⚠ Do not say how many versions of the story exist. The docket establishes that it is unsourced and architecturally implausible; it does not establish a count of variants.*

**The honest state of enforcement, dated 14 September 2026.** Powers live **2 August 2026**; the AI Office **reportedly** sent first requests for information to frontier model providers around **29 August 2026** *(secondary, unconfirmed)*; **no confirmed penalty, formal proceeding or fine under the AI Act has been established.**

**The levels.** *Technical* — a scraped embedding index. *Organisational* — five regulators acting separately. *Institutional* — a three-way split of competence and no European bailiff. *Economic* — €105m assessed, nothing collected, and the arithmetic of simply not paying. *Rights-based* — a complaint right that produces an investigation and not a remedy.

**The contested joints.** Are national market surveillance authorities resourced for this? — **the weight leans clearly to no, and the Commission has said so in its own words.** Does Art. 86 add anything? — *contested; this episode teaches Art. 86 and stops where the comparison to other Union law begins.* Can fines be collected from non-EU firms without EU establishment? — **still moving, and Clearview is the evidence: five authorities, €105 million, no money.**

**What the rule assumes — here, about the institution.** The market-surveillance model assumes **a product you can withdraw**. Withdrawal is the sanction that makes product safety bite: a defective lift comes out. A scraped face database is not withdrawable; a model served from a foreign data centre is not recallable; an embedding, once computed, does not come back. **The Act inherited an enforcement tool whose central move does not exist for its subject matter — and what it has instead is money it cannot always collect.**

**Deliberately compressed — and this list is what goes if the episode cannot flex.** Arts. 76–78 (supervision of real-world testing, the powers of fundamental-rights authorities, confidentiality) and Arts. 79–84 (the procedures for systems presenting a risk, for systems the provider classified as not high-risk, the Union safeguard procedure, compliant systems that nevertheless present a risk, formal non-compliance, and Union testing support structures) are described as **a single escalation ladder**, not walked through. Art. 69 and Annex X are named. The episode says aloud that a whole procedural chapter has been summarised, and that **Art. 77** — which lets national fundamental-rights bodies demand documentation — is the most under-noticed provision in the Act.

**Handover.** We have built the whole instrument, from the gate to the fine. One question has been held since the first episode, and it is the only one left: was a **product** the right thing to regulate? **Episode 12.**

**Transfer questions.** 1. If this rule were broken, who exactly would find out, and by what mechanism? 2. Which body may fine this actor — and does the claim I just read match that architecture? 3. Can the sanction actually be collected, and from whom? 4. Is the remedy here the removal of a product, and does removal mean anything for this system? 5. When did this obligation become applicable, and when did it become enforceable?

**Sources.** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Arts. 65, 66, 70, 72–75, 75a–75d, 85–87, 99, 100, 111, 113. Commission press release IP/26/1714, 31 July 2026. Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens decision on Clearview AI (2024); CNIL, Garante, HDPA and ICO decisions (2022). ⚠ **On air, the reported first fines are named as unverified and architecturally implausible, with the reasoning given — never repeated as fact.**

## Episode 12 — [VERDICT] A Product

**Length:** 32 min. Shorter than its predecessors deliberately: a verdict episode is not a recapitulation, and this one has one argument and one date to land.

**Prerequisites:** all eleven.

**Opening instance** — *type: a documentary state.* The list of harmonised standards cited in the Official Journal for this Regulation. **As of June 2026 there is nothing on it.** **EN 18286**, an AI quality-management-system standard, is the most advanced JTC 21 deliverable, at **Approval** stage, and the likeliest to be cited first. The regime it serves applies from **2 December 2027**. Open on the empty list and the date on the calendar.

**The question, stated as it was held.** Since Episode 1 this series has said it would not answer this until now. **Was regulating artificial intelligence as a product the right choice?**

**The competing answers, and who holds them.**
1. **Yes — and there was no alternative that scales.** Held by the Commission and the co-legislators, and defensible on its own terms: product-safety machinery is the only European apparatus that imposes duties **before** harm, reaches **every unit** placed on the market, has an **existing enforcement network**, and yields obligations firms can plan against. *Evidence planted:* Episode 4 (the ladder gives an answerable question), Episode 7 (Chapter III is a genuinely demanding engineering programme, whatever else it is), Episode 9 (the general-purpose chapter was written and in force within two years of the technology arriving).
2. **No — it delegated rights questions to people with no competence in them.** **Veale and Zuiderveen Borgesius (2021)**: "1980s product safety regulation", and fundamental-rights questions handed to "standardisation bodies with no fundamental rights experience." *Evidence planted:* Episode 6 (the objection in its own words), Episode 8 (the objection empirically vindicated), Episode 3 (a regime that produces no remedy for the person harmed), Episode 11 (a complaint right that yields an investigation, and €105 million of adjacent fines that yielded no money).
3. **No — it should have started from the rights.** **EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 5/2021**: the positive-list method cannot anticipate; societal risks to groups are invisible to it; biometric identification in public should have been banned outright. *Evidence planted:* Episode 4 (the design-time bet, and a classification that does not move when nationality is removed from the model), Episode 5 (prohibitions that are rights-shaped and sit awkwardly in a product statute), Episode 7 (Art. 27 as a graft).
4. **The frame is misposed — what was regulated was neither a product nor a right, but a market.** The operative concepts are **placing on the market** and **putting into service**; the sanctions are market sanctions; the actors are supply-chain roles. *Evidence planted:* Episode 3 (roles, not technologies), Episode 9 (a model regime with no market concept that fits it), Episode 11 (withdrawal as a sanction that does not exist for this subject matter).
5. **It was right and it was unfinished.** The frame is defensible; the infrastructure was not built. *Evidence planted:* Episode 8, in full.

**Where the weight actually leans, said separately for two different questions.**
- **On the narrow standardisation question the critics have been vindicated by events, and this is stated without hedging.** The prediction was made in 2021; by June 2026 no deliverable conferred the presumption; the law moved to fit the standards rather than the standards moving to fit the law. That is not a matter of opinion.
- **On the broad question of instrument shape in principle, the record is not in**, and the episode says so rather than closing the series with a manufactured conclusion.

**THE VERDICT — named, and it is narrow on purpose.**

**The series comes down on answer 5, sharpened: on the record available in September 2026, the product frame was a defensible choice that has not delivered the thing it was chosen for.** It was chosen for administrability — duties ex ante, at scale, against a determinate specification. **Its engine is the presumption of conformity, and the presumption has nothing under it.** Answer 4 is the clearest way to *see* why: what this instrument actually governs is a market, and its paradigm remedy is removal from one.

**Three facts decide it, each established earlier in the series and each named again here.**
1. **As of June 2026 not one JTC 21 deliverable had been cited in the Official Journal**, so the mechanism the high-risk regime runs on confers nothing (Episode 8).
2. **The application date moved to fit the standards**, by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 — sixteen months for Annex III, twelve for Annex I. The law was rescheduled around a private body's drafting timetable (Episode 8).
3. **The frame's paradigm remedy does not exist for the systems this series examined.** You cannot withdraw a scraped face database, a benefits model run inside a ministry, or a model served from a foreign data centre (Episodes 4, 9, 11).

**State it as the narrator's own position, earned — and say plainly what would overturn it:** a harmonised standard cited in the Official Journal in time for 2 December 2027, and that deadline holding. If the substance arrives, the product frame will have been slow and workable. **If the deadline moves a second time, the critics' structural claim stops being a prediction.**

**The order of the close — a sequence, not a choice.** First the verdict, named and owned. **Then the date, as the last word.** **Art. 112 ✓** is the Act's own scheduled moment for asking whether it worked: an annual assessment of whether Annex III and Article 5 need amending; a report by **2 August 2028** on the supervision and governance system, and on whether the AI Office has sufficient powers; a report by **2 August 2029**, and every four years after, that must assess **the structure of enforcement and the possible need for a Union agency**; and an assessment of enforcement by **2 August 2031**. The verdict is the argument's landing; the date is the reminder that the instrument is unfinished and the listener will be able to check.

**What the writer must also say out loud:** that the series held this question deliberately, since Episode 1, where the promise was made — and that the holding was so the listener would have the machinery before the argument.

**Load-bearing terms** (only two, and both are new here): **evaluation and review** — wrong: "a report nobody reads"; right: a scheduled statutory obligation with named dates and a power to propose amendment. And **"the law was delayed"** — wrong: postponement; right: on these facts, a substantive rewrite of when a duty exists, made by an amending regulation.

**Deliberately compressed:** no new apparatus is taught except Article 112.

**Transfer questions — the series' closing set.** 1. When a rule is late because its technical substance is late, has the law been delayed or has it been rewritten? 2. Who wrote the part of this rule that actually decides cases? 3. Does this instrument give the person harmed anything at all? 4. What single checkable fact would tell me in twelve months whether this regime works? 5. What is the strongest argument against my own view, and who makes it best?

**Sources.** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 112. Veale and Zuiderveen Borgesius (2021). EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 5/2021. Cantero Gamito (2025). CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 status, June 2026, including EN 18286 at Approval. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744.

**The single fact-check:** **whether any JTC 21 deliverable has been cited in the Official Journal at the moment of writing.** This is the verdict's deciding fact and it moves. If one has been cited, **the verdict changes** and the episode must be re-planned — say so to the producer rather than writing around it.

## The verdict episode
