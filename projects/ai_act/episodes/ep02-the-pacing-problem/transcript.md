## Two men, in print, three years apart {tone: measured}

In 1996 a law journal published a short essay with a joke for a title. Frank Easterbrook called it "Cyberspace and the Law of the Horse." It ran in the University of Chicago Legal Forum.

The joke does real work. Easterbrook's argument goes through a horse.

Suppose you set out to teach the law of the horse. You collect every case that ever involved one. People kicked by horses. People who bought a horse that turned out to be lame. People who left a horse to a nephew in a will. You now have a very large pile of cases and no subject. What you have is tort law, and contract law, and property law, wearing a horse costume. The pile teaches you less than the underlying law would have taught you on its own.

Cyberspace, he said, is a horse. There is no coherent law of it. Writing one is a mistake.

Three years later Lawrence Lessig answered him in the Harvard Law Review. He borrowed Easterbrook's title to do it: "The Law of the Horse: What Cyberlaw Might Teach." Lessig's reply went to what a new subject makes visible that an old one hides.

{pause: 1.4}

That is a real disagreement. Two named people, both of whom knew the field, in print, on the record. About whether you should ever write a statute named after a technology.

Europe has now answered that question, at length.

## What this episode is, and what it is not {tone: lucid}

This is The Instrument. What makes a thing an AI system at all, and the moment anybody's duty begins — that is built.

Today there is no statute. No article numbers to hold, no quoted text, no test to apply. This episode hands you a criterion instead. A way of judging whether an instrument was written at the right moment, and whether it can survive the thing it was written about.

Nothing today needs memorising. The names are in the episode so the positions have holders, not so you can list them afterwards.

## Nineteen months {tone: pointed}

One small fact carries the whole argument.

On the twenty-first of April 2021 the European Commission published its proposal for the Artificial Intelligence Act. That document has a name and a number — C-O-M, twenty twenty-one, two hundred and six, final — and I will say it once and then stop. In that proposal, you told whether something was an AI system by looking it up. There was a list, in an annex, of the techniques that counted. The list was the way in.

The definition Europe actually adopted does something different. It asks whether a system infers — whether it works out how to produce its output, rather than being told.

Take a second with that. What is missing from it?

{pause: 1.4}

A technique. Any technique. The adopted definition does not name one. Somewhere in that window, the list went away.

Something else happened in that window. ChatGPT was released to the public on the thirtieth of November 2022. Nineteen months after the proposal.

And the chapter of the Act that governs general-purpose models — the threshold set in raw computing power, the code of practice, the whole apparatus for a model not built for any particular job — is not in the April 2021 text. At all. It does not exist there.

## The reading I am going to refuse {tone: precise}

There is an obvious thing to say about that. It is wrong, so let me say it and then take it away.

The obvious thing is that the law was out of date before it passed. Brussels drafted for the technology of 2021 and shipped into the world of 2023. Too slow. Always too slow.

But look at what actually happened in those months. The list went away, and the general-purpose chapter got written, and the definition was rebuilt around what a system does rather than what it is made of. The drafters noticed. They changed the instrument while it was still in their hands.

So those nineteen months show something more awkward than a law that cannot keep up. They show an instrument that was a moving object right up to the moment it stopped moving.

That suggests a narrower question than whether law is slow. Ask instead whether a rule was drawn where the technology is, or where it was when somebody started drafting. You can put that to any instrument, and it has a different answer every time.

Four people have four different views about what follows. They all have names, and they are all in print.

## The refusal, at its strongest {tone: measured}

Start with Easterbrook, because his is the position most people skip.

He is not saying technology is unimportant. He is saying a statute named after a technology carries three defects, and they compound.

It dates. The name on the front of the law is a snapshot of what people feared in the drafting year. Technologies do not hold still for their portrait.

It entrenches. Whoever is already large when the rule is written has the lawyers to shape it and the budget to absorb it. A newcomer has neither.

And it is unnecessary, because you already have law. Somebody was misled: that is contract, or consumer protection. Somebody was injured: that is tort. Somebody was discriminated against: that is discrimination law, which has existed for decades, and which does not care whether the discriminating was done by a person, a spreadsheet or a neural network.

Take Easterbrook seriously and you get a very specific world. No gate. No tiers. No duty on anything at all until it has hurt somebody. Because the ordinary law he trusts arrives after the harm, through a court, brought by the person harmed.

That is the cost. Hold it in mind. For the rest of this series we are examining a machine built precisely to attach duties before anyone is hurt, and Easterbrook's answer is that the machine is not worth what it costs.

## Lessig's answer, and the idea underneath it {tone: curious}

Lessig replied in 1999. Studying the new thing, he said, teaches you something general. What it teaches is where constraint actually comes from.

The claim is easier to feel than to state. A rule tells you what you may do. But most of what you do is decided by what is possible, and what is possible is decided by whoever built the room you are standing in. A door that will not open is a better rule than a sign saying do not enter. On a network, the door is software. Lessig's book from the same year, Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace, later rewritten as Code, Version Two Point Zero, is built out of that one observation.

The year before that book, Joel Reidenberg had published the same structural point under a name of his own. Lex Informatica, in the Texas Law Review. Technical capability, he argued, sets rules that behave like law — which is to say, and this is me drawing it out rather than him, that they apply to everyone, they enforce themselves, and nobody voted for them.

An ordinary example makes that concrete, and the example is mine rather than his. Somewhere in a system there is a field that holds a person's name, and it was given a length, and a set of characters it will accept. Whoever chose those two things was not making policy. They were sizing a column in a database on a Tuesday afternoon. But from that afternoon onward, a person whose name is longer than the field, or written in characters the field will not take, cannot be recorded correctly by that organisation — and every form, letter and decision downstream inherits the truncation.

Now compare that with a legal rule. A legal rule has a text you can read. It has an author you can name. And it always has a route by which somebody can challenge it. The field length has none of those. There is no clause, no author on the record, and nobody to petition, because from inside the system it does not present as a decision at all. It presents as the way things are.

That is Reidenberg's claim in miniature. The constraint is total, it is invisible, and it was never argued about.

{pause: 1.4}

So: regulation by architecture. If you want the phrase to do work, do not hear it as code is law, which is a slogan. Hear it as a drafting instruction. It says that a regulator who wants effect should specify the built environment rather than the conduct, because the built environment enforces itself and conduct rules do not.

Both men were answering the 1990s belief that the internet was ungovernable. Their answer was that it was already governed. Just not by anyone you elected.

{pause: 1.4}

And the European Act is full of this. Full of it in places where it never says the word. The duty to make a high-risk system generate its own logs. The duty to mark synthetic content so a machine can read the mark. The requirement that technical documentation exist before the thing is sold. None of those regulate a decision. All of them regulate a structure, so that later, when somebody asks a question, the structure has already answered it.

So far we have two positions. Easterbrook: do not write the statute, use the law you have. Lessig and Reidenberg: if you write it, write it into the architecture, because that is where constraint lives anyway.

Neither is about timing. The next two are.

## Collingridge {tone: contemplative}

In 1980 David Collingridge published a book called The Social Control of Technology. It contains a trap nobody has got out of since.

Controlling a technology fails in one of two ways, he argued, and there is no third.

Early, while the thing is small and pliable and you could still steer it, you do not know what to steer away from. Nobody has been harmed yet. The harms are invisible because they have not happened.

Late, once the harms are documented and you know exactly what you would change, the technology is embedded. By then there are jobs attached to it, and capital already sunk into it, and everyone holding either of those will fight you for it. Change is now slow, expensive and contested.

Knowledge arrives on one schedule. Power to act expires on another. They do not overlap.

That is the Collingridge dilemma. It is why regulation always feels either premature or too late, when structurally it is one or the other. He was writing against a live backdrop of chemicals, nuclear power and asbestos — regimes that turned up after the harm was already on the record and the industry had already built its factories.

Now. What would you build, if you believed that?

{pause: 1.4}

You would build an instrument that assumes it is wrong. Write the rule early, because early is when you can. Then install inside the rule a power to change the rule, once you have learned something. Not a new statute every time. A mechanism.

The Act has three of those, and I will name them once as destinations rather than teach them. A power to amend the list of high-risk uses. A general power to adopt delegated acts — changes the Commission can make on its own, inside limits the Act already set. And a standing duty to evaluate and review the Regulation on a clock. Whether or not anybody in Brussels said Collingridge's name, those three are a Collingridge answer.

## The pacing problem {tone: measured}

The fourth position has a name you already half believe. That is why it needs handling carefully.

You have heard some version of it. Technology moves fast, the law is slow. Said that way it is a complaint, and complaints are not claims.

Made properly it is a claim, and a specific one. Larry Downes made it in 2009, in a book called The Laws of Disruption. His sentence, and these are his words: technology changes exponentially, but social, economic and legal systems change incrementally. Those are his words. Mine now: it does more than it looks. It describes two rates of change with different shapes. Which means the gap between them does not close. It widens.

In 2011 the claim got a book of its own, edited by Gary Marchant, Braden Allenby and Joseph Herkert — a collection about the growing gap between what gets built and what gets overseen. Its subtitle is where the phrase finally settled. The pacing problem.

The policy conclusion is the part that matters, and it is the opposite of Collingridge's. If institutions cannot keep pace, do not write anything precise enough to be overtaken. Prefer principles to rules. Prefer a regulator with discretion to a number in a statute. A threshold is obsolete the moment the technology walks past it, and then you are enforcing an old opinion about a new machine.

Hold both now. Collingridge: regulate early, and expect to be wrong. The pacing problem: never write anything a technology can outrun.

If you had to build one instrument that satisfied both at once, what would it have to contain?

{pause: 1.4}

## Both {tone: pointed}

It would have to contain a number, and a power to change the number.

That is what the Act does. There is a point in the general-purpose chapter where a model is presumed to have the most advanced capabilities going, once the computation used to train it crosses a stated quantity. A hard line, in the text, checkable by anyone with the training logs. That is the Collingridge move. Commit early, in public, to something specific.

And one paragraph later, the Commission is given power to move that number. Not by passing a new Act. By a mechanism inside this one.

The figure does not matter today. Keep the shape. A threshold, plus a lever attached to the threshold. Regulate early and expect to be wrong, and build in the apparatus for being wrong.

That is the second time I have put this to you, and it is the centre of the episode, so once more, plainly. Every instrument is written at a moment. Two questions are worth asking of any of them. Was this drawn where the technology is, or where it was when drafting began? And what can this instrument change about itself without going back to the legislature — and who holds that power?

## The objection to my own answer {tone: precise}

I find the Collingridge framing the most useful of the four, and the Act's response to it — commit early, install the levers — the right instinct. The strongest thing against that comes first, before I go any further.

A power to amend is not a mechanism. It is a promise about future political will.

A lever only moves if somebody pulls it. And the people who would have to pull it are the people most exposed to whoever has the best access. A statute that can adjust itself is a statute that can be adjusted by whoever is closest to the room.

Now test that against the record. The heavy part of this Act is the high-risk regime — the one this series spends most of its time inside. It was meant to start applying in the summer of 2026. It did not. For systems sold on their own, the date is now the second of December 2027.

And look at how it moved. Not by any lever inside the instrument. By a second Regulation, amending the first. The Commission proposed it in November 2025. The European Parliament approved it in June 2026. Seven months, a full legislative round — which did other things too, but this is the one that matters here — to change a date.

That is sharper than anything I could have invented. The Act does hold a genuine internal lever on this argument's own example: the computing threshold can be moved by the Commission, without a new Act and without a full legislative round. But when Europe needed to move something that mattered, the lever was not what it reached for. It went back to the legislature.

Whether that is Collingridge working as designed, or Collingridge being quietly abandoned, is not settled, and I will not pretend it is. It is still moving. December 2027 is when we find out.

## The awkward part {tone: pointed}

One more thing about the pacing problem, and it usually goes unsaid.

Two readings are available. Pick one before I answer.

The first: it is an analytic claim about institutions. Two rates of change, a widening gap, a real problem for anyone designing a rule.

The second: it is a lever. This moves too fast to regulate is, and has always been, the most useful sentence available to anyone who would prefer not to be regulated. It requires you to defend no particular practice. It relocates the whole argument to a discussion of legislative speed.

{pause: 1.4}

The answer is both. The same sentence does both jobs, which is exactly what makes it hard to handle.

Both sides have holders with names. In 2023 William Aspray and Philip Doty published a paper in the Journal of the Association for Information Science and Technology, asking in its title whether technology really does outpace policy, and whether it matters. What that paper is worth to us is its posture. It treats the outpacing claim as something to examine rather than assume, which is rarer than it sounds.

On the other side, in good faith, Adam Thierer of the Mercatus Center has written for years on the pacing problem, on Collingridge, and on what he calls permissionless innovation — the position that the default should be permission to build, with the burden on whoever wants to stop you.

I am not going to tell you the weight of opinion leans one way here, because I do not think it does. What I will give you is the move that keeps you honest. Refuse both of the easy ones. Do not take the pacing claim wholesale, because a claim that proves every regulation premature has proved nothing. And do not dismiss it as industry noise, because Collingridge was not in industry, and neither were Marchant, Allenby or Herkert, and the gap they describe is real.

Take it seriously, and ask who is saying it.

## Where it stands {tone: lucid}

Three things, and then I am done.

Easterbrook against Lessig is settled as a matter of what got built, and unsettled as a matter of argument. Europe wrote the statute named after the technology. The cost Easterbrook named was paid, and this series is partly an audit of what was bought with it.

Collingridge against the pacing problem is genuinely contested, and the Act refuses to choose between them. Its refusal is the fixed threshold with the movable lever.

And whether staged application solves the dilemma or only defers it is still moving, which is the one of the three that has a test date on it.

This is the tool, and you will use it three times before the series is done. Once on the ladder of risk, where the whole architecture turns out to rest on a bet made at design time. Once on standards, where the law was moved to fit a document that private bodies had not finished. And once on a threshold drawn exactly where the technology stood in the year somebody drew it. You do not need to hold on to any of that. Each will announce itself when it arrives.

## What you take with you {tone: measured}

Three questions. You will use them long after you have forgotten every name in this episode.

The first. When you meet a rule — any rule, about anything technical — ask whether it was drawn where the technology is, or where the technology was when somebody started drafting. The gap between those is usually years, and it is usually invisible on the page.

The second. Ask what this instrument can change about itself without anyone reopening the statute, and who holds that power. Every serious modern regulation has such a mechanism. The interesting question is never whether it exists. It is who gets to pull it, and what they have used it for so far.

The third is smaller and sharper, and you should keep it somewhere less comfortable. When somebody tells you a thing moves too fast to be regulated, ask who benefits from that being true.

{pause: 1.4}

Next time we go back inside the text. If you are going to regulate anyway — and Europe has — then before anything else you must say who is responsible. The Act has an answer, and it is narrower than most organisations expect. An airline once argued in a tribunal for a third option no such law contains.

It did not go well.
