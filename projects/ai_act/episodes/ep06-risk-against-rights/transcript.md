## Two documents, two months apart {tone: measured}

On 21 April 2021, the European Commission published a proposal for a regulation laying down harmonised rules on artificial intelligence. It carries a document number — COM(2021) 206 final — and it is the first full draft of the instrument this series has been taking apart. A definition, a list of banned practices, and a heavy regime for whatever it called high-risk. The definition in that draft worked by pointing at a list of techniques, set out in an annex to the proposal itself.

Two months later, on 18 June 2021, two European institutions published a joint reading of it.

The European Data Protection Board — the body where the national data-protection authorities sit together — and the European Data Protection Supervisor, who polices the Union's own institutions. Their document is Joint Opinion 5 of 2021. It is not a press release. It is the considered response of two bodies who had spent fifteen years enforcing Europe's other great information statute, reading a brand-new one and saying, in effect: you have built the wrong kind of instrument.

Two of their objections matter today, and there were more than two.

The first was about the list of prohibitions. The proposal banned a fixed set of named practices, and the two authorities called that a positive list — you can only forbid what you thought of in advance. The second was about who counts as harmed. The draft assessed risk to individuals; the authorities wanted risks to *groups*, to society, assessed as well.

And they asked for one thing they did not get. They wanted a general ban on biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces. What the adopted Act contains instead is a prohibition on live facial recognition by police in public, with three narrowly drawn exceptions written into it — one of them the targeted search for a missing person.

{pause: 1.4}

What to hold from all of that is this. Both documents agree about the danger.

Nobody in this argument thinks these systems are harmless. The Act's first article is written in the language of fundamental rights. The disagreement is somewhere else entirely, and it took me a while to see where.

## What kind of episode this is {tone: lucid}

This is The Instrument, and this episode has no statute in it.

No operative text. No article number I will ask you to carry. None of the change-one-fact work we have been doing — where a single word moves and a system falls in or out of a regime — because there is no instrument here to apply. What there is instead is an argument about whether the instrument should exist in this shape at all.

Think of it as the thing you judge with rather than the thing you judge.

So the ordinary rhythm is suspended for about half an hour. When it comes back, you will have something you did not have before: a question to put to the heavy chapter, and a reason the question is worth putting.

What you should already be holding, from the episodes that built up to this one. That the Act treats an AI system as a *product placed on a market* — that is where its duties come from and that is when they start. That duties attach to roles, not to technologies. And the one that does the most work today: the design-time bet. The tier a system lands in is fixed by what it is *intended* for, decided before it ships, by the person who built it.

Harm, though, happens later. In a room, to a person, in a context nobody at the design stage had to imagine.

That gap is what this whole argument is about.

## The road not taken, written down by the people who did not take it {tone: curious}

Now, the usual problem with an argument about the road not taken is that the road not taken has no documents. It is a thing commentators reconstruct afterwards.

Not here.

On the same day the Commission published its proposal, it published the impact assessment that sits behind it — a staff working document, SWD(2021) 84 final, 21 April 2021. And an impact assessment does something specific. It sets out the options that were considered, including the ones that lost.

There were five.

I am not going to read you five options. Two of them matter. One was a sectoral, ad-hoc approach — no single AI law at all, but rules written inside each field that already has a regulator. The other was the maximal one: mandatory requirements for every AI application, whatever risk it posed.

The Act as adopted matches a different option on that list — the horizontal instrument with hard requirements for the high-risk systems, and voluntary codes of conduct for everything below. I want to be careful about what I am claiming. The part of that document I have does not show the Commission's own statement of which option it preferred. So: the Act matches that option. I am not telling you the document declares it the winner, because I have not read the page where it would say so.

That is still remarkable. The alternatives to the instrument we have are enumerated, in writing, by the body that chose against them. Most of the time you are guessing at the road not taken. Here it has a document number.

## The boundary, and one thing I am handing forward {tone: warm}

Before the positions themselves, a word on what I am not going to do.

This is an argument *about* rights-based regulation. It is not a course in rights. What a fundamental right actually is, where the Charter of Fundamental Rights came from, how a court decides whether a limit on a right goes too far — that is a series of its own, and it is called The Charter. You do not need any of it today. If you have been waiting for me to explain how that test works, stop waiting, and let it go.

Everything in this episode works without it.

## Position one, at its strongest {tone: measured}

So let me give you each position at its best, with someone's name on it.

The first is the Act as built. Risk-based product safety. Its holders are the Commission and the co-legislators, and their defence is in that impact assessment: proportionality — heavier duties where the risk is greater, lighter where it is smaller, and none at all at the bottom.

The defence on the merits goes like this. This is the only European machinery that operates *before* anyone is harmed, and at scale. It reaches every unit placed on the market. It imposes duties at the moment a thing is put up for sale, not after a claimant has found a lawyer. It runs on an enforcement apparatus that already exists — market surveillance authorities, notified bodies, a marking on a product — with decades of institutional practice behind it. And it gives a firm a determinate answer to the only question a firm can act on: what, precisely, must I do before I ship?

There is academic work on the model the Act actually adopted, and it is worth naming because it is a defence and not a demolition. Ronit Justo-Hanani, writing in the journal Policy Design and Practice in 2026, examines the Act as co-regulation — a hybrid, where a public rule points at a private specification and the two together make the duty. The argument runs in the Act's favour, with a condition: the design presupposes regulatory capacity that has to be built, and the framework should mandate data-sharing between private and public actors to build it.

A defence that names its own precondition. That is the shape of an honest one.

{pause: 1.4}

Now the cost of adopting this position, because every position here has one. If your duties have to be determinate before anyone is harmed, they have to be about *process*. There are a half-dozen of them in the high-risk chapter, and you do not need them today. Two give you the shape: keep a risk-management file, and design in human oversight. Compliance is then demonstrated the way compliance always is in product law — by documents, measured against a standard.

Which means a system can satisfy every one of those duties, completely, and still ruin somebody's week.

## Position two, at its strongest {tone: measured}

The second position is the one those two authorities were arguing for in June 2021.

A rights-based instrument triggers on something different. Not on what the system *is* — its category, its intended purpose, the box it was put in at design time — but on how severely it interferes with a protected interest of a real person. The assessment is contextual: same software, same settings, different context, different answer. And the remedy runs to the person interfered with, not only to a regulator.

Hold the contrast, because it is the spine of the episode. A product frame asks what the system is. A rights frame asks what it does to someone.

Their positive-list objection deserves a demonstration, and the Act has already supplied one.

The list of prohibited practices was written in 2021. It was adopted in 2024. And in 2026 it was reopened — Regulation 2026/1744, in force from 27 July 2026, inserted two new prohibitions into it, covering AI-generated sexual imagery of identifiable people made without their consent, and AI-generated child sexual abuse material. Those become enforceable on 2 December 2026.

Nobody sensible thinks those practices were fine in 2021. They were not on the list because in 2021 the list's drafters were not looking at systems that could do that on a phone, at will, to anyone.

That is the positive-list problem, happening. You can only ban what you thought of. And when you think of something new, you need a legislature.

{pause: 1.4}

What would a rights-based instrument have cost? Be honest about this, because its advocates generally are. Contextual assessment cannot tell a firm in advance what to do. Severity is judged after the fact, by someone with authority to judge — which in practice means a court, or a regulator acting like one, which in practice means afterwards. You gain sensitivity to the actual situation and you lose the thing product law is for: acting early, uniformly, on everyone, before the harm.

## The structural critique {tone: pointed}

The third position is not a rival design. It is a diagnosis of where this one came from.

Michael Veale and Frederik Zuiderveen Borgesius published it in 2021, in Computer Law Review International, under the title "Demystifying the Draft EU Artificial Intelligence Act". Their claim: the Act takes its structure from four decades of European product-safety regimes rather than from fundamental-rights law — and when you borrow machinery, you inherit its assumptions. Conformity assessment, a marking, and a presumption that a system meeting the standard is lawful.

Two of their phrases, and these are their words, not mine. They describe the Act as leaning on — quote — "1980s product safety regulation" — end of quote. And they object to the delegation of what are effectively fundamental-rights questions to — quote — "standardisation bodies with no fundamental rights experience" — end of quote.

Put plainly: the hard questions get answered by engineers in a committee, writing a specification document, and the law then treats the answer as settled.

Their status matters and I will state it twice, differently. Contested but foundational — everybody in this field argues with this paper or from it. And dated on specifics: they were reading the 2021 draft, not the Act as adopted and certainly not the 2026 amendments. Where they describe a provision, check the provision. Where they describe the architecture, they were describing something that did not change.

If you take them seriously, you do not write the harmonised-standards machinery the way it is written.

{pause: 1.4}

So — a question, and there is an answer to it. Of the two objections on the table so far, the positive list and the standards, which one has already been proved right by events?

{pause: 1.4}

The standards. And it is a date that proves it.

The high-risk regime runs on a presumption: meet the harmonised standard, and you are presumed to meet the legal requirement. A standard confers that presumption only once it has been finalised and its reference published in the Official Journal. As of June 2026, not one deliverable from JTC 21 — the joint CEN and CENELEC technical committee writing these standards — had been cited there. The most advanced of them, a quality-management standard for AI Act purposes, was at approval stage and is the likeliest to be cited first.

The engine of the high-risk regime, at the point that regime was due to apply, had nothing under it. That is why the deadline moved to December 2027.

On the narrow question, the critics were right, and they were right five years early.

## The fourth position, and whose it is {tone: measured}

There is a fourth. Let the medicines regulator regulate medical AI. Let the financial supervisor handle credit models. Let whoever inspects policing handle policing tools.

This one has a holder, and I nearly told you it did not.

Martin Ebers — professor of information technology law at the University of Tartu, president of the Robotics and AI Law Society — argues for it in a paper called "Truly Risk-Based Regulation of Artificial Intelligence: How to Implement the EU's AI Act", in the European Journal of Risk Regulation. Note the shape of the argument, because it is cleverer than the label suggests. He does not say risk-based regulation is wrong. He says this Act is not truly risk-based *by its own standard* — that it announces proportionality and then departs from it, by fixing its risk categories in advance and closing them, on a thin empirical basis, with a definition of AI broad enough to sweep in things nobody worried about. His remedy is assessment tailored to the risk an application actually poses in the field it operates in.

And this is where it joins the first half of this episode. A sector-by-sector approach was Option 2 in the Commission's own impact assessment. Assessed in 2021. Not taken.

So a named professor argues today for the road the Commission wrote down and walked away from.

What would that cost? Deep competence in each field, and no coherence across them — twenty-seven Member States, multiplied by however many sectoral regulators each of them has, each drawing the line somewhere slightly different. That reckoning of the costs is mine. It is not his, and you should not hear it as his.

## The strongest thing that can be said against me {tone: contemplative}

Of everything on the table, I find the standards objection the most persuasive, because it is the only one with a date attached.

So let me put the best answer to it, before I go anywhere near resolving anything.

The drafters saw it coming. The Regulation does not simply hand the substance to the standards bodies and hope. It gives the Commission a fallback: where the standardisation organisations will not take the job, or do not deliver in time, or where what they do deliver — quote — "insufficiently address fundamental rights concerns" — end of quote — the Commission may write the specification itself, as a binding act. That lever has a limit written into it: it is available only while no reference to a harmonised standard covering the same ground has been published.

Somebody in the drafting room had read the objection, or reached it independently, and built a lever for exactly that failure. That is a serious answer. It means the delegation was never unconditional.

Whether the lever gets pulled is a different question, and I will not pretend to more than I have. I have found no record of it being used for the high-risk requirements. What I can tell you is that when the standards were late, the response was not to write specifications centrally. The response was to move the law's dates to fit the standards.

Which tells you something about how heavy that lever is to pull.

## Where this stands {tone: precise}

Let me say plainly where we have got to, because there have been four positions and you may have been driving.

Three things are established. The Act is a product-safety instrument by deliberate choice, from a documented menu of alternatives. Two European data-protection institutions said in 2021 that this was the wrong shape, and lost. And on one narrow limb of their objection — the delegation of substance to technical standards — the critics have been vindicated by events, as of June 2026, and I date that because it could change.

{pause: 1.4}

On the big question, the weight does not lean.

I am not going to invent a consensus for you. There is no settled view among informed people about whether a rights-triggered instrument would have been better, and the reason is that nobody has built one at this scale, so the comparison has no other side.

What I can do is say what each side would have to show.

The product side would have to show standards actually arriving, and then show a conformity assessment catching something that a rights review would have caught — a real defect, stopped at the market's edge, by a document. That is the case they have not yet been able to make.

The rights side would have to show that a severity-triggered, contextual regime can be administered by a market-surveillance authority across a continent, without collapsing into case-by-case adjudication after the harm. Naming one jurisdiction where that works would do it. I know of none.

Two contested joints left, and they pull in opposite directions. The first is a duty that has not started yet. The Act tells a deployer of certain high-risk systems to assess the system's impact on fundamental rights before switching it on — either a genuine graft of rights method onto product law, or a form nobody will read. Still moving, and untestable for now, because it does not bite until December 2027.

The second one you can reach yourself. Somewhere in this Act there is a whole chapter that is very hard to explain as product safety at all. Which one?

{pause: 1.4}

The prohibitions. A ban on inferring a worker's emotions is not a rule about defective goods. That looks a great deal like rights-based regulation, smuggled into a product statute in the one chapter where the drafters could get away with it. Contested — and a good case can be made that it is exactly what happened.

There is one thing I have deliberately not built this episode on, and you may have noticed its absence. The obvious battleground here is live facial recognition in public — the thing the two authorities wanted banned outright. I have left it aside because I cannot do it properly: there is no documented European deployment or enforcement case under that prohibition that I can put a name, a date and a decision to. An argument about rights deserves better than a hypothetical, so I am not going to fake one.

## The honest statement, and what you now ask {tone: contemplative}

The strongest true sentence available in 2026 is this. The product route bought administrability, and paid for it in contextual sensitivity and in individual remedy — and the bill has not fully arrived.

Say that again slightly differently, because it is the whole episode. Europe chose the instrument that can act before harm, uniformly, on everything. The price is that the instrument asks what a system *is* rather than what it does to someone, and that when it does its job perfectly, the person on the other end of the machine gets nothing from it directly.

{pause: 1.4}

So what do you carry out of half an hour with no statute in it?

Three questions, and I am leaving two more on the table.

The first. When you meet any rule about any automated system — does it trigger on what the system *is*, or on what it does to a person? Almost every rule you will ever read does one or the other, and once you can hear which, you can predict most of what follows from it.

The second. If the developer complies with every duty in the rule, completely, does the affected person get anything at all? Sometimes the answer is yes. Very often it is that the regulator gets something and the person gets nothing, and that is a design decision somebody made, not an accident.

And the third, which is the one this episode nearly failed. When someone tells you there was a better alternative — was it actually proposed, by a named person, in a place you can find? Or is it a debating position with nobody behind it? I could source three of the four positions today, and for the fourth I had to go and find its holder or cut it.

That test is not pedantry. A road not taken with no traveller on it is not a road.

## Back inside the text {tone: lucid}

This argument does not resolve in the abstract. It cannot — the comparison has only one built side.

So we do the concrete thing instead. Next time, we open the heaviest chapter in the Act and go through it requirement by requirement: what a provider of a high-risk system actually has to do, in what order, and on whose say-so. And with the lens you now have, one question runs underneath the whole of it.

What do those duties assume about the human being who is supposed to be supervising a machine?
