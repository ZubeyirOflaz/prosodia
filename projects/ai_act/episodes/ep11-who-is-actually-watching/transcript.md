## The total, and the amount {tone: measured}

In 2024, the Dutch data protection authority fined an American company thirty and a half million euros.

The company is Clearview AI. It had scraped faces off the open web — photographs people had posted, and photographs other people had posted of them — and built them into a facial recognition service, which it sold to police forces.

The Dutch authority was not the first to act. Two years earlier, in October 2022, the French regulator had fined the same company twenty million. Clearview did not pay. So the French regulator added five point two million more, in overdue-penalty payments. A penalty for not paying the penalty.

Five European authorities have fined this company. Between them, the total comes to roughly one hundred and five million euros.

That is the first number.

The second one is shorter. Clearview disputes that European regulators have jurisdiction over it at all. It has not paid. Collection of every one of those fines remains contested.

One hundred and five million assessed. Nothing collected.

{pause: 1.4}

Somebody has to go and get it.

There is one more figure that follows this company everywhere, and it needs handling carefully, because how you handle it is half of today's lesson. Clearview says its database holds more than sixty billion images. Listen to the shape of that sentence. The company says. Nobody independent has counted it, and a number a firm publishes about its own product is evidence about the firm's marketing before it is evidence about the product. I have now said it once. I will not say it again.

## Where this sits {tone: lucid}

This is The Instrument, a series about Regulation 2024/1689 — the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act — and about what follows from one decision made at the start: to regulate artificial intelligence the way Europe regulates lifts, toys and medical devices. As a product, placed on a market.

The series has built one machine in six parts. Two of them decide today's episode: a definition that says what is inside the law at all, and roles, which say whose duty a duty is. The rest — tiers, red lines, requirements, a separate regime for the general-purpose models — you do not need to hold this morning.

Every one of those parts is a rule about what somebody must do.

Today is the other half. Who finds out, who may act, and whether anything arrives at the end of it.

## Market surveillance {tone: precise}

Start with the phrase the entire enforcement half of this Act rests on. Market surveillance.

If you work anywhere near finance, you have just heard the wrong thing. This has nothing to do with securities, and nothing to do with competition. The word market here means the market in goods — the shelf, the warehouse, the shipment. And surveillance means watching what is on it, after it is already there.

The Act's own definition is deliberately thin. A market surveillance authority is, in the Act's words, the national authority carrying out the activities and taking the measures pursuant to Regulation (EU) 2019/1020. End of quotation. That is a cross-reference. The Act is pointing at a body that already exists, under a law that already exists, doing a job Europe has been doing for forty years.

What job? These are the people who pull a batch of dangerous toys off the shelves, and who test a consignment of chargers and find that they catch fire. They order a manufacturer to fix something, or to take it back. They work after the product is on the market, which is why the tradition calls their half post-market. The certificate comes first, and somebody else issues it. These are the people who find out whether the certificate was worth anything.

Now the Act hands them artificial intelligence.

There is a twist in the handover, and it is worth keeping. For most systems, the market surveillance authority is whatever product-safety body a Member State chooses to designate. But where a system is used for law enforcement, the Act makes that authority the national data protection authority instead. The privacy regulators. The same kind of body that fined Clearview.

So, the second term. National competent authority. It sounds like it must mean the AI regulator, the one office in each country with the whole file on its desk. It means something narrower. In the Act's words, a national competent authority is a notifying authority or a market surveillance authority. End of quotation. One of the two, and the two do different jobs. The notifying authority appoints and supervises the certifiers. The market surveillance authority polices what is on the market. And where a system is used by the European Union's own institutions, the Act says to read national competent authority as the European Data Protection Supervisor.

Two bodies in each Member State, doing different halves. And a third body, for the Union's own machinery.

So which of those two polices a system that is already out there, running, in use?

{pause: 2.0}

The market surveillance authority. The notifying one works on the way in. The market surveillance one works on everything afterwards.

## The Office that is not an office {tone: pointed}

Now the one that almost everybody gets wrong.

The AI Office. You have heard it described as Europe's new artificial intelligence agency, and if you have read about this Act anywhere in the press you are probably carrying a picture of a building in Brussels, with a staff, a letterhead, and a director who can take someone to court.

Here is the definition, and it is worth hearing all the way to the end. The AI Office means — quote — the Commission's function of contributing to the implementation, monitoring and supervision of AI systems and general-purpose AI models, and AI governance. And then the clause that decides everything: references in this Regulation to the AI Office shall be construed as references to the Commission. End of quotation.

A function. Not a body.

The Act gives the thing a name, and then tells you that the name is a way of saying the Commission — which means its powers over any particular system are not a matter of institutional weight. They are written down, article by article, and they stop somewhere.

## Three enforcers, and a rule about which {tone: measured}

So who enforces this Act? The honest answer has three parts, and the Commission set them out itself, in a press release dated the thirty-first of July 2026 — two days before the enforcement powers switched on.

For the transparency duties and the prohibited practices, responsibility is split three ways.

The first share goes to the AI Office, which is to say the Commission: for AI systems offered by the same provider as the general-purpose model underneath them, and for systems built into a very large online platform or search engine.

The second share is by far the biggest. It goes to national competent authorities, for all other AI systems. Everything else. Every hiring tool, every credit scorer, every system in every hospital and school and police station across twenty-seven countries.

The third share goes to the European Data Protection Supervisor, for systems used by the Union's own institutions and agencies. That share comes with its own money. The Supervisor may fine a Union institution up to one and a half million euros for a prohibited practice, and up to seven hundred and fifty thousand for anything else.

Call that the three-way split. The phrase will come back.

{pause: 1.4}

Underneath the split sits a competence rule, and I think it is the most useful thing in this episode. The 2026 amendment rewrote Article 75, and the rewritten version says the AI Office is exclusively competent — those two words are the Act's own — for two classes of system.

The first: systems built on a general-purpose model, where the model and the system come from the same provider, or from the same corporate group. Same house, top to bottom. And even that class has pieces cut back out of it — law-enforcement systems are pulled out, and there are further exclusions I am not going to walk you through.

The second: systems that are, or that sit inside, a very large online platform or a very large search engine, as designated under the Digital Services Act.

That is the whole of the AI Office's exclusive territory. And it carries a limit that people drop the moment they repeat it: the competence runs to the providers of those systems, and it reaches a deployer only where the deployer is the provider, or part of the same group.

Everything outside those two classes belongs to the Member States.

So far, two things. There are three enforcers and not one. And which of them may act on any given system is settled by a written competence rule, not by how important the system happens to feel.

## Why it was split at all {tone: contemplative}

It could have gone the other way. Europe could have built one agency, in one place, with one enforcement staff, the way it effectively has for the general-purpose models. Why didn't it?

Two reasons, and the first is older than this Act.

Market surveillance is a Member State competence. In the product-safety tradition the Act borrowed its whole architecture from, the checking of goods on a market has always been national work, done by national inspectors under national administrative law. To centralise it for artificial intelligence and nothing else would have meant building a parallel European inspectorate alongside the one that already exists for everything else on the shelf.

The second reason is arithmetic. Consider what central enforcement would actually mean. Every recruitment tool in a country of eighty million people. Every credit model at every regional bank. Every triage system in every emergency department. A single European office does not inspect those. Nobody's single office does.

So the Act decentralised, and in doing so it traded something away. What it traded is twenty-seven different levels of capacity, expertise, staffing and appetite.

The Act has an answer to that cost, and it is worth knowing exactly how far the answer goes. There is a European AI Board — representatives of the Member States, sitting together with the Commission, to keep application of the Act consistent across the twenty-seven. It issues opinions, recommendations and guidance, and it supports the standards work. It cannot fine anyone. Coordination is the whole of its power, which makes it a corrective to divergence and not a substitute for an enforcer.

So the cost stands. The same provision, enforced hard in one country and not at all in another. The Commission named that cost in its own press release, and the wording is careful: quote — effective enforcement will also depend on Member States ensuring that national competent authorities are properly designated and adequately resourced. End of quotation.

Properly designated. Adequately resourced. That is a statement of dependency, published by the body that wrote the law, two days before the law's enforcement powers began.

## What they can actually do {tone: precise}

From the second of August 2026, a national market surveillance authority can act under this Act. What does acting mean, concretely?

It can demand documentation from a provider. It can order corrective measures. And it can take the thing off the market.

The AI Office, inside its two classes, gets a heavier kit, and this part the Act spells out in its own words. It has all the powers of a market surveillance authority. It can ask for information by simple request, or by formal decision — and the difference is that a simple request you may decline, while a decision you may not. Either way, if you answer, answering incorrectly is itself finable.

The AI Office can also inspect, remotely or on site. Its officials may enter premises and take copies of anything they find there. And then, in the Act's own list, they may — quote — seal any business premises and books or records for the duration of, and to the extent necessary for, the inspection. End of quotation.

Sealing a room. That is a criminal-investigation verb, in a product-safety statute.

Now hear the limit written into the same sentence, because it is the limit this whole episode turns on. The officials may enter business premises, land or property — quote — located in the Union. End of quotation. Powers over places inside the Union.

Two more pieces and I will leave the machinery. If an operator offers undertakings to fix things, the AI Office can make those undertakings legally binding by decision, and close the file. And where it finds non-compliance, it can fine — applying the Member State penalty scale as if it were a national authority — and it can add a daily charge to force compliance, which the Act caps at five per cent of average daily income or worldwide annual turnover, per day. Per day. And the whole of it is appealable. The Court of Justice can cancel a fine, reduce it, or raise it.

Underneath all of that sits the quiet obligation that is supposed to feed it. A provider must run what the Act calls a post-market monitoring system — the activities a provider carries out to collect and review experience from the systems it has already placed on the market — and must report serious incidents. The enforcement chapter assumes information flows uphill. The information comes, in the first instance, from the company being watched.

## What I am leaving out, and saying so {tone: neutral}

I have just compressed a great deal, and you should know that I did.

Between the powers I have described and the remedies I am coming to, the Act runs nine further articles of procedure. Two of them will give you the shape. There is a procedure for when a provider has classified its own system as not high-risk and an authority disagrees. And there is a procedure for when a system is fully compliant and still dangerous.

Think of all nine as one ladder. A national authority finds a problem, requires a fix, escalates if it does not get one, and the matter can end up decided for the whole Union rather than one country. That is the shape. I am not walking the rungs, and if you ever have to work a real case, that is where you will live.

Two smaller things get a name from me and nothing more. Article 69 lets a Member State borrow experts from the Act's scientific panel to help with its own enforcement, which is a quiet admission about where the expertise actually sits. And an annex at the back — Annex Ten — is simply a list of the Union laws that set up Europe's big border and migration databases, reached through the article on systems that were already running before this Act applied to them.

One more I will name, because I think it is the most overlooked provision in this Act — and that is my judgement, not a finding. Article 77 concerns the powers of national fundamental-rights bodies, and as I understand it, it lets them demand documentation from providers and deployers; I am working from the article's subject matter rather than from its words in front of me, so take the detail as a pointer. Not the product-safety inspector. The equality commission, the ombudsman, the human-rights institute. A door into the technical file, opened from the rights side of the building. Almost nobody talks about it.

## The three ceilings {tone: measured}

Money. And the shape first, because the shape is the teachable part.

There are three penalty ceilings in this Act. Which one applies to you is decided by which rule you broke — not by how much harm you did. Hold that, because it is counter-intuitive and it survives everything else in this beat.

The top ceiling is for a prohibited practice — the red lines. Seven per cent of total worldwide annual turnover, or thirty-five million euros, whichever is higher. Say that last part to yourself, because it is the half people drop. Whichever is higher. For a large firm the percentage bites; for a small one the flat sum does.

The middle ceiling covers the ordinary obligations — the duties this series has spent nine episodes on. Three per cent, or fifteen million.

And then the twist, which is the part worth carrying out of here. Supply an authority with incorrect, incomplete or misleading information, and the ceiling is one per cent, or seven and a half million. The smallest number in the Act attaches to misleading the regulator.

{pause: 1.4}

And one inversion. For a small or medium-sized enterprise, or a start-up, the rule flips: they pay the lower of the two figures, not the higher. The same infringement is priced differently depending on who committed it.

## Applicable, and enforceable {tone: precise}

Now the dates, and this is the only episode that lays them out end to end. Six of them. You do not need to hold six. Hold the gap between the first two, because the gap is the lesson.

The second of February 2025. The definitions arrive. The literacy duty arrives. And the prohibitions arrive — the practices the Act says may not be done at all.

The second of August 2025, six months later. The penalties for those prohibitions arrive.

Sit with that for a second. For six months, a set of practices was prohibited across the European Union, and no penalty existed for doing them. The rule was applicable. It was not yet enforceable. Those are two different states, and a lawyer who conflates them will give you confident advice that is wrong by half a year.

The second of August 2026 is the big one. The transparency duties begin — the duty to tell a person they are talking to a machine. The AI Office's powers over general-purpose models begin. And the national market surveillance powers begin. That is the date this episode is really about. Before it, nobody in a Member State could act under this Act at all.

The second of December 2026 brings the two prohibitions the 2026 amendment added.

The second of December 2027 brings the high-risk regime for the stand-alone systems — hiring, credit, benefits. The heaviest chapter in the Act, and it is not in force as I speak.

And the second of August 2028 brings high risk for the systems embedded in products that Europe already regulates. Why those last two moved is a story this series has already told, and it is not about enforcement.

Systems already on the market when a deadline passes have a transitional article of their own. It exists, it is not simple, and I am not teaching it today.

So: where are we, on the fifteenth of September 2026? Past the powers. Past the transparency duties. More than a year away from the high-risk regime that most people think of when they think of this Act.

## Change one fact {tone: curious}

Let me take Clearview and move it, one detail at a time.

First, the disclosure, and it belongs here rather than at the start. Every one of those five fines was a data protection fine. Not one of them was imposed under the Act this series is about — that Act did not exist in enforceable form when Clearview did what it did. And the other law, the one that did apply, collected nothing.

Now, the first variant. Imagine Clearview today, offering the same service to a European police force. The Act's red line covers — quote — AI systems that create or expand facial recognition databases through the untargeted scraping of facial images from the internet or CCTV footage. End of quotation. That is what this company does, described in advance. Top ceiling — seven per cent, or thirty-five million, whichever is higher.

Who imposes it? Not the AI Office. Clearview's product is not built on a general-purpose model from the same house, and it is not a very large online platform. It falls to a national authority, under national procedure. So it falls to be collected by exactly the mechanisms that have so far collected nothing.

Second variant. Change one fact — give Clearview a European subsidiary. Now there are premises located in the Union. There is a legal entity with a bank account inside the jurisdiction. Collection becomes genuinely possible.

But something else becomes possible too, and this series has already watched it happen to another American firm. Establishing in Europe gives a company an argument about which national authority is entitled to decide its case at all. Being reachable and being hard to reach turn out to arrive together.

Third variant, and this one is ordinary rather than dramatic. A hiring platform fails to keep the documentation the high-risk regime demands. No red line, no scraping, no faces. That is the middle ceiling, three per cent or fifteen million. It is not the AI Office's to impose. It is a national authority's, under Article 99. And it cannot be imposed at all until the second of December 2027.

Three variants, one question each time: which body, which ceiling, which date.

## What a person actually gets {tone: measured}

Everything so far has been regulators. Where are you in this?

In three places, and they are smaller than you would hope.

The first is the complaint. The Act says — quote — any natural or legal person having grounds to consider that there has been an infringement of the provisions of this Regulation may submit complaints to the relevant market surveillance authority. End of quotation. Anyone. No standing requirement, no showing that you personally were harmed.

And then the next sentence, which tells you what the complaint is worth. Quote: such complaints shall be taken into account for the purpose of conducting market surveillance activities. End of quotation.

{pause: 1.4}

Taken into account, for the purpose of conducting market surveillance activities. A complaint here is an input to a regulator's workload. It starts an investigation. It does not start a case, and it does not end with anything arriving in your hand.

The second is the explanation. Article 86 gives an affected person the right to obtain — quote — clear and meaningful explanations of the role of the AI system in the decision-making procedure and the main elements of the decision taken. End of quotation.

Hear the limits, because there are three and they are load-bearing. You get it from the deployer, the organisation that used the system — not from whoever built it. It covers the stand-alone high-risk systems, the hiring and credit and benefits ones, with critical infrastructure carved out. And it bites only where the decision produces legal effects, or something comparably serious for you. The right also applies only to the extent it is not already provided somewhere else in Union law.

So the right is not the right to know how the model works. It is the right to be told what part the system played, and what the main elements of the decision were. Those are different things, and in a contested case the difference is where the argument will be.

The third is narrower still and matters more than it looks. Someone inside the organisation who reports an infringement is protected — the Act does that in a single sentence, by pulling across the European whistleblower directive whole.

## The fines that were not {tone: pointed}

Now the exercise this whole episode was built to let you do.

Shortly after the enforcement powers went live in August 2026, a story appeared on aggregator sites and spread: that the AI Office had issued the Act's first fines, against three ordinary commercial companies — a hiring platform, a credit-scoring provider, a retail chain running emotion recognition — for a total in the tens of millions. The figure usually given is forty-seven million euros.

There is no evidence for it, and three reasons to think it is wrong. I will give them in the order of how much they teach.

First, there is no authoritative source. A search restricted to the Union's own domains returns the enforcement framework pages, and the announcement that enforcement was beginning, and no press release announcing any fine.

Second, the Commission's own release points the other way. That release of the thirty-first of July 2026 describes machinery being switched on — the scientific panel, the complaints tools, the whistleblower channels. It names no company. It names no penalty.

Third, and this is the one to keep: the story does not fit the architecture. And here I have to be careful, because the easy version of this argument was true in 2024 and is now wrong. The easy version says the AI Office only ever deals with general-purpose models, so it cannot fine an ordinary company. That is no longer right. Since the 2026 amendment, the AI Office can fine the provider of a high-risk system — when that system falls inside its two classes of exclusive competence.

So do not reason from a general impression. Apply the rule.

A pan-European hiring platform. Is it a system built on a general-purpose model from the same house?

{pause: 2.0}

No. Is it a very large online platform?

{pause: 2.0}

No. A credit-scoring provider — same two questions, same two answers. A retail chain running emotion recognition in its shops is a deployer rather than a provider, and it is nobody's very large search engine.

All three fall outside. All three belong to national competent authorities. The reports credit the AI Office with fines that, on the amended text, are still not the AI Office's to impose.

{pause: 1.4}

That is the single most useful thing this series can hand you, and it is why the architecture had to come before the enforcement. You just reached a conclusion about a news story from a competence rule you can quote — rather than from a feeling about how the thing is probably shaped.

And the arithmetic underneath it. Confirmed penalties under this Act, as of the fifteenth of September 2026: zero. Formal proceedings: none established. There are secondary reports, unconfirmed, that the AI Office sent its first requests for information to frontier model providers — the companies building the largest general-purpose models — around the end of August 2026. Requests for information are not proceedings. Penalties for the red lines have existed for thirteen months. The market-surveillance powers are six weeks old. And nothing has yet been collected from anyone.

## What the rule assumes about the institution {tone: grave}

That brings me to the thing underneath all of it.

Product-safety enforcement works because of one move, and the Act has two words for the two halves of that move. They sound like synonyms. They are not.

Withdrawal of an AI system means, in the Act's words, any measure aiming to prevent an AI system in the supply chain being made available on the market. End of quotation. Stop it going out.

Recall of an AI system means any measure aiming to achieve the return to the provider, or taking out of service, or disabling the use of an AI system made available to deployers. End of quotation. Bring back what already went out.

That pair is why product safety bites. A defective lift comes out of the building. A contaminated batch comes off the shelf, and an inspector can go and look at the empty shelf. The threat is not really the fine. The threat is that your product stops existing in the market, and everybody who bought one is told to stop.

Now hold a scraped facial-recognition index against that.

You cannot withdraw it from a supply chain, because there is no supply chain — there is a server and an interface. You cannot recall it, because nothing was ever handed over that could come back. An embedding, once computed from a photograph, does not return. A model served from a data centre in another country is not on a shelf anybody can clear. And the person whose face is in it cannot be made un-indexed by an order addressed to a warehouse.

Say it plainly. The Act inherited an enforcement tradition whose central move does not exist for much of its subject matter. What it has instead is money.

And we have already seen what money does when the company is somewhere else.

Call that an institutional problem — a competence split three ways, with no European bailiff behind it. But institutional is only a convenient name for everything upstream. Push back one level and it is economic: the calculation a firm makes when the expected cost of ignoring a foreign regulator is lower than the cost of complying, and when nothing in the jurisdiction can be sealed, because nothing of theirs is here. Push back once more and it is a technical fact about what kind of object this is. A thing with no shelf to be taken off.

One hundred and five million assessed. Nothing collected. That is what happens when you point a tool built for objects at something that is not one.

## Where it is contested {tone: neutral}

Three disagreements, and they are in different states.

Are the national authorities resourced for this? The weight leans clearly to no, and I am not making a brave claim: the Commission said the thing itself, in the sentence you heard earlier, two days before the powers began. When the drafter publishes a caveat about whether the enforcers exist, the dependency is already stated in the record.

Does the right to an explanation add anything that European law did not already give? Genuinely contested — and I am going to stop at the edge of it, because answering properly means comparing it to another instrument, and that comparison is not this episode's.

Can fines be collected from a firm with no European establishment? Still moving. And Clearview is the evidence, in both directions: five authorities, roughly one hundred and five million euros, and no money. Whether that changes is a question about whether one country's fine can be made to stick in another — and about who would actually go and get it. Nobody has answered it here yet.

## What you now ask {tone: measured}

So what do you take with you, when you meet a system and somebody tells you it is regulated?

Five questions, and I will give you the three that do the most work.

If this rule were broken, who exactly would find out — and by what actual route does the information reach a person with power?

Which body may sanction this actor? And then the follow-up, which is the one that catches errors: does the claim I just read match that architecture? You now have a rule you can apply to a headline in under a minute.

And: can the sanction actually be collected, and does removal mean anything for this system? If the answer to the second half is no — if there is no shelf, nothing to recall, no premises located in the Union — then you are looking at a regime whose paradigm remedy is unavailable, whatever its penalty ceilings say.

We have built the whole instrument, from the gate to the fine.

One question has been held back since the first minutes of the first episode, and it is the only one left.

Was a product the right thing to regulate?
