## A list with nothing on it {tone: measured}

There is a document I am going to describe rather than read to you, because it has nothing in it to read.

When the European Union wants a technical standard to carry legal weight, it does not enact the standard. Somebody else writes that — a private association, in committee, over years. What the Union does is publish a reference to it in the Union's own gazette, the Official Journal. One line. The document's number, the body that produced it, and the law it serves.

Before that line exists, the standard is paperwork.

After it exists, a company that builds to the standard is presumed, in law, to have met the legal requirement.

For the Artificial Intelligence Act, the set of those lines is empty.

As of June 2026, not one deliverable from the joint committee writing Europe's artificial intelligence standards had been cited in the Official Journal for this Regulation. Not one. The most advanced of them is a standard about quality management systems. It is called EN 18286, it stands at approval stage, and it is the likeliest to be cited first.

June is the date I can stand behind. This was made in September 2026, and nothing I have found says the position has moved.

The regime those standards were written for applies from the second of December 2027.

{pause: 1.4}

An empty page. And a date on a calendar.

## The question, and why it waited {tone: lucid}

This is The Instrument.

For eleven episodes this series has taken the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act apart, one piece at a time, and asked of each piece what it can see and what it structurally cannot. Today it takes nothing apart. There is exactly one new article of law in this episode, it arrives at the end, and it is about the future.

In the first episode I made a promise, and I would rather keep it head-on than arrive at it sideways.

The promise was a question. Was regulating artificial intelligence as a product — a thing placed on a market, by somebody, for somebody else to use — the right choice? And the promise was that I would not answer until now.

That holding was deliberate, and the reason for it matters more than the suspense did. A verdict on machinery you have not seen is an opinion with music under it. You have seen the machinery now. All of it, from the test that decides whether any of this law is in the room with you, through to the question of whether anybody can collect a fine.

So the question can finally be put.

Was a product the right thing to regulate?

## The answer Europe gave, at its strongest {tone: measured}

Start with Europe's own answer, and give it its best form, because it is considerably better than its critics usually allow.

Product-safety machinery is the only apparatus Europe has that imposes duties *before* anyone is harmed. That is not a small thing, and no rival on the table could do it. Ordinary law arrives afterwards — through a court, brought by whoever was injured, about facts that have already happened. A product regime arrives at the factory door.

It also reaches everything. Not the systems somebody complained about. Every unit placed on the market, including all the ones nobody will ever notice.

And it runs on institutions that already exist. Market-surveillance authorities, notified bodies, decades of administrative practice across twenty-seven countries. Invent a new kind of regulator for artificial intelligence instead, and you spend ten years building it before it can do anything at all.

Then the argument I think is actually the strongest, and it gets made least often.

A product regime gives a firm a determinate answer to the only question a firm can act on before it ships. What, exactly, must I do? Read the list. Find yourself on it, or fail to. Build to the requirement. That is worth a great deal to an organisation that is genuinely trying to behave well, and rather more than any amount of principle.

This series has put evidence under all of that. The ladder does produce an answerable question: you can take a real system to it and come away with a classification. The requirements chapter is a serious engineering programme, whatever else it is — a provider that honestly runs it will end up knowing things about its own system that most organisations never learn about theirs. And when a technology nobody had drafted for arrived in public, a chapter governing it was written, agreed and in force inside two years.

Slow is a relative word.

## Two objections, and who made them {tone: precise}

Against that, two objections. They get muddled together constantly, and they are not the same objection.

The first is about who ends up writing the rule.

Michael Veale and Frederik Zuiderveen Borgesius put it in print in 2021, reading the draft, in *Computer Law Review International*. Their charge was that the Act leans on — and these are their words — "1980s product safety regulation" — end of quotation. And that it hands what are really fundamental-rights questions to, quoting again, "standardisation bodies with no fundamental rights experience". End of quotation.

Put plainly: the hard questions get settled by engineers in committee, writing a specification, and the law then treats the answer as sound.

The second objection is about what the instrument triggers on at all. That one belongs to the European Data Protection Board and the European Data Protection Supervisor, in their joint opinion of 18 June 2021 — the document this series has returned to more often than any other. Their complaint was that a fixed list of forbidden practices cannot anticipate what nobody has thought of yet, and that harms to groups and to society are invisible to an instrument built to assess risk to individuals. They also wanted biometric identification in public banned outright. They got a narrow prohibition with exceptions written into it.

And that second objection this series has already demonstrated, rather than reported.

Go back to the benefits risk model. Take nationality out of it — remove the variable completely — and leave postcode in. In a segregated housing market, postcode carries a great deal of the same information. The discriminatory effect barely moves. Now ask what the Act's classification does.

{pause: 1.4}

Not a millimetre. High-risk before, high-risk after, and for the same reason both times: what the system is for, and who runs it.

That is what it means for a harm to a group to be invisible to an instrument that assesses risk to an individual. The tier was not tracking the thing that mattered most, and it could not, because it was never built to look there. Stated in the abstract, that objection is a complaint. Worked through on a real model, it is the strongest evidence this series produced for the rights side of the argument.

Now, on the first of those two, the critics have been vindicated by events, and I am not going to soften it.

The prediction was made in 2021. By June 2026 no deliverable conferred the presumption. And when the two timetables collided — the law's, and the standards bodies' — the law is what moved. Anyone can check that, and checking it does not require an opinion about anything.

Two things in hand, then. Europe's defence of its own choice is real, and it is a defence about administrability. And one of the two standing objections to that choice has a date attached to it that the defence cannot match.

## What was actually regulated {tone: pointed}

There is a third way of describing this instrument, and it is less a rival answer than a better pair of glasses.

What the Act governs is neither a product nor a right. It is a market.

Look at the concepts doing the work. Placing on the market. Putting into service. Making available. Every duty in this Regulation switches on at one of those moments, and not one of them is a moment of harm — they are all moments of commerce. The cast is supply-chain roles: whoever puts a thing out under their own name, and whoever runs it under their own authority. And the sanctions are market sanctions.

So — a question, and take a second with it.

What can a product regime do that no fine can?

{pause: 1.4}

Take the product away.

That is the remedy that makes product safety frightening to a company, far more than money does. The batch comes off the shelf. The certificate is suspended. Your thing stops existing in the market you wanted to sell it in.

Hold on to that. In about four minutes it becomes the third fact in the verdict.

## Where I come down {tone: pointed}

So. Here is my answer, and it is mine, and it is narrower than you may want it to be.

On whether an instrument of this shape was the right kind of thing in principle, the record is not in, and I am not going to close a series by manufacturing a conclusion. What I can give you is a verdict on what this one has produced so far.

On the record available in September 2026, the product frame was a defensible choice that has not delivered the thing it was chosen for.

Slowly, because both halves carry weight.

*Defensible.* Given what Europe had, and given that it wanted to act before harm rather than after, choosing product safety was a reasonable thing to do, and I will not join anybody who calls it foolish.

*And it has not delivered.* The frame was chosen for administrability — duties fixed in advance, applied at scale, measured against a determinate specification. The specification is not there.

The engine of the high-risk regime is the presumption of conformity. And the presumption has nothing under it.

Three facts decide that, and all three were established earlier in this series rather than invented for the ending.

## The first fact {tone: measured}

The first you already have, because this episode opened on it.

As of June 2026, not one deliverable from the technical committee had been cited in the Official Journal. So the mechanism the whole high-risk regime runs on confers nothing. A presumption is a promise about a document, and it is never wider than the document underneath it — that limit is written into the Act's own sentence. With no document, the promise is about nothing.

That is the fact this verdict turns on, and it is the one that moves. If a standard is cited tomorrow, the position changes and you should want to know.

## The second fact, and a word to be careful with {tone: precise}

The second fact has a phrase attached that I would like to take away from you in its ordinary sense.

People say the law was delayed.

Delayed is what happens to a train. It arrives, later, unchanged.

What happened here was different in kind. The application dates were moved by a second Regulation — 2026/1744, in force from the twenty-seventh of July 2026, and the Act's only amendment to date. Sixteen months for the stand-alone high-risk systems. Twelve for the ones embedded in products Europe already regulates. A full legislative round, Parliament and Council, to change when a duty exists.

Postponement is the wrong word for that. When a duty begins is part of what the duty *is*. For sixteen months, across a very large class of systems, there is now no obligation where there would have been one. Nobody's paperwork ran late. The rule is different.

And note what the law was rescheduled around. A private association's drafting timetable.

So when you meet this in the wild — and you will, because every technical regime eventually does it — the question to ask is not whether a rule is late. It is whether the lateness of its technical substance has quietly rewritten the rule.

## The third fact {tone: grave}

The third fact is the one I think the frame cannot survive, and it is structural rather than contingent.

The frame's paradigm remedy does not exist for the systems this series examined.

The Act defines the two moves itself. Withdrawal is, in its words, "any measure aiming to prevent an AI system in the supply chain being made available on the market" — end of quotation. Recall is "any measure aiming to achieve the return to the provider or taking out of service or disabling the use of an AI system made available to deployers". End of quotation.

Those are the words of a regime built for objects.

Now try them on what we actually looked at.

A scraped database of faces has no supply chain to be kept out of. A risk model built inside a ministry and never sold to anybody was never on a market, so there is no market to withdraw it from. And a model served from a data centre in another country, through an interface, cannot be returned to its provider — it never left them.

{pause: 1.4}

You can fine all three. Whether anyone collects is a separate question, and this series has already watched a hundred and five million euros of adjacent fines produce no money whatsoever.

So here is what the frame assumes about the machine, in one sentence. It assumes a thing that can be fully specified before it meets the world, and taken back after.

Neither half holds for most of this subject matter.

## One step further, and this part is mine {tone: contemplative}

Now a step beyond that, marked plainly as my own, and further than the case I set out to make.

Everything so far says the frame is unfinished — that the infrastructure did not arrive in time, and that when it does, the frame may work. I have come to think the standards question is the *visible* half of a larger one.

Suppose the standards arrive. Suppose EN 18286 is cited next spring and three more follow it that year. Ask yourself who is then checking a high-risk system against them.

{pause: 1.4}

For nearly all of them: the provider. Its own assessment, its own file, inside its own building. The Act routes most stand-alone high-risk systems down a procedure it calls internal control, and its own text says of that route, quoting, "which does not provide for the involvement of a notified body". End of quotation.

Employment systems. Credit scoring. Access to benefits. Three of the places an ordinary person is most likely to meet one of these things, and in none of them does anybody outside the company look before it ships. Biometrics is the exception, and even there the outside route is largely the provider's to choose.

So a full shelf of cited standards would fix something real. It would give a self-assessment a method, and a document that somebody could argue about afterwards. It would not put a second pair of eyes on the file.

That claim is mine, and it needs a test of its own, because a verdict that cannot lose is worth nothing. Here is the test. The first time a market-surveillance authority opens an internal-control file for a high-risk system and reasons publicly about what it found, we will learn whether self-assessment against a specification produces the quality it names, or only the documentation of it.

## What would overturn this {tone: precise}

And what would overturn the main verdict? Without an answer to that, it is a mood rather than a judgement.

Two things, both checkable by anyone with a calendar.

A harmonised standard for the high-risk requirements, cited in the Official Journal, in time to be usable before the second of December 2027. And that date holding.

If both happen, the product frame will have been slow and it will have been workable, and the critics' strongest point will turn out to have been a point about a schedule. I would have been right about the delay and wrong about the shape, and I would take that outcome gladly.

And if the date moves a second time?

{pause: 1.4}

Then the structural claim stops being a prediction.

A deadline that slips once is a bad estimate. A deadline that slips twice, for the same reason, is an instrument telling you that the thing it depends on cannot be produced on the timescale it was designed around.

## What you now ask {tone: measured}

Three questions to carry out of all of this, and they work on any regime, not only this one.

The first. Who wrote the part of this rule that actually decides cases? You asked this one about a standards committee, and about who checks the person who checks. Almost never the legislature. Find the document that an engineer would open on a Tuesday, and find out who was in the room when it was drafted, and who paid for them to be there for four years.

{pause: 1.4}

The second. Does this instrument give the person harmed anything at all? You have had this question since the episode about an airline and one passenger, when it was about a single chatbot and a bereavement fare. It is the same question, asked of a whole instrument. Sometimes the answer is yes. Very often the regulator gets the instrument and the person gets a form, and that is a design decision somebody made deliberately, not an accident of drafting.

And the third, which is the one I would most like to leave you holding. What single checkable fact would tell me, in twelve months, whether this regime works?

For this Act, I have given you mine. A line in a gazette.

## The Act's own appointment {tone: lucid}

One article of new law, as promised, and then I am done.

Buried near the back of the Regulation is a provision almost nobody talks about, headed evaluation and review. Article 112, and that is the only citation you will get from me today.

The phrase sounds like a report nobody reads. In this Act it means something with teeth: a standing statutory obligation, with dates written into the text, attached to a power to propose amendment of the Regulation itself. The Commission does not decide whether to look. It is told when.

Every year, for as long as the delegated powers run, it must assess whether the high-risk list and the list of prohibited practices need changing, and report what it found to the Parliament and the Council.

By the second of August 2028, it must evaluate whether the supervision and governance system is effective — and separately, whether the AI Office was handed enough power to do the job it was given. That is an institution being asked, by the statute that created it, whether it turned out to be enough.

And by the second of August 2029, and every four years after that, a report on the evaluation and review of the whole Regulation — which, in the Act's own words, "shall include an assessment with regard to the structure of enforcement and the possible need for a Union agency to resolve any identified shortcomings". End of quotation. Where appropriate, and only where appropriate, that report comes with a proposal to amend the law attached.

There is more of Article 112 than that, including an assessment of enforcement due in 2031, and I am leaving it alone.

But hear what the 2029 report is. Europe wrote into its own artificial intelligence law a scheduled moment for asking whether the way it chose to enforce this thing was the right way — and whether a European agency ought to exist instead.

That question is not mine, carried in from outside. It is on the instrument's own calendar.

{pause: 1.4}

So. Two dates.

The second of December 2027, when the heaviest part of this law is supposed to begin, and when we find out whether anything was ever written for it to run on.

And the second of August 2029, when Europe has promised to tell itself whether it built the enforcement it needed.

I have given you my answer. Those two are when you get to check it.
