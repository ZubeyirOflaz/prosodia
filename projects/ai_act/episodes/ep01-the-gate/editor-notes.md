# Editor's notes on episode 1 — NOT marked ready

The writer/editor loop ended at its round limit rather than on a clean verdict, so the defects below are still in `transcript.md`.

## BLOCKING

**1. The provider/deployer allocation inside the third-country variant contradicts the variant itself — and it does so at the moment two terms of art are first introduced.**

Section "Change one fact". The variant is set up correctly:

> "Keep CAS exactly as it was — but this time the Dutch police did not build it. They bought it, from a company in California with no office and no representative anywhere in Europe."

Six sentences later, still inside that variant:

> "The Californian company would be the first. The Dutch police would be the second — and, because they built the thing themselves, they would be both at once."

In this variant they did *not* build it — the script has just said so. The clause silently jumps back to the real CAS with no signal. A listener tracking closely hears a flat contradiction; a listener who lapsed hears that a force which bought a system off a vendor is somehow also its provider, which is the exact misreading Episode 3 will have to undo. It also makes the later handover line — "The Dutch police were both at once" — land on the wrong case.

Fix, in place of the dash clause: *"The Californian company would be the first. The Dutch police would be the second, and only the second. In the real CAS they were both at once, because they built the thing and then ran it themselves."* That keeps the beat, keeps the preview of Episode 3, and stops the variant eating its own premise.

## IMPROVEMENTS

- **The levels beat runs four enumerated items.** "Ask why CAS failed and you can answer at four different heights" — technical, organisational, institutional, rights. It survives because the count is announced first, each level is a full sentence rather than a bare item, and the passage immediately consolidates ("Push the technical answer one level back and you get the organisational one"). But four is at the ceiling. Folding the organisational and institutional readings into one ("a closed box in a command room, inside a decade-long programme that never had to justify its own renewal against an outcome") gets you to three and loses nothing, since the payoff is only that the gate sees the technical one.
- **Two instances of the banned "not X, it's Y" sentence shape.** "That is not my judgment on it — it is the police's own stated reason for switching it off." And "That is not 'scrap the gate'. It is two European supervisors saying the drafting instinct was wrong in kind." Both are recoverable by inversion: *"The police said so themselves when they switched it off."* / *"Two European supervisors, saying the drafting instinct was wrong in kind."*
- **One three-beat crescendo, also on the series off-limits list.** "Different route, different actors, different paperwork. Same law." Cut to two beats: *"Different route, different paperwork. Same law."*
- **"Regression" is never given plain footing.** First use is inside the narrator's own objection — "This is a regression over three databases. We have had regressions since the nineteenth century" — and the force of the objection depends on the listener knowing what the word denotes. The plain description sits nearby ("a model fitted to years of recorded crime and population data") but is never attached to the word. Six words would do it: *"a regression — a curve fitted to past numbers, used to guess the next one."*
- **"General-purpose AI model" is quarantined but never glossed.** "Set aside that phrase — it is a separate category, and you do not need it today" is good permission-to-forget handling, but a listener who hears an undefined term inside a quotation carries it anyway. Add four words: *"a model that can do a great many different things — set it aside."*
- **Article 2(8) is spoken near-verbatim without audible brackets.** "But the drafters narrowed that hole themselves, in a sentence of their own, and it matters here. Testing in real-world conditions is not covered by the exclusion." The Act reads "Testing in real world conditions shall not be covered by that exclusion." Since you are effectively quoting, bracket it as you bracket everything else — the script's discipline on this is otherwise exact, and this is the one place it slips.
- **"Through the gate" is not distinguished from "regulated" until very late.** The correction arrives in "As written, against as enforced" ("Nobody assessed CAS under the AI Act. Not once."). A listener could hold the wrong idea for fifteen minutes. One sentence after "Through the gate. Comfortably." would fix it: *"Which does not mean anybody had to do anything about it. Being inside the law is not the same as being regulated by it."*
- **"And *only four* is the part worth keeping"** reads awkwardly aloud — "only four" is being used as a noun phrase. Try: *"That number is the part worth keeping, because a system that scores a square for four things is silent about everything else that happens there."*
- **Four "Which"-fragments** ("Which leaves the load-bearing word." / "Which sends you straight to a second definition." / "Which brings us to the beat this whole series exists for." / "Which is to say"). Idiomatic once or twice, a tic at four. Vary two.
- **A six-minute stretch with no pause and no question.** "The second gate" and "Which level are we standing on" run roughly 730 words — about five and a half minutes — with no `{pause}` and no retrieval question. The two genuine retrieval beats ("Did CAS infer?", "Does European law reach that company?") both land before it. A third question with a real pause after "Everything else in this instrument hangs off those two" would carry the listener across the middle.
- **"How likely was a burglary here. A car stolen from that street."** Two questions punctuated as statements, the second an unmarked fragment. The narrator has no way to read the second cleanly. Consider: *"How likely was a burglary this week? How likely was a car taken from that street?"*
- **Length.** By my count the script runs a little under 5,000 spoken words — roughly 38 minutes at 130 words per minute, against a 34-minute guide. That is a guide and the material earns most of it, but if it needs to come down, the two places with slack are the levels beat (see above) and the second half of "Where this is contested", where the Veale and Zuiderveen Borgesius paragraph makes its point twice.
