## A committee suspends its own procedure {tone: measured}

Two private associations agreed to stop using their own procedure.

October 2025. They are called CEN and CENELEC. Between them they write a great many of the technical standards that European products are built to. And the procedure they set aside is, in a real sense, the thing they exist for. A committee drafts a text. The national standards bodies of the member countries are asked what they think. There is an enquiry stage, and then a formal vote. The point of all of it is that by the end, everyone who had to be asked has been asked.

That is what a standard is. A standard is an agreement, and the procedure is how the agreement gets made.

What they adopted instead were called exceptional measures. Fewer publication stages. Smaller drafting groups.

Technical committee members warned publicly that this could undermine consensus. The phrase that comes down to us is a report, not any named person's words: "serious unintended consequences."

Now the reason. The reason is the thing to carry with you.

The European Union had passed a law whose central mechanism could not run at all until certain of these documents existed. The law named a date. The second of August, 2026. The documents were not going to be ready.

So the standards bodies suspended their consensus procedure to try to catch the statute.

They did not catch it.

{pause: 1.4}

The statute moved instead.

## Where we are {tone: lucid}

This is *The Instrument*, a series about the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act — and about a decision the Union took before it wrote a word of that Act, which was to regulate artificial intelligence the way it regulates lifts, toys and pressure vessels. As a product placed on a market.

You already have the ladder: which systems the Act calls high-risk, and the two different doors they come in through. You have the requirements that land on a system once it is up there, risk management and human oversight among them. And you have the argument about whether a product frame can carry a question about somebody's rights at all.

What today adds is the machinery in between. The apparatus that is supposed to turn a requirement written in a statute into a test that a real engineer can actually run on a Tuesday — who runs it, and who checks the person who ran it. And what happens when the document the whole arrangement depends on has not been written yet.

That last one is not a thought experiment. By the end of this you will have it dated.

## Two pictures to get rid of {tone: precise}

Before anything else, two things you probably believe, and both of them will make the rest of this unhearable.

The first is that a standard is a kind of rule. It is not. A standard is a document written by a private association, and on its own it obliges nobody. You can ignore every standard in Europe and break no law. What a standard does is different, and stranger, and we will get to it.

The second is that conformity assessment is an inspection. Somebody comes, they look at your thing, they sign something. That is not what the phrase means either.

The Act defines it in Article 3, and the definition is thin on purpose. Beginning of its words. Conformity assessment means the process of demonstrating whether the requirements set out in Chapter III, Section 2 relating to a high-risk AI system have been fulfilled. End of its words.

Sit with the verb. *Demonstrating.* Not verifying. Not testing. Demonstrating — which is something a person does about their own work, in front of whoever is watching, if anyone is.

And for most of the stand-alone systems on the high-risk list, as you will hear, nobody is.

## What "harmonised" actually means {tone: precise}

So here is the word the entire episode turns on, and I would rather over-teach it than have you lose it.

**Harmonised standard.**

Two wrong meanings are available. One: it is an EU rule. It is not — nobody in Brussels wrote it. Two: it is a standard that several countries agreed on, the way an international standard is. Also no, and this is the one that catches technically-minded people.

A harmonised standard is a standard whose *reference has been published in the Official Journal of the European Union*.

That is what makes the difference. Not the quality of the document, not who drafted it, not how widely it was adopted, but whether a citation to it has appeared in the Union's own gazette. The Commission looks at the finished standard, decides, and publishes — or does not.

Until that citation exists, the document confers nothing.

{pause: 1.4}

Same words, same engineering, same committee — and one line in a journal turns it into law.

## The chain, end to end {tone: lucid}

I will walk you along it, because the shape matters more than any one link.

It starts with the Commission, which issues a **standardisation request** to the European standards organisations. That request is made under a separate piece of law, Regulation 1025 of 2012, and the Act's own Article 40 tells the Commission what to put in it: that the standards have to be clear, that they have to be consistent with the standards already used in the regulated product sectors, and that the organisations must give evidence of their best efforts. Best efforts. Notice that a legislature asking a private body to do something has to ask for evidence that it tried.

Then the drafting. A technical committee — for artificial intelligence, that is a joint committee of CEN and CENELEC known as JTC 21 — writes a text. The national bodies comment. Enquiry. Formal vote. Out comes a European standard, an EN.

Then the Commission assesses what came out, and if it is satisfied, cites the reference in the Official Journal.

And only then does anything happen in law.

What happens is the presumption.

## The words that do the work {tone: quoting, rate: slow}

Article 40, paragraph 1. I am going to read the part that governs high-risk systems and skip the parts about general-purpose models, which are not today's business. Beginning of the Act's words.

"High-risk AI systems which are in conformity with harmonised standards, or parts thereof, the references of which have been published in the Official Journal of the European Union in accordance with Regulation (EU) No 1025/2012, shall be presumed to be in conformity with the requirements set out in Section 2 of this Chapter, to the extent that those standards cover those requirements."

{pause: 1.4}

End of the Act's words. From here I will stop saying the number and call it **the presumption**.

Now the plain version, because that sentence has three hinges in it and you were only ever going to catch two.

If you build your system to a standard whose reference is in the Official Journal, the law *assumes* you have met the legal requirement. Not that you have proved it. That you are assumed to have met it, until somebody shows otherwise.

And there is a limit welded to it, in the last clause. **To the extent that those standards cover those requirements.** A presumption is never wider than the document underneath it. If the document covers half the requirement, you are presumed compliant with half.

Hold that limit. I am going to spend the last third of this episode on it.

## Why this bargain, and what it cost {tone: contemplative}

So why would anybody build a law this way? It looks, said quickly, like a legislature outsourcing its job.

It is older and better-thought-through than that.

The problem it answers is real. A parliament can say a machine must be safe. A parliament cannot say at what temperature the insulation on a particular class of cable must stop deforming, and it certainly cannot keep that number current for forty years. If you write the number into the statute, the statute is obsolete the moment the materials improve, and you have to reopen a legislature to fix a cable.

So Europe struck a bargain, decades before anyone in this story was thinking about artificial intelligence. The legislature sets what are called essential requirements — safe, healthy, does not catch fire. Private experts, in standards bodies, write the specifications that would satisfy those requirements. And conformity to a specification buys you a presumption that you have met the law.

It is genuinely elegant. It scales — it reaches every unit placed on the market, not just the ones somebody complained about. It updates faster than any parliament could. And it puts the drafting where the expertise actually is, which is in industry, because that is where the people who build the things are.

What was traded away was the substance.

Because on this arrangement, the law is a sentence long and the specification runs to hundreds of pages, and those pages are where a real engineer finds out what to do. Whoever writes them is, functionally, writing the rule.

For a cable, most people can live with that. The question this Act asks is whether you can live with it when the requirement is not about temperature.

## The objection, and the answer it got {tone: pointed}

You have already met the objection in this series, so I will not rebuild it — only carry it forward to where it now lands.

In 2021, on the draft, Michael Veale and Frederik Zuiderveen Borgesius wrote that this Act takes its structure from four decades of European product-safety law, and that doing so hands what are really fundamental-rights questions to — their words — "standardisation bodies with no fundamental rights experience." That is their sentence, not mine.

It is a prediction. It is dated, it is attributable, and it is checkable.

Now watch what the Act does about it, because the Act saw the objection coming.

Article 67 sets up an advisory forum, and gives it a set of permanent members. There are five of them. Two are CEN and CENELEC, the bodies we started with. One is the European Union Agency for Fundamental Rights.

So the standards associations sit permanently beside the rights agency, in the same room, on the same body.

{pause: 1.4}

That is a real institutional answer. It is also a seating plan. It puts the rights agency in the room where advice is given to the Commission. It does not put it in the room where the specification is drafted, and it gives it no veto over what comes out.

The Act saw the objection, and answered it with who sits next to whom.

## Who checks — and the two words you must not merge {tone: precise}

Now to the part of the machinery that decides everything, and to a naming problem the Act has handed us.

There are two institutions here, and their names sound almost the same.

A **notifying authority** is a public body. Each Member State designates at least one. Its job is to assess, designate and monitor the organisations that do conformity assessment.

A **notified body** is one of those organisations. Usually a private laboratory or certification house — though not always, as you'll hear. Its job is to actually perform the assessment.

Notifying authority. Notified body. In your ear those are nearly one word, and if you merge them the central question of this episode — who checks the checker — becomes literally unhearable.

So I am going to stop using them. From here, the public body that hands out the badge is **the licensor**. The body that wears it is **the examiner**. The licensor appoints. The examiner inspects.

{pause: 1.4}

Now. The Act spends eleven articles on the examiners — how they are appointed, what they must prove, and what happens when somebody challenges their competence. Eleven articles. I am treating all of it as one institution rather than eleven rules, and I am telling you that I am doing it, because it is a real compression and you should know it happened. The shape is what you need: there is a public gatekeeper, and behind the gate there are examiners with numbers.

Three more in this part of the chapter I am naming and not teaching — Articles 42, 45 and 46. Article 42 earns one sentence, because it is a second presumption and a far narrower one: a system trained and tested on data that reflects the setting it is actually going to be used in is presumed to comply with one paragraph of the data-governance requirement. One paragraph. That is the whole of it, and it is a good miniature of everything that follows.

And all that apparatus has a hole in it.

For most stand-alone high-risk AI systems, it does not apply.

## The default is that nobody outside looks {tone: pointed}

Article 43 decides who checks what, and it does it by pointing at the list of high-risk uses — Annex Three — and splitting it. From here I will call it **the allocation rule**.

Points two through eight. That is seven areas of life, and between them they cover nearly everything the Act calls high-risk apart from biometrics. The two you are likeliest to meet: the systems that decide who gets a job, and the systems that decide who gets credit.

For all of it, the provider follows a route the Act calls **internal control**, set out in Annex Six. The Act adds a clause describing that route, and the clause is the whole point. Quote: "which does not provide for the involvement of a notified body." End quote.

In the terms we are using: no examiner. At all.

Internal control is not internal audit. It is not a second team inside the company checking the first. It is the route in which the party demonstrating conformity and the party whose conformity is in question are the same party, and the paperwork stays in the building.

Point one of the list — biometrics — is the exception, and even there the third-party route is an option the provider may choose, not a requirement, so long as harmonised standards existed and the provider applied them.

And that condition is going to matter enormously, shortly.

Now, the other door. A system that reaches the market inside a product that European law already regulates — a medical device, a machine — does not follow this split at all. It follows the conformity assessment its own product law already demands, with the AI requirements folded into that assessment. And the certificates run to different lengths: up to five years on the embedded route, four on the stand-alone list.

So — the system that scored your loan application. Who signed off on it?

{pause: 1.4}

The people who built it.

Now, a car.

## The machinery under load {tone: grave}

You have the car already. A manufacturer fitted software that could recognise the conditions of the type-approval test, and ran full emissions control while it was being watched.

What happened afterwards is the part this episode needs.

The Court of Justice held such devices unlawful. Case C-128/20 and two others decided with it, on the fourteenth of July, 2022, on questions referred up from the Austrian Supreme Court and from the regional courts of Eisenstadt and Klagenfurt. The manufacturers' defence was that the software protected the engine. The Court rejected it. What was actually happening was a thermal window: full exhaust-gas recirculation between fifteen and thirty-three degrees, and below a thousand metres — which is, near enough, the conditions of the test — and outside that band the nitrogen oxides went past the limits.

The European Parliament ran its own inquiry, the EMIS committee, and reported in 2017.

And then.

{pause: 1.4}

Member States applied neither financial nor legal penalties to the manufacturers. No mandatory recalls. No type approvals withdrawn. Volkswagen maintained throughout that it had never breached EU rules.

The framework was eventually revised, and the revision tells you what the drafters concluded the failure had been. Member States were required to run a minimum number of independent tests each year. The fix for a test that could be recognised was: somebody else should also test, sometimes, unannounced.

Now the transfer, and this is the claim I most want to survive this episode.

The product was built to pass the assessment rather than to meet the standard.

Nothing was falsified and nothing was hidden. The car was optimised against a known, published, fixed evaluation. And an AI system tuned to perform on the benchmark used for its conformity assessment, and differently once it is deployed, is the identical failure with different physics.

I am not importing that worry from outside: it is what the machinery this Act borrowed did, under load, when someone had a strong incentive to game it.

## The other half, from the air {tone: measured}

And the aviation case you already have from this series gives you the second half of the problem, so I will take one beat and move.

The American regulator's Organization Designation Authorization programme lets a manufacturer perform certification functions on the regulator's behalf. The inspector general's report found that the regulator could not properly assess risk inside that arrangement — that the independence of the manufacturer's own certification personnel was not ensured, and that the agency's engineers stationed at the manufacturer were balancing certification work against their oversight duties.

Delegated assessment, and a regulator that could not see through the delegation.

The AI Act's position is harder than that, not easier. In the aviation case there was at least a delegation. There was a programme, there were designated persons, there was an agency with a duty to oversee them, and there was an inspector general able to find that the oversight had failed.

Under internal control there is no delegation, because there is nobody to delegate to. The assessment simply happens inside the provider, and the first outside body to see any of it is a market-surveillance authority, afterwards, if it asks.

So the honest question the aviation report lets us put is this: what does a regulator need in order to check work it has handed away? And does any AI market-surveillance authority in Europe currently have it?

I do not think anyone knows. I think that is the accurate answer and not a dodge.

## Change one fact {tone: precise}

Three variations. Same system, one detail moved each time. This is where you hear where the rule's edges are.

**One.** A credit-scoring system. That is point five of the high-risk list — essential services. Internal control, no examiner, as we said. But now add the fact this episode opened on: there is no harmonised standard for it. None has been cited. So what is the provider assessing itself *against*?

Against the requirements themselves. Directly. The provider reads Articles Eight through Fifteen — accuracy, robustness, data governance and the rest — and demonstrates, in its own documentation, that it has met them.

Nobody knows what that looks like. I am not being rhetorical. There is no agreed method, no cited document, and no examiner who would have to accept or reject the answer.

**Two.** Take that same system and move it to the top of the list. Make it a biometric system — point one — and put it in the hands of an immigration authority.

Everything changes.

Because for point one of the list, the provider only gets to choose internal control *where harmonised standards existed and were applied*. Where they do not exist — and where the Commission has not written a replacement, which is a thing it can do under Article 41 — the allocation rule sends the provider to the third-party route, and the word in the Act is "shall". An examiner becomes compulsory.

And then a further sentence, which is the one I would most like you to leave with. Where such a system is intended for law enforcement, or immigration, or asylum, or a Union institution — quote — "the market surveillance authority referred to in Article 74(8) or (9), as applicable, shall act as a notified body." End quote.

Listen to the middle of that sentence, because the middle is the point — the part it would be easy to skip past. The Act does not name the authority. It sends you to another article to find out which one. And on the reading I have, the authority you land on for a law-enforcement system is the Member State's data protection authority — so the body that examines the system is the same body that guards the right the system might interfere with. I will say plainly that I am taking that from following the cross-reference, not from reading Article 74 to you, and I have not read its words to you today.

But hold the two cases side by side. A system deciding your creditworthiness: assessed by its maker, against nothing written. The same technology pointed at a border: mandatory third-party assessment, by a public authority.

One entry on one list, and everything about who checks it changes.

**Three.** Now suppose the machinery starts working. Suppose a standard is finished and cited — and the likeliest first candidate is a standard about quality management systems, called EN 18286.

Every provider that implements it gets a presumption. Real legal effect, immediately.

So — a question for you, and I mean it as a question.

That provider is now presumed to comply with *what*?

{pause: 1.4}

With quality management. And with nothing else.

A standard about how you organise your processes is not a standard about whether your system is accurate, or robust, or safe for the people it is pointed at. To the extent that those standards cover those requirements. The presumption follows the document, and only as far as the document goes.

## The level, and the level under it {tone: contemplative}

Let me stop and say where we are, because a lot has gone past.

Three things established. The law's engine for high-risk systems is a presumption, and the presumption runs on a document being cited in the Official Journal. For most stand-alone high-risk systems, the party that assesses is the party being assessed. And what a presumption covers is bounded by what the document covers.

Now one push, briefly, and then I stop, because this move is a licence to digress and an episode that never lands has used it once too often.

The obvious level at which to describe this crisis is the **technical** one. A specification document is late.

Push back one. Why is it late? Because of how it is being written — by consensus, across national bodies, which is slow, and which is why the acceleration cut stages and shrank the drafting groups. That is an **organisational** answer, and it is better.

Push back again. Who is in those groups? Participation in standards work costs money and time. Somebody has to fund a person to attend for years. That selects who is in the room, systematically, and it does not select for civil society. That is an **economic** answer, and it is better still.

And underneath all of it: the substance of a public regulation, on rights, is sitting inside a private association's committee procedure. That is an **institutional** fact, and it was a deliberate choice, made decades ago, for good reasons, about cables.

I will stop there. But notice that each level was a convenient way of describing everything upstream of it, and none of them was the cause.

## What the rule assumes about the machine {tone: contemplative}

Now the beat this whole series exists for.

The presumption makes an assumption, and the assumption is not about law. It is about machines.

It assumes that compliance can be established by testing a thing against a written specification, and that passing the specification is a good proxy for meeting the requirement.

For a lift, that assumption is close to perfect. The load it must carry does not change while it is carrying it. The building does not quietly become a different building. You specify, you test, and the test result stays true.

For a system whose performance depends on the distribution of data it meets after deployment, the assumption holds badly. The population changes. The way people use it changes. The thing it is measuring changes because it is being measured. You can pass every test in the document on Monday and be a different system by the following spring, having been modified by nobody.

This is a live problem in the literature, not a rhetorical one — there is a paper with the title "Lost in Vagueness: Towards Context-Sensitive Standards for Robustness Assessment under the EU AI Act", and the existence of that title tells you where the difficulty is thought to be.

And now put it beside the car.

Dieselgate showed that a fixed test can be gamed by someone who wants to game it. The AI case is worse, and worse in a specific way. Here the deployment conditions move on their own. Nobody has to cheat for the test to stop describing the system.

## Where it stands, and when {tone: measured, note: "date-stamped throughout; this is the contingent material"}

Now the dates, and everything in this beat is contingent. It moves. I am telling you where it stood when this was made.

The first deadline for the standards was April 2025. It was missed.

October 2025 was the acceleration — the exceptional measures this episode opened on.

November 2025, the Commission tabled the package that became Regulation 2026/1744, the Act's only amendment to date.

And the high-risk dates moved. Stand-alone high-risk systems now apply from the second of December, 2027. High-risk systems embedded in already-regulated products, from the second of August, 2028.

That is the hinge from the opening, resolved. The standards bodies suspended consensus to catch the statute, and the statute moved anyway.

And now the fact this series will eventually be judged on.

As of June 2026, not one deliverable from JTC 21 grants a presumption of conformity — because none has been cited in the Official Journal.

{pause: 1.4}

Not one. The most advanced is EN 18286, at Approval stage, and it is the likeliest to be cited first.

**Status: still moving.** It may have changed by the time you hear this, and if it has, that is the sentence to check.

## What is argued, and by whom {tone: measured}

Three disagreements, honestly labelled.

Can conformity assessment against a technical standard discharge a duty about somebody's fundamental rights? **Contested** — and no longer only in theory. The 2021 prediction has an empirical record now, and the record is that the documents did not arrive.

Was the acceleration a pragmatic rescue or a legitimacy failure? **Contested.** Marta Cantero Gamito wrote the sharpest short piece on it, in November 2025, and its title carries the argument: "From Consensus to Exceptionality — What the EU's AI Standards Crisis Reveals About Delegated Technical Governance." I am reading her title and not her text, and I will not put words in her mouth beyond it. But *from consensus to exceptionality* is a claim, and it is that the emergency procedure became the procedure.

Third. The Commission has a fallback, and it has not been forced to use it yet.

Article 41 lets the Commission write its own technical rules. They are called **common specifications**, and the name undersells them badly. A common specification is not a longer, more detailed version of the same thing. It is the Commission taking the substance back off a private association and putting it into Union law, where a committee of national representatives has to sign off. And conformity with one carries the same presumption a cited standard does.

There are four triggers for it. One is simply that the standards do not exist. The other one that matters here is in the Act's own words, and it is remarkable: the Commission may act where the relevant harmonised standards — quote — "insufficiently address fundamental rights concerns." End quote.

That clause is the 2021 objection, written into the statute as a trigger. The drafters anticipated that standards bodies might not handle rights well, and gave the Commission a lever for exactly that case.

Whether it will ever be pulled is, to me, the most consequential open question in this entire apparatus. I have found no record of the Commission adopting one, and if it has, that is the second sentence in this episode to check. **Still moving.**

## The paper at the end {tone: precise}

One more link to close, because you should know what the machinery actually produces.

Where an examiner is involved, it issues a certificate, valid for a fixed term — up to five years on the embedded product route, four on the stand-alone list, renewable on re-assessment.

Then the provider draws up an **EU declaration of conformity**. Hear what that is. It is not a certificate issued to you by anyone. It is a document you write about yourself, stating that your system meets the requirements, which you keep available to national authorities for ten years. By drawing it up, the Act says, the provider assumes responsibility.

Then the **CE marking**. And now the example everybody expects me to reach for. I am going to name it and refuse it: the CE mark is not a badge meaning the European Union tested this. The Act's own definition, beginning of its words — the CE marking means a marking by which a provider indicates that an AI system is in conformity with the requirements set out in Chapter III, Section 2 and other applicable Union harmonisation legislation providing for its affixing. End of its words.

The provider affixes it. For a system supplied digitally, there is now a digital CE marking, reachable through the interface. Only where an examiner was involved does that mark get followed by the examiner's identification number — which means, for most stand-alone high-risk AI systems, a CE mark with no number after it is the system telling you that nobody outside checked.

And then **registration**, in an EU database. Worth one correction: this is not a public register of AI systems in general. It is high-risk systems. And for three categories of them — biometrics, law enforcement, and migration — the entries go into a secure non-public section, reachable only by the Commission and by designated authorities.

The three areas where an outsider would most want to look are the three where the register is closed.

I am not covering the procedural escalation that follows any of this — what happens when an authority thinks a compliant system is still dangerous. That is real, it runs to several articles, and you do not need it today.

## Where I come down {tone: pointed}

A local judgement, and I will mark it as mine.

I do not think the bargain was foolish. Given what Europe had — an internal market, a working product-safety apparatus, and an obligation to act before harm rather than after it — delegating specification to standards bodies was a defensible thing to do. It is the only European machinery that reaches every unit placed on the market.

But a presumption is a promise about a document. And as of June 2026, for the high-risk regime, that document is not there. The citation list is empty. June is the date I can stand behind; this is being made in September 2026, three months later, and nothing I have seen says it has changed.

A promise about a document, and no document.

That is arithmetic about the Official Journal, not prediction, and anyone can check it.

## What you now ask {tone: lucid}

So, what do you take to the next system you meet — and I mean any system, in any regime, not this one.

Ask who checks it, and whether they are the people who built it. That question alone will separate most real assurance from most paperwork.

Ask what document the checker is checking against, and whether anyone has published it. If the answer is that the method is proprietary or still being developed, you have your answer about what the certificate means. And if there is a document, ask what it leaves uncovered, because presumptions are exactly as wide as the paper beneath them, never wider.

Ask whether this thing could be built to pass the test rather than to meet the requirement — and if you cannot see how it could not be, assume someone eventually will.

And ask the last one, which this episode was really about. If the specification is late, what moves? Does the obligation move, or does the deadline?

{pause: 1.4}

In this case, twice now, it was the deadline.

## Handover {tone: measured}

Everything today assumed a system built for a purpose. Somebody intended it to do a thing, and the whole apparatus — the classification, the mark — hangs off that intention.

The next chapter concerns models built for no particular purpose at all.

And this machinery has no idea what to do with them.
