## The foundational critique: it is product-safety law

**Michael Veale and Frederik Zuiderveen Borgesius**, *"Demystifying the Draft EU Artificial
Intelligence Act — Analysing the good, the bad, and the unclear elements of the proposed
approach"*, **Computer Law Review International 22(4) (2021) 97–112**; open preprint arXiv
2107.03721; also in the UvA, Radboud and UCL repositories.

Their argument, on the 2021 draft: the Act draws its structure from **EU product-safety regimes of
the last four decades** rather than from fundamental-rights law, and inherits that machinery's
assumptions — conformity assessment, CE marking, harmonised standards, presumption of conformity.
Two consequences they name: reliance on **"1980s product safety regulation"**, and the delegation
of what are effectively fundamental-rights questions to **"standardisation bodies with no
fundamental rights experience."**

**Status: contested but foundational.** Dated on specifics (it addresses the 2021 draft, not the
adopted Act or the 2026 amendments), and still the sharpest framing available. Any episode that
asks "why this rule and not another" is arguing with or from this paper.

**Teaching note.** This is the cleanest available instance of the persona's "why this rule and not
another" beat: a fundamental-rights instrument was a live alternative, and a product-safety
instrument is what was built. What was traded away is the question.

## The standardisation deficit — and its real-time vindication

This is the strongest single episode candidate in the docket, because a 2021 prediction can be
checked against what actually happened.

**The prediction (2021).** Harmonised standards would carry the substance of the high-risk regime,
and they would be written by bodies without fundamental-rights competence.

**What happened (2025–2026), established from secondary sources:**

- The standards were **not going to be ready** for the 2 August 2026 high-risk date.
- **October 2025:** CEN and CENELEC adopted **exceptional measures** to accelerate AI standards —
  reducing publication stages and using smaller drafting groups.
- **Technical committee members publicly warned** this could undermine consensus and produce
  "serious unintended consequences."
- **November 2025:** the Commission tabled the Digital Omnibus; the high-risk deadline was
  ultimately moved to **2 December 2027**.

So: the law was delayed because the standards were late, and the standards process suspended its
own consensus norms to try to catch up. **The substance of a fundamental-rights regime was on a
timetable set by a private standards body.**

**Scholarship to pull from (titles located; not yet read):**
- Marta Cantero Gamito, *"From Consensus to Exceptionality — What the EU's AI Standards Crisis
  Reveals About Delegated Technical Governance"*, REALaw blog, 28 November 2025.
- *"Standards and the EU AI Act: legitimacy, state of play, and future challenges"*, Information &
  Communications Technology Law (Taylor & Francis, 2025).
- *"European AI Standards — Technical Standardisation and Implementation Challenges under the EU AI
  Act"*, European Journal of Risk Regulation (Cambridge).
- *"Lost in Vagueness: Towards Context-Sensitive Standards for Robustness Assessment under the EU
  AI Act"*, arXiv 2511.15620.

**Where it stands, verified 2026-09-12 — and this is the series' verdict question in one fact:**
**as of June 2026 not one JTC 21 deliverable grants presumption of conformity, because none has been
cited in the Official Journal.** A standard confers the presumption only once finalised, harmonised
and cited there. **EN 18286** — a quality management system for AI Act purposes — is the most
advanced, at Approval stage, and the likeliest to be cited first. The original April 2025 deadline
was missed; the October 2025 acceleration was the response.

So the high-risk regime's central mechanism had, at the point the regime was due to apply, **no
standards capable of conferring the presumption it runs on**. That is why the deadline moved to
December 2027, and whether it moves again is the question the final episode has to answer.

**Status: still moving.** Say so, and date it.

## Contested joints worth an episode each

| Question | Why it is contested | Status |

| Is a risk-tier model the right shape at all, against a rights-based or a per-sector one? | Tiers fix the risk at design time; harm is contextual and shows up in deployment | contested |

## Where the docket has NO position

The persona forbids manufacturing balance. Where the weight of informed opinion genuinely leans,
say so. Where it does not — and the risk-tier design question is one of those — say that instead of
inventing a consensus.
