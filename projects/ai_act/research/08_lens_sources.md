## Position 1 — The Collingridge dilemma

**David Collingridge, *The Social Control of Technology*** (London: Frances Pinter, 1980; New York:
St Martin's Press, 1980). *Verified: publisher, both imprints, year.*

**The canonical formulation**, as it is quoted across the literature:

> "When change is easy, the need for it cannot be foreseen; when the need for change is apparent,
> change has become expensive, difficult and time consuming."

⚠ **Confidence: the wording is stable across many secondary citations; the page is not established
here.** Usually given as p. 11. Either confirm the page against the book, or have the script
paraphrase and attribute rather than quote. Do not read a page number aloud that nobody checked.

**The argument.** Controlling a technology has two failure modes and no third option. Early, while
it is still steerable, you do not know enough about its consequences to know what to steer away
from. Late, when the consequences are legible, the technology is entrenched and control is slow and
costly.

**Where it lands in the Act.** If you believe this, you build in the ability to change the
instrument after you have learned something: **Art. 7** (power to amend Annex III), **Art. 97**
(delegated acts), **Art. 112** (evaluation and review). Those three articles are a Collingridge
answer whether or not anyone drafting them said the name.

**What it is a response to.** The post-hoc regulation of chemicals, nuclear power and asbestos —
regimes that arrived after the harm was demonstrated and the industry established.

## Position 2 — The pacing problem

Two holders, and they are not saying quite the same thing.

**Larry Downes, *The Laws of Disruption: Harnessing the New Forces That Govern Life and Business in
the Digital Age*** (New York: Basic Books, 2009). *Verified: title, publisher, year.* The "law of
disruption" is Downes's: **"technology changes exponentially, but social, economic, and legal
systems change incrementally."** He coined the phrase earlier, in *Unleashing the Killer App*
(1998), and builds it explicitly on Schumpeter's creative destruction (1942) and Kuhn (1962).
*Confidence: high on the formulation, which is quoted verbatim in reviews of the book; ⚠ confirm the
page before quoting it as his words.*

**Gary E. Marchant, Braden R. Allenby and Joseph R. Herkert (eds), *The Growing Gap Between
Emerging Technologies and Legal-Ethical Oversight: The Pacing Problem***, The International Library
of Ethics, Law and Technology vol. 7 (Dordrecht: Springer, 2011), DOI `10.1007/978-94-007-1356-7`.
*Verified: exact title, all three editors, series, volume number, publisher, year, DOI. The plan's
earlier bracketed note asking to verify the title and date is now closed.*

**The argument.** Technology moves on one clock and institutions on another, and the gap between
them widens rather than closing. The policy implication is the opposite of Collingridge's: prefer
**principles and regulators with discretion** over rules with fixed thresholds, because a threshold
is obsolete the moment the technology moves past it.

**The tension to make audible.** Collingridge says regulate early and expect to be wrong;
the pacing problem says do not write anything a technology can outrun. The AI Act does both at
once — a fixed compute threshold in Art. 51(2) *and* a delegated power to move it in Art. 51(3).

## Position 3 — Regulation by architecture

**Lawrence Lessig, *Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace*** (New York: Basic Books, 1999), revised as
***Code: Version 2.0*** (New York: Basic Books, 2006) — the source of "code is law", and the second
edition is freely available, having been produced through a reader-edited wiki. *Verified: both
editions, publisher, years.*

**Joel R. Reidenberg, "Lex Informatica: The Formulation of Information Policy Rules Through
Technology", *Texas Law Review* 76(3) (February 1998), pp. 553–593.** *Verified: journal, volume,
issue, month, year, page range.* Reidenberg's term precedes Lessig's book by a year and makes the
same structural point: technical capability sets rules that behave like law.

**The argument.** What actually constrains behaviour is the built environment, not the rule about
it. A regulator who wants effect should therefore specify the architecture rather than the conduct.

**Where it lands in the Act.** Logging obligations (**Art. 12**), machine-readable marking of
synthetic content (**Art. 50**), technical documentation (**Art. 11** and Annex IV). The Act is full
of Lessig in places where it never uses the word.

**What it is a response to.** The 1990s belief that the internet was inherently unregulable.

## Position 4 — The refusal, and it has a famous holder

This position was in the plan with **no holder at all**. It has one, and it is the best-known
statement of the view in the literature.

**Frank H. Easterbrook, "Cyberspace and the Law of the Horse", *University of Chicago Legal Forum*
vol. 1996, art. 7, p. 207.** *Verified: journal, volume, article number, page; freely available at
`chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/uclf/vol1996/iss1/7/`.*

**The argument.** There is no more a coherent "law of cyberspace" than a "law of the horse". You do
not improve legal understanding by collecting every case that happens to involve a horse; you study
property, tort and contract, and apply them. A technology-specific statute names the technology,
dates badly, and entrenches whoever was incumbent when it was written. Regulate outcomes and harms
through the ordinary law you already have.

**Its ablest answer, and it is by name:** **Lawrence Lessig, "The Law of the Horse: What Cyberlaw
Might Teach", *Harvard Law Review* 113 (1999), p. 501.** *Verified: journal, volume, year, first
page; freely available at `cyber.harvard.edu/works/lessig/finalhls.pdf`.* Lessig answers Easterbrook
directly: studying cyberspace teaches something general precisely because it makes the regulating
force of architecture visible. **Give this exchange as an exchange** — it is a real, dated,
published disagreement between two named people, which is exactly what the lens spine asks a lens
episode to open on.

**Consequence of adopting position 4:** no gate, no tiers, no CE mark, and no duty on anything
before it hurts someone.

## The contested joint — is the pacing problem real, or a move?

The plan flags this as "contested, and the critique is serious: 'it moves too fast to regulate' is
also the industry's preferred position." Both sides now have names.

**The critique, with a holder:** **William Aspray and Philip Doty, "Does technology really outpace
policy, and does it matter? A primer for technical experts and others", *Journal of the Association
for Information Science and Technology* 74(8) (2023), pp. 885–904, DOI `10.1002/asi.24762`.**
*Verified: authors, exact title, journal, volume, issue, year, page range, DOI.* The paper
reconsiders **the outpacing argument** directly — it sets out the arguments that support it and
weighs whether the belief survives examination, rather than assuming it. This is the citation to
use when the episode says the framing is contested.

**The ablest defender of the other side, named:** **Adam Thierer** (Mercatus Center / Technology
Liberation Front), who has written on the pacing problem, the Collingridge dilemma and
"permissionless innovation", including *"Soft Law for Hard Problems: The Governance of Emerging
Technologies"*, **Colorado Technology Law Journal** (2019), and the Mercatus commentary "The Pacing
Problem and the Future of Technology Regulation". *Verified: author, journal, approximate year;
⚠ confirm the CTLJ volume and page before citing it precisely.*

**Say the awkward part out loud.** The pacing problem is a serious analytic claim **and** the
deregulatory position's favourite argument, and the same sentence does both jobs. The episode is
more honest, not less, for naming Thierer as someone who makes the argument in good faith and
Aspray and Doty as people who think the argument does more work than its evidence supports.

## The opening anchor — two documents, three weeks apart

- **The Commission's proposal**: *Proposal for a Regulation laying down harmonised rules on
  artificial intelligence (Artificial Intelligence Act)*, **COM(2021) 206 final, Brussels,
  21.4.2021**. *Verified against EUR-Lex (CELEX `52021PC0206`).* The proposal defined an AI system
  by reference to **techniques and approaches listed in its Annex I** — which is the list-based
  definition Episode 1 says was abandoned, and the confirmation that Ep 1's account of the drafting
  choice is right.
- **ChatGPT's public release: 30 November 2022.** *Confidence: high, universally reported; ⚠ if the
  script states it as a date, take it from OpenAI's own announcement rather than from here.*
- **The gap the episode is built on**: the general-purpose chapter that now runs to Arts. 51–56 does
  not exist in COM(2021) 206. That is checkable directly against the proposal text and is the
  episode's single strongest fact.

destinations — where a position lands in an instrument the listener already met in Episode 1. Do not
quote the Act in this episode.
