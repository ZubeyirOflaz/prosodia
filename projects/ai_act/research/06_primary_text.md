## Article 3 — the definitions the series runs on

**3(1) 'AI system'** — "a machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of
autonomy and that **may exhibit adaptiveness** after deployment, and that, for explicit or implicit
objectives, **infers**, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions,
content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments"

**3(3) 'provider'** — "a natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body that
develops an AI system or a general-purpose AI model or that has an AI system or a general-purpose
AI model developed and places it on the market or puts the AI system into service **under its own
name or trademark**, whether for payment or free of charge"

**3(4) 'deployer'** — "a natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body **using an
AI system under its authority** except where the AI system is used in the course of a personal
non-professional activity"

**3(6) 'importer'** · **3(7) 'distributor'** · **3(11) 'putting into service'** — "the supply of an
AI system for first use directly to the deployer or for own use in the Union for its intended
purpose"

> **Teaching note.** "May exhibit adaptiveness" is why the Commission's February 2025 guidelines
> say adaptiveness is **not** necessary: a system that never learns after shipping is still an AI
> system. And "under its own name or trademark" is the hinge of the whole provider/deployer split.

## Article 2(1) — scope, and why foreign cases may not be hypothetical

- **(a)** "providers placing on the market or putting into service AI systems or general-purpose AI
  models **in the Union, irrespective of whether those providers are established or located within
  the Union or in a third country**"
- **(b)** "deployers of AI systems that have their place of establishment or are located within the
  Union"
- **(c)** "providers and deployers of AI systems that have their place of establishment or are
  located in a third country, **where the output produced by the AI system is used in the Union**"
- **(d)** importers and distributors · **(e)** product manufacturers placing an AI system with their
  product under their own name or trademark · **(f)** authorised representatives of non-Union
  providers · **(g)** "**affected persons that are located in the Union**"

> **This closes the question `05_foreign_instances.md` raised.** Limb (c) means a foreign system
> whose *output* is used in the Union is in scope. Several of the foreign instances are therefore
> only counterfactual as to the facts as they happened, not as to the law that would apply today.

## Article 5(1) — the prohibitions, with the 2026 insertions

- **(a)** subliminal, purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques "with the objective, or the
  effect of materially distorting the behaviour"
- **(b)** exploiting vulnerabilities "due to their age, disability or a specific social or economic
  situation"
- **(ba)** *[inserted by the Omnibus]* generating or manipulating realistic images, video or audio
  of "an identifiable natural person's intimate parts" without "freely-given, specific, informed,
  unambiguous and explicit consent"
- **(bb)** *[inserted by the Omnibus]* material within Article 2(c) and (e) of Directive 2011/93/EU
  (child sexual abuse material)
- **(c)** social scoring — evaluation or classification "based on their social behaviour" leading to
  detrimental treatment
- **(d)** criminal risk assessment "**based solely on the profiling** of a natural person" —
  **exception**: "does not apply to AI systems used to support the human assessment of the
  involvement of a person in a criminal activity, which is already based on objective and verifiable
  facts directly linked to a criminal activity"
- **(e)** creating or expanding facial recognition databases "through the untargeted scraping of
  facial images from the internet or CCTV footage"
- **(f)** inferring emotions "**in the areas of workplace and education institutions**" —
  **exception**: "except where the use of the AI system is intended to be put in place or into the
  market for medical or safety reasons"
- **(h)** real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law
  enforcement, "unless and in so far as such use is **strictly necessary**" for (i) targeted search
  for victims of abduction/trafficking/sexual exploitation or missing persons; (ii) prevention of "a
  specific, substantial and imminent threat to life or physical safety" or a terrorist threat;
  (iii) locating or identifying a suspect for offences in Annex II

> **The "change one fact" material is in the qualifiers**: *solely* in (d), *workplace and education*
> in (f), *untargeted* in (e), *strictly necessary* in (h). Move one word and the analysis moves.

## Article 43 — who actually checks a high-risk system

- **Annex III points 2–8: internal control only.** "providers shall follow the conformity assessment
  procedure based on internal control as referred to in Annex VI, **which does not provide for the
  involvement of a notified body**."
- **Annex III point 1 (biometrics)**: the provider chooses internal control (Annex VI) **or**
  assessment of the quality management system and technical documentation **with a notified body**
  (Annex VII).
- **Special case**: where point 1 systems are for law enforcement, immigration, asylum or EU
  institutions, "**the market surveillance authority shall act as a notified body**."

> **This is the Boeing parallel, confirmed from the text.** For most stand-alone high-risk systems
> the assessment is done by the party being assessed.

## Articles 99 and 101 — the penalty tiers, and who may impose them

| Infringement | Ceiling |
|---|---|
| Prohibited practices (Art. 5) | **€35m or 7%** of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher |
| Other obligations — Arts. 16, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 31, 33, 34, 50 | **€15m or 3%** |
| Incorrect, incomplete or misleading information to authorities | **€7.5m or 1%** |
| SMEs and start-ups (Art. 99(6)) | the **lower** of the percentage or the amount |

### Who enforces what — three bodies, not one

> **SUPERSEDED IN PART, 2026-09-13.** This split predates Arts. 75a–75d. Under Art. 75(1) as
> amended the AI Office is *exclusively* competent for a defined class of AI **systems** — including
> high-risk ones — and Art. 75c(4) lets it fine them. Read the second update under gap 8 in
> `04_gaps.md` before using anything below.


From the Commission's own press release **IP/26/1714, 31 July 2026** (retrieved as a PDF, 2026-09-13),
responsibility for enforcing the transparency rules and the prohibited practices is **split three
ways**:

- **The AI Office** — for AI systems offered by the **same provider as the underlying GPAI model**,
  and for systems integrated into **very large online platforms or search engines** designated under
  the Digital Services Act.
- **National competent authorities** — for **all other AI systems**.
- **The European Data Protection Supervisor** — for AI systems used by **EU institutions, bodies and
  agencies**.

The Commission adds its own caveat, which is an episode beat in itself: *"Effective enforcement will
also depend on Member States ensuring that national competent authorities are properly designated
and adequately resourced."*

**Article 101 is separate and matters for the architecture**: the **Commission** may fine providers
of **general-purpose AI models** up to **3% of global annual turnover or €15m**. So the Commission's
own fining power runs to GPAI providers; penalties under Art. 99 are laid down and applied by
**Member States** through their market surveillance authorities. Keep the two apart — a claim that
"the AI Office fined a retail chain" does not fit this architecture (see `04_gaps.md`), and the
three-way split above makes that mismatch sharper still: a retail chain and a hiring platform fall
to **national competent authorities**.

### What Article 50 actually requires, in the Commission's words

Chatbots and other interactive systems must **tell users they are dealing with AI, not a human**;
deepfakes must be **labelled**; and AI-generated or altered content must carry **machine-readable
marks** so it can be detected. On 31 July 2026 the Commission published a first list of **more than
180 organisations** that had signed a **Code of Practice on transparency of AI-generated content** —
a *second* code, distinct from the GPAI Code of Practice, and not previously recorded in this docket.

### GPAI obligations, in the Commission's words

All GPAI providers must document specified information and supply it to competent authorities or
downstream providers, **put in place a copyright policy**, and **publish a sufficiently detailed
summary of the content used to train their models**. Providers of systemic-risk models carry further
obligations addressing **chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear risks, loss of control,
cyber offence, harmful manipulation and threats to fundamental rights** — and the release
specifically names risks "to European cybersecurity and to AI acting outside human control".

## Articles 65–68 — the governance bodies

- **65–66, European AI Board** — representatives of the Member States plus the Commission;
  coordinates consistent application, issues opinions, recommendations and guidance, supports
  harmonised standards and codes of practice.
- **67, Advisory Forum** — balanced membership across industry, start-ups, SMEs, civil society and
  academia. **Permanent members: the Fundamental Rights Agency, ENISA, CEN, CENELEC and ETSI.**
  *(Worth an episode beat on its own: the standards bodies sit permanently alongside the
  fundamental-rights agency.)*
- **68, Scientific Panel of Independent Experts** — selected by the Commission for up-to-date
  expertise and **independence from any provider**; advises on systemic risks, model classification,
  evaluation methodology and cross-border market surveillance; **may alert the AI Office to systemic
  risks**.
