# Docket 09 — Why this instrument and not another  ·  compiled 2026-09-15

Written for **Episode 6 (LENS — Risk Against Rights)**, and it also carries the evidence Episode 12
needs for the verdict.

**The gap it closes.** Episode 6 puts four positions in play and the plan could source only two of
them. Both sourced ones were **critical** of the Act; the defence of the risk-based product-safety

## The primary source: the Commission's own options

**Commission Staff Working Document, *Impact Assessment accompanying the Proposal for a Regulation
laying down harmonised rules on Artificial Intelligence (Artificial Intelligence Act) and amending
certain Union legislative acts*, SWD(2021) 84 final, Brussels, 21 April 2021.**
*Verified against EUR-Lex (`eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/ALL/?uri=SWD:2021:84:FIN`); full PDF
also at `artificialintelligenceact.eu`.*

**Five options were assessed, and these are their words:**

| | Option |
|---|---|
| 1 | EU legislative instrument setting up a voluntary labelling scheme |
| 2 | **A sectoral, 'ad-hoc' approach** |
| 3 | Horizontal EU legislative instrument establishing mandatory requirements for high-risk AI applications |
| 3+ | Horizontal instrument for high-risk applications **+ co-regulation through codes of conduct for non-high risk applications** |
| 4 | Horizontal instrument establishing mandatory requirements for **all** AI applications, irrespective of the risk they pose |

**This is the single most valuable thing in this file.** The persona's "why this rule and not
another" beat asks for the road not taken, and here it is enumerated by the body that took the
other road — not reconstructed, not inferred. Episode 6's four positions map onto it almost
exactly: position 1 is Option 3+, the sectoral alternative is **Option 2**, and the
rights-maximalist position is nearest Option 4.

⚠ **One inference, marked as one.** The Act as adopted is plainly Option 3+ — a horizontal
instrument with mandatory requirements for high-risk systems, plus codes of conduct for the rest at
**Art. 95**. The fetched record did not show Section 8 ("Preferred option") in full, so *say the
Act matches Option 3+, not that the document declares 3+ preferred*, unless somebody reads
Section 8.

## Position 1 — the risk-based, product-safety framing, defended

**The institutional defence** is SWD(2021) 84 itself: proportionality, an instrument that operates
*ex ante* and at scale, reaching every unit placed on the market, resting on an enforcement
apparatus that already exists, and giving firms a determinate answer.

**The academic treatment of the model the Act actually adopted:** **Ronit Justo-Hanani,
"Risk-based approach to EU AI act: benefits and challenges of co-regulation", *Policy Design and
Practice* 9(2) (2026), pp. 171–180, DOI `10.1080/25741292.2025.2610869`.** *Verified: author,
title, journal, volume, issue, pages, DOI; received March 2025, accepted November 2025, published
online 2 January 2026.* Examines co-regulation — the public/private hybrid the Act runs on — and
argues the framework should **mandate data-sharing between private and public actors** to build the
regulatory capacity the model presupposes. Useful because it defends the design **and** names its
precondition, which is exactly the shape of an honest defence.

**Use it this way.** Give the defence its best form before answering it: this is the only European
machinery that acts before harm, at scale, through institutions that exist. Then the objection the
series has already earned — that machinery delegates its substance to standards bodies, and as of
June 2026 not one deliverable has been cited in the Official Journal.

## Position 4 — the sectoral alternative, with a holder

**Martin Ebers, "Truly Risk-Based Regulation of Artificial Intelligence: How to Implement the EU's
AI Act", *European Journal of Risk Regulation* (Cambridge University Press).** Also SSRN abstract
`4870387` and Stanford–Vienna Transatlantic Technology Law Forum, TTLF Working Paper No. 101.
*Verified: author, exact title, journal, the SSRN and TTLF records. ⚠ Volume, issue, year and pages
not established here — confirm on Cambridge Core before any of them is spoken.*

Ebers is Professor of IT Law at the University of Tartu and President of the Robotics & AI Law
Society (RAILS).

**The argument, and note its shape:** he does not say the risk-based approach is wrong. He says the
Act **is not truly risk-based** — that it claims proportionality and then departs from it through
pre-defined closed risk categories, thin reliance on empirical evidence, an over-broad definition
of AI, double regulatory burdens and overlapping enforcement. His remedy is **sector-specific**
assessment, tailored to the risks an application actually poses in its own field.

**Why this is the strongest version of position 4.** It is an immanent critique: it holds the Act
to its own stated principle rather than to an external one. And the position is not merely
academic — **it was Option 2 in the Commission's own impact assessment**, considered and not taken.
An episode can say that a named professor argues today for the option the Commission assessed and
rejected in 2021, which is a far better beat than "some prefer a sectoral approach".

**Related, if a second voice is wanted:** the Robotics and AI Law Society's collective *Critical
Assessment* of the Commission's proposal (2021). ⚠ Full citation not established here.
